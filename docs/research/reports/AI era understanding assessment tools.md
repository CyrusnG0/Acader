# Nobody ships the whole understanding loop yet

No "perfect" product exists as of 2026-10-14. The field of AI oral-assessment tools is crowded, with at least eight named vendors that question students about their own work or their course, plus several university-built systems. But **none publicly combines all four things Acader wants**: an in-platform workspace where AI helps without writing; questions anchored to specific code lines, claims and citations; grounding in how *this instructor* taught the material; and output framed as comprehension tiers rather than integrity or authenticity flags. The closest neighbours each cover one or two pieces. Viva (vivaproof.com) anchors questions to passages of a submission but reports an "Authenticity: Clean / Concern" rating. Cadmus has process tracking and oral questions tied to written work, but in Phase 1 the teachers write those questions, and its AI-personalised prompts are only "exploring" for late 2026/2027. Prova pairs a recorded work session with a short AI oral but publishes no anchoring detail. FeedbackFruits reportedly hands a human examiner AI-suggested questions. Policy is moving in Acader's direction. Australia's regulator calls detection "not viable" and names interactive orals as a secure option. Hong Kong universities already fall back on oral explanations and "fact-checking interviews" when a detector flag is disputed. The research backs the founder's "the live oral is the gate" premise: GPT-4 answers 88% of auto-generated questions about a learner's code correctly, so async written answers prove little. The real opening is therefore narrower and more defensible than "AI asks about your work", which is now a commodity. It is the **TA-facing, course-grounded, line- and citation-anchored question sheet** that makes a 3–5 minute human oral cheap, standardised and fair. Many sources behind this map are thin: vendor pages, a vendor-written comparison blog, abstracts and search snippets. The verification list at the end matters.

## Twenty products cover pieces, none covers the loop

The market splits into five layers. **Process-capture writing environments** record how text was built: Turnitin Clarity, Grammarly Authorship, GPTZero Writing Replay, Brisk and Cadmus. **Submission-anchored AI vivas** question the student about their upload: Viva/vivaproof, Prova, Vocina, Rocketproof, Eduface and Integrevise. **Course-anchored AI orals** question students about the course, not their own submission: Professr, Tough Tongue AI, Socratic Mind and NYU Stern's in-house system. **Human-led orals with AI assistance** include FeedbackFruits and Cadmus. **Code platforms** such as PrairieLearn, Codio and CodeGrade grade code and written explanations with LLMs but never ask a follow-up question. Process replay is now table stakes: Turnitin, Grammarly, GPTZero, Brisk and Cadmus all ship it, and several bundle it free. Their standard response to pasted text is to *label the paste*, not to check comprehension of it ([Brisk](https://www.briskteaching.com/inspect-writing); [GPTZero Chrome listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/gptzero-ai-detector-and-h/kgobeoibakoahbfnlficpmibdbkdchap); [Grammarly Authorship](https://www.grammarly.com/edu/authorship)).

Three patterns matter more than any single row. **Only Viva publicly demonstrates passage-level anchoring, and no product found anchors questions to a student's citations.** Prova and Dartmouth's Classmoji prototype claim "your own code" walkthroughs without showing line-level anchoring ([SIGCSE 2026](https://sigcse2026.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2026-lightning-talks/17/AI-Driven-Oral-Examinations-for-Code-Assessment-Evaluating-Understanding-Beyond-the-)). **Course grounding and submission anchoring never appear together**: Professr, Tough Tongue and NYU's case library ground in the course, while Viva, Vocina and Rocketproof ground in the upload. **The two most mature submission-viva vendors frame output as integrity**: Viva has "Authenticity: Concern" and Rocketproof has "integrity flags". That leaves the comprehension-only, human-decides framing open as a positioning opportunity. Vocina's "not an AI detector" line shows at least one rival has seen the same opening.

The honest steelman against "nobody does this" is that a well-resourced buyer could approximate Acader today by combining Cadmus, Clarity and a TA with a list of questions. The convergence risks are concrete. Cadmus ships Phase 2 AI prompts. Turnitin adds reflection questions to Clarity. PrairieLearn turns its code-aware grader into a follow-up asker. The internal estimate of a 12–18 month window is an assumption, not a finding.

## Regulators are retiring detection and asking for orals

The policy direction across the jurisdictions researched is consistent. Detection is demoted to supporting evidence at most, and assurance of learning moves to supervised or oral tasks. This helps a comprehension-evidence product and hurts any product that can be read as a detector.

**Australia leads.** The **University of Sydney's two-lane model** puts "most" assessment in Lane 2 (open, AI allowed and scaffolded). Lane 1 is "in-person, supervised tasks that confirm individual student achievement". The framework started in Semester 2 2025, program redesign runs through 2026, and online programs likely need in-person secure assessment "by 2027" ([USyd framework](https://intranet.sydney.edu.au/education-students/curriculum/assessment/assessment-feedback-framework.html); [Teaching@Sydney timeline](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/navigating-ai-in-higher-education-tasks-ahead-for-2025-and-2026/)). Sydney lists in-person interactive orals and viva voces as Lane 1 examples. It argues a 10–15 minute interactive oral assesses about what a 3,000-word essay does, at about 15–20 minutes per student ([Five myths about IOs](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/five-myths-about-interactive-oral-assessments-and-how-to-get-started/)). **The implication for Acader is sharp: an unsupervised remote AI viva does not qualify as Lane 1.** A TA-run in-person oral using Acader's question sheet does, and the async Room sits in Lane 2 as formative evidence. **TEQSA** collected credible AI action plans from all 203 providers in 2024 and announced a "regulatory-led" approach from 2026. In September 2025 it published *Enacting assessment reform in a time of artificial intelligence*, which offers three pathways. One is unit-level assurance through "at least one secure task per unit, such as an interactive oral". Per Jason Lodge's summary, it states that detecting AI use "is not viable" ([TEQSA RFI](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/about-us/news-and-events/latest-news/artificial-intelligence-request-information-next-steps); [TEQSA PDF](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/enacting-assessment-reform-in-a-time-of-artificial-intelligence.pdf); [FutureCampus](https://futurecampus.com.au/2025/09/30/from-compass-to-map-teqsa-charts-new-pathways-for-assessment-in-the-ai-era/)). Griffith runs interactive orals for cohorts up to 750, but practitioners report "significant workload issues" at scale ([THE Campus](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/campus/interactive-orals-offer-solution-ai-overreliance-higher-education); [HERDSA](https://herdsa.org.au/herdsa-connect/interactive-orals-scale-what-happened-when-we-tried-draw-owl)). That workload is the problem tooling can solve.

**The UK is "embed and adapt", with vivas arriving as samples.** The Russell Group's 2023 principles commit members to adapting assessment while protecting rigour ([Russell Group](https://russellgroup.ac.uk/policy/policy-briefings/principles-use-generative-ai-tools-education)). Oxford and Sussex academics proposed that "a small, randomly selected percentage of students would complete a viva" within existing moderation ([THE](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/735603)). Jisc meetups report mini-viva experiments with mixed results on anxiety and scheduling ([Jisc NCAI](https://nationalcentreforai.jiscinvolve.org/wp/?p=7658)). The demand driver is stark. **HEPI 2026 found 94% of UK undergraduates use GenAI for assessed work**, while only 36% feel their institution encourages it, and the report notes "growing anxiety about misconduct accusations" ([HEPI 2026](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/)).

**Hong Kong is the most important market for Friday, and it already runs a manual version of Acader's workflow.** HKU moved from a ban to integration in 2023 and named "oral examinations" among its alternative assessments ([HKU press](https://hku.hk/press/news_detail_26434.html)). HKBU's 2025 student guidelines say "AI detection tools may support, but not replace, professional academic judgment", and let instructors request "AI usage records, early draft versions, oral explanation of the work" ([HKBU PDF](https://ar.hkbu.edu.hk/f/upload/6036/GenAI_Advisory_Guidelines__Summarised_version_for_students__2025.pdf)). PolyU allows GenAI in take-home continuous assessment with the student accountable, and its continuing-education arm says the Turnitin AI score must not be the sole basis for adverse action ([PolyU notice](https://www.polyu.edu.hk/ar/students-in-taught-programmes/notices-to-students/2025/2025-02-21_guidelines-for-students-on-the-use-of-generative-artificial-intelligence/); [CPCE](https://it-training.cpce-polyu.edu.hk/mod/book/view.php?id=1496)). The incumbents are detection tools. HKUST has had Turnitin AI detection since 2023-04-05, CityU shows the AI report to teachers in Canvas, and CUHK has its Senate-mandated home-grown VeriGuide, which now advertises an AI writing detector with a vendor-claimed ">90% accuracy" ([HKUST CEI](https://cei.hkust.edu.hk/en-hk/turnitin); [CityU](https://cityu.edu.hk/dlsupport/teacher-canvas-turnitin-ai-writing-detection.html); [CUHK policy](https://cuhk.edu.hk/policy/academichonesty/Eng_htm_files_(2013-14)/p09.htm); [keep.edu.hk](https://keep.edu.hk/?p=79577)). HKBU's student newspaper reported in May 2026 that CUHK acknowledged possible false positives and routes appeals through "fact-checking interviews", and that a nursing student failed a course after an AI flag. These details come from a search snippet; the page returned 403 ([The Young Reporter](https://tyr-jour.hkbu.edu.hk/2026/05/31/ai-detection-tools-spark-false-plagiarism-fears-among-university-students/)). This matters in an English-as-second-language system: Stanford's Liang et al. found seven detectors misclassified **61% of non-native TOEFL essays** as AI-written ([The Markup](https://themarkup.org/machine-learning/2023/08/14/ai-detection-tools-falsely-accuse-international-students-of-cheating)). No UGC-level policy, HK procurement norms or HK licensee of Cadmus, Clarity or Studiosity were found.

**The US is moving bottom-up.** Blue books are back ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/03/14/ai-blue-books-colleges-jobs)). AP reported in April 2026 that Cornell, Penn, UCSD and NYU are spreading oral exams. In one Cornell course, TAs now grade only 20-minute Socratic defences of problem sets ([WCAX/AP](https://www.wcax.com/2026/04/22/perfect-homework-blank-stares-why-colleges-are-turning-oral-exams-combat-ai/)). Detector retreat is documented: Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector in 2023 and Waterloo in September 2025 ([Vanderbilt](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/); [Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/educational-technology-hub/news/discontinuing-use-ai-detection-functionality-turnitin)). Faculty concern about cheating rose from 36% to 55% between 2024 and 2026 in Tyton's survey (secondary coverage only) ([D2L](https://www.d2l.com/blog/five-findings-from-the-2026-time-for-class-report-that-surprised-us/)).

**In the EU, any tool that evaluates learning outcomes is high-risk.** AI Act Annex III point 3 covers both evaluating learning outcomes and monitoring prohibited behaviour during tests. Per law-firm reporting, the Digital Omnibus postponed stand-alone Annex III obligations to **2 December 2027** ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). Acader's "AI recommends, human decides" posture reduces exposure but probably does not remove the classification if tiers feed grades. Treat that as an assumption needing legal review. The upside is that logging and human oversight, built in now, become an EU selling point.

## Research says the live oral is the gate, and short structured orals work

The founder's attack scenario is supported by direct evidence. **GPT-4 correctly answered 88% of 399 auto-generated "questions about learners' code"** (GPT-3.5 managed 69%). Accuracy reached 100% on parameter-name and line-purpose questions but dropped to **70% on variable tracing** ([Lehtinen et al., ICSE-SEET 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.11734v1)). An async written answer to an anchored question is therefore weak evidence on its own. Line anchoring raises the cost of pasting, but does not stop a student pasting code plus question into a chatbot. Every serious vendor concedes the remote voice channel is also leaky: NYU's authors state that "live AI coaching and a second person off-camera cannot be fully prevented" ([arXiv 2603.18221](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18221v2)), and Sherpa's 2023 pilot found students with notes "that could have come from a chatbot" ([EdSurge](https://www.edsurge.com/news/2023-10-05-as-ai-chatbots-rise-more-educators-look-to-oral-exams-with-high-tech-twist)). **No independent measurement of any anti-relay defence exists.** The defensible design is a short, in-person or human-witnessed oral with adaptive follow-ups. The AI's job is to write the questions; the evidence comes from the human conversation.

Short orals are workable at scale when they are structured. The University of Washington ran **5-minute in-person code interviews** on five weekly assignments in a 200+ student course. It needed three TAs per 30-student section, which the authors call "not sustainable long term", though still likely less work than grading paper exams ([arXiv 2410.01010](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01010)). The most useful finding for Acader is in that paper's grade distributions. Unstructured Winter interviews gave **76% "Exemplary"**. Spring interviews with fixed question types (trace a test case, compare approaches, find a bug) gave **30% Exemplary, 63% Satisfactory, 7% Approaching**, so structure produced real discrimination. The broader literature agrees that standardised questions are the main reliability lever for orals, which otherwise carry "lower levels of reliability and validity" ([UAntwerpen](https://uantwerpen.be/en/centres/centre-expertise-higher-education/didactic-information/teaching-tips/assessing-students/oral-tests-objectivity)). Pre-generated anchored question sheets supply exactly that structure.

The comprehension signal is real and is not a cheat signal. Aalto's work found **27% of students with passing programs failed at least one simple question about their own code** ([ITiCSE 2023](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/403399e9-4ec3-4e50-8f47-8f1520a6677f)). Lehtinen's dissertation puts the figure at up to 20% (snippet-level) ([Aalto](https://www.aalto.fi/en/events/public-defence-in-computer-science-msc-tech-teemu-lehtinen)). Students do well on syntax questions but score only 30–50% on tracing ([arXiv 2404.11734](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.11734v1)). Tracing questions are therefore the hardest type for both students and LLMs, which makes "what does this variable hold after line 14?" a strong default anchored question. A CS1 study by Fowles et al. allowed GenAI alongside code-review interviews for three semesters. It found **no significant change in exam averages** even as paste-to-typed ratios rose, and student sentiment was positive (abstract only, preprint) ([arXiv 2605.21374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21374)).

LLM question *generation* is reported as good; LLM *grading* is the weak point. TAMIGO found GPT-3.5 "highly effective at generating viva questions when given enough context", but feedback on answers was "mixed" with hallucinations (snippet-level) ([arXiv 2407.16805](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.16805)). NYU's three-LLM council started far apart (Gemini 16.3/20 vs Claude 13.0). It reached Krippendorff's α of 0.83–0.95 only after deliberation, with informal human comparison ([arXiv 2603.18221](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18221v2)). NYU's agent also stacked multiple questions in 31% of turns, picked one "random" case in 86% of exams in one week, and cut students off after a 5-second silence. The fixes were moving selection into code and allowing 30 seconds of silence. On cost, the Fall 2025 run was **about $0.42 per student**, while the v2 paper puts the all-in cost at **$3.40–$4.10 per student** versus about $21 for two human graders ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/nyu-professor-fights-ai-cheating-with-ai-powered-oral-exams-that-cost-42-cents-per-student/); [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18221v2)). Quote the range, not the headline. Students in that study found the AI oral more stressful than written exams (83%, then 63%), and only 13–25% preferred it. Anxiety evidence elsewhere shows stress peaks at the first oral and drops with repetition. It falls harder on women, some first-generation students and English-language learners ([ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/oral-proficiency-exams-in-high-enrollment-computer-science-courses); [UCL 2026](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/teaching-learning/publications/2026/may/making-oral-assessments-inclusive)). This supports frequent, short, low-stakes orals with generous think time and mandatory alternatives.

Keystroke process data separates authentic writing from transcription at about 99% accuracy, but only in a lab setting with paste disabled. It is English-only and has small samples ([EDM 2024](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2024/proceedings/2024.EDM-short-papers.47/index.html)). No field study measures error rates for revision-history tools, and no fairness study covers assistive-tech users. That validates Acader's rule that "typing is not proof": process data is entry context, not a gate.

## Five gaps that no product or study fills

The first gap is **course-grounded questioning of an individual submission**. Every tool found grounds questions in either the upload or the course, never both. No study tests whether course-material grounding improves question quality or discrimination over generic LLM questions. This is Acader's core differentiation hypothesis and its planned blind comparison, and it is genuinely open research rather than a proven advantage.

The second is the **TA question sheet**. No product is clearly marketed as "here are the three questions to ask this student, with what a strong and a partial answer sounds like". FeedbackFruits comes closest per a vendor blog, and the Church et al. viva simulator produces a transcript for a human examiner ([arXiv 2511.05530](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.05530v1)). UW's TAs ran structured interviews with no generated questions at all. One course-design paper on AI-permitted code has examiners strip comments before a 10-minute defence and ask "why should I believe this?" ([arXiv 2607.15518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.15518)), but no tool generates those probes.

The third is **citation-anchored questioning for writing**. Copyleaks, GPTZero and CheckIfExist check that a citation exists, and Scite flags retractions ([arXiv 2602.15871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15871)). Grammarly's Citation Finder goes the other way and *supplies* sources ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/08/18/grammarly-leans-writing-assistance-agents-students-professionals/)). Nobody asks "you cited X for this claim; what does X actually find, and why does it support you?"

The fourth is **validation of very short orals**. No study establishes how many anchored questions a 2–5 minute oral needs for a stable comprehension judgement. None measures TA inter-rater reliability with AI-generated question sets, and none compares AI-written with instructor-written viva questions on the same submission. The adjacent MCQ evidence is mixed: GPT-4 items aligned well with objectives ([arXiv 2312.03173](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.03173)), but students scored about 9% lower on LLM-authored items ([AAAI 2025](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/34984)).

The fifth is a **non-accusatory remedy for disputed detector flags**. Hong Kong institutions already use oral explanations and fact-checking interviews ad hoc, but no product formalises a fair, recorded, course-grounded chance for a student to show understanding. Coverage of AIED, LAK and L@S 2025–2026 proceedings and of European oral-exam research was shallow. Run a targeted ACM DL or Scopus search before saying "no prior work" in public.

## What Acader should build and say next

### Product ideas that follow from the evidence

**Make the TA viva sheet the product, not a feature.** For each submission, Acader produces three questions. Each is anchored to a specific line, claim or citation and tagged with the lecture moment where the instructor taught the concept ("Week 4, 12:40, the loop-invariant example on the board"). Each comes with short cues for what a Strong and a Partial answer sound like, and one follow-up per question. The TA runs a 3-minute oral, taps a tier, and Acader records it as Oral-verified. This addresses the binding constraint every practitioner names (TA prep and standardisation, not talk time). It fits Sydney's Lane 1 and TEQSA's unit-level pathway when run in person. It uses the founder's validated lecture miner, so professors become the heroes. Default to **tracing and "what happens at this line" questions**, the type both students and GPT-4 do worst on. **Show the code with comments stripped** in the oral view.

**Reposition the async runner as rehearsal, not evidence.** Given the 88% LLM answer rate, async text answers should never produce "Oral-verified" on their own. Use them as a free practice viva the student owns. This lowers first-oral anxiety, which the evidence says drops with repetition. It also helps choose *which* questions go on the TA sheet. That turns the paste attack from a hole into a non-issue: pasting into a chatbot only cheats the student's own rehearsal.

**Offer sampled and student-requested orals, never "flagged" ones.** UK academics already propose random sampling inside moderation. Acader can schedule a random sample, any student who asks to have their understanding on record, and students where the evidence is thin. In product copy, the last group is "a recommended oral check" to strengthen the evidence, and a human decides. A student-initiated "put my understanding on record" button is an original, student-protective feature. It matters most in Hong Kong, where students now fear false flags.

**Ship the citation viva as the writing-track wedge.** Pair a cheap existence check (Crossref/Semantic Scholar) with one anchored question per key citation: what the source found, and why it supports this specific claim. No vendor does the second half.

**Build the trust layer competitors skipped.** Use deterministic, code-side question selection with the LLM only wording the questions, which follows NYU's documented failure modes. Keep think-time defaults of at least 30 seconds, log everything, require a human sign-off on every tier, and provide mandatory typed or in-person alternatives. Together these form an EU AI Act-ready story and an answer to Vivaproof's "Authenticity: Concern". Since viva generation is near-free, as NYU shows, price the workflow and the evidence record, not the LLM call.

## Conclusion

The question has shifted from "does an AI viva exist?" (yes, many, and one university built a near-free one) to "who makes the human oral cheap enough to run on every unit?". The research makes AI-only async questioning a weak gate, and regulators in Australia and the policies of Hong Kong universities already point to human orals as the remedy. The scarce resource is therefore structured TA time, and that is what Acader's lecture-grounded, line- and citation-anchored question sheet saves. Acader's most defensible position is not a better AI examiner but the **preparation layer for human examiners**, combined with a student-owned practice loop. That framing makes the paste attack irrelevant instead of an embarrassment.

The biggest unknown is not the competition but Acader's own core claim. Nobody, including Acader, has shown that course-graph grounding produces better or more discriminating questions than a generic LLM reading the same code. The concierge pilot's blind comparison is the single experiment that turns this report's "gap" into either a moat or a myth. It should be framed that way on Friday: as an open, honest test that the instructor is invited to help run.
