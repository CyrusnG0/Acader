## S1 — Confirming source: Bloom, Liang, Roberts & Ying

- **Citation:** Bloom, N., Liang, J., Roberts, J., & Ying, Z. J. "Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment." NBER Working Paper 18871, March 2013. (Later published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2015. **[UNVERIFIED by me from the journal page; the demo cites the NBER paper I read.]**)
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w18871 (PDF: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18871/w18871.pdf)
- **Setting, in the paper's own words (abstract, p.2):**
  - "We report the results of a WFH experiment at CTrip, a 16,000- employee, NASDAQ-listed Chinese travel agency."
  - "Call center employees who volunteered to WFH were randomly assigned to work from home or in the office for 9 months."
  - "Home working led to a 13% performance increase, of which about 9% was from working more minutes per shift (fewer breaks and sick-days) and 4% from more calls per minute (attributed to a quieter working environment)."
  - "Home workers also reported improved work satisfaction and experienced less turnover, but their promotion rate conditional on performance fell."
  - "…over half of them switched, which led to the gains from WFH almost doubling to 22%."
- **Sample (p.4):** the experiment drew on "these 249 employees"; "those with even-numbered birthdays were selected to work at home". Eligibility included "at least six months tenure, broadband access and a private room at home in which they could work".
- **Attrition (p.4):** "attrition fell sharply among the home workers, dropping by 50% versus the control group."
- **Downside (p.4):** "one downside of WFH appears to be that, conditional on performance, it reduced rates of promotion…"
- **What it can support:** WFH raised measured performance for volunteer call-centre staff at one company over 9 months; most of the gain came from more minutes worked.
- **What it can't support:** results for creative or innovation work, for people who didn't volunteer, for other companies, or for junior staff in particular.
- **Note:** both the "one company" fact and the "call centre" fact come straight from the abstract above.

## S2 — Complicating source: Gibbs, Mengel & Siemroth

- **Citation:** Gibbs, M., Mengel, F., & Siemroth, C. "Work from Home & Productivity: Evidence from Personnel & Analytics Data on IT Professionals." IZA Discussion Paper No. 14336, May 2021. (A revised version later appeared in the *Journal of Political Economy: Microeconomics*, 2023. **[UNVERIFIED by me: that version's numbers may differ, e.g. a search summary gave productivity falling 8–19%. The demo uses only the IZA May 2021 text I read and says so.]**)
- **URL:** https://docs.iza.org/dp14336.pdf
- **Abstract, verbatim (p.3):**
  - "Using personnel and analytics data from over 10,000 skilled professionals at a large Asian IT services company, we compare productivity before and during the work from home [WFH] period of the Covid-19 pandemic."
  - "Total hours worked increased by roughly 30%, including a rise of 18% in working after normal business hours."
  - "Average output did not significantly change. Therefore, productivity fell by about 20%."
  - "Time spent on coordination activities and meetings increased, but uninterrupted work hours shrank considerably."
  - "Employees also spent less time networking, and received less coaching and 1:1 meetings with supervisors."
  - "Employees with children living at home increased hours worked more than those without children at home, and suffered a bigger decline in productivity than those without children."
- **Tenure (p.5):** "We find some evidence that employees with greater tenure at the company increased their output slightly during WFH, whereas employees with lower tenure do not."
- **Hours (p.5):** "Much of this increase came from working outside of normal office hours."
- **What it can support:** at one large IT services company during Covid, people worked far more hours and output stayed flat, so output per hour fell; coaching and 1:1 time fell.
- **What it can't support:** any "junior mentorship fell by 42%" claim (**no such figure exists in the paper**), any "1,200 people" sample, or WFH effects outside the Covid period.
- **Single-company fact (abstract, p.3):** "a large Asian IT services company". This is the basis of the same-standard check against S1.

## S3 — Adjacent source (juniors and feedback): Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais

- **Citation:** Emanuel, N., Harrington, E., & Pallais, A. "The Power of Proximity to Coworkers." NBER Working Paper 31880, issued November 2023, revised June 2026. The publisher page I read lists it as published in *The Quarterly Journal of Economics* 141(3), 2026. **[The QJE listing comes from the NBER page; I did not open the journal page.]**
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w31880 (PDF: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31880/w31880.pdf)
- **Abstract, verbatim (p.2, June 2026 revision):**
  - "We study software engineers at a Fortune 500 firm from 2019 to 2024, leveraging two shocks to proximity: (i) the office closures in 2020 and (ii) the subsequent return-to-office mandates in 2022 and 2023."
  - "We find that sitting near teammates increases coding feedback by 18.3% and improves code quality."
  - "Gains are concentrated among less-tenured and younger employees, who are building human capital."
  - "However, there is a tradeoff: experienced engineers write less code when sitting near teammates."
- **What it can support:** being near teammates gives more coding feedback, mostly to less-tenured and younger engineers, at some cost in senior engineers' own output.
- **What it can't support:** a "-42% junior mentorship" number (not in the paper), or anything about essay-writing or non-software jobs.
- **Version warning:** earlier versions (2023) report different headline figures (a search summary said 22% more feedback in the 2023 abstract). The demo uses **18.3%, from the June 2026 revision** only, and names that version.

## S4 — Mechanism source: Yang et al., Microsoft

- **Citation:** Yang, L., Holtz, D., Jaffe, S., Suri, S., Sinha, S., Weston, J., Joyce, C., Shah, N. P., Sherman, K., Hecht, B., & Teevan, J. "The effects of remote work on collaboration among information workers." *Nature Human Behaviour*, published online September 2021 (issue date in the page metadata: 2022/01).
- **DOI:** 10.1038/s41562-021-01196-4 (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01196-4)
- **Abstract, verbatim (publisher page):**
  - "…we use rich data on the emails, calendars, instant messages, video/audio calls and workweek hours of 61,182 US Microsoft employees over the first six months of 2020…"
  - "Our results show that firm-wide remote work caused the collaboration network of workers to become more static and siloed, with fewer bridges between disparate parts."
  - "Furthermore, there was a decrease in synchronous communication and an increase in asynchronous communication."
  - "Together, these effects may make it harder for employees to acquire and share new information across the network."
- **What it can support:** at Microsoft in early 2020, remote work made collaboration more siloed and moved talk toward asynchronous channels.
- **What it can't support:** that remote work raised cross-team contact, or that scheduled video calls restore it. The paper says synchronous communication *decreased*.
- **Replaces:** the draft's "Pentland (2022), MIT Media Lab — 3x patents from watercooler collisions". **[UNVERIFIED and not used.]** I found no matching paper.

## Facts used for the same-standard check (all verified above)

- Bloom: one company ("CTrip, a 16,000- employee, NASDAQ-listed Chinese travel agency"), call-centre volunteers, 9 months.
- Gibbs: one company ("a large Asian IT services company"), 10,000+ professionals, Covid period.
- Both are therefore single-company studies. A student who discards Gibbs for "one company only" but builds on Bloom has used a filter on one source and not the other.
