Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but not with a hard cap on initial rents. It should adopt a narrow package: security of tenure plus a light, published per-square-foot band on initial rents, phased in only as enforcement capacity is built. The evidence says the problem is real, that the hard-cap remedy would hurt the people it is meant to help, and that the middle path is both defensible and incomplete.

The case for acting is strong. About 110,000 households live in SDUs, and per square foot they pay a 15 to 25 percent premium over mainstream flats [D1p1]. Rent burden rose from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within a tenancy but left the initial rent untouched [D1p3], and Mrs Wong, who has never had a written agreement, says it changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. The pain point is at the entry price, and legal counsel agrees the ordinance leaves "the actual pain point" unregulated [D9p1].

Yet a hard cap is the wrong tool. Stock grew from 100,800 to 111,200 units through the 2021 ordinance [D2p1], and it is tempting to read that as proof that more control is safe. The working paper explains why that is misleading: the ordinance is a second-generation control, and these show small or null effects on stock, whereas first-generation hard caps on levels show consistent contraction [D6p2]. Estimated supply elasticity is 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. One think-tank reading makes the same point: the stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument [D12p2]. For a 15 percent cut in initial rents, the model gives incumbents about 900 dollars a month while 3,000 to 4,000 households a year cannot enter the segment, mostly new arrivals [D6p4].

Berlin shows the same pattern. Its cap cut offer rents, then listings contracted as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, rents for new contracts jumped above trend [D7p1, D7p2]. The lesson is that the tenants a cap protects are those already inside, and the bill goes to those outside [D7p3]. This is not abstract here. Mr Chan has watched two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year, and fears landlords would convert cubicles to storage rather than accept regulated tenants [D3p2]. Mrs Wong reports two converted flats in her stairwell, yet believes landlords cannot exit [D4p3]. The Landlords Association promises exactly that exit [D5p2]. Its financial claims are weak, since it concedes it has no data on members' actual margins and relies on a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. But the exit behaviour is corroborated independently by tenants and by the literature, so it should not be dismissed just because the lobbyist's numbers are thin.

A hard cap also carries legal and administrative risk. Counsel expects compensation claims under the Basic Law and judicial review, with litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1, D9p3]. And a cap is only as real as its inspection regime: current capacity covers under 6 percent of stock a year, and a tenancy register would take an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1].

That last point also exposes the weakness of my own recommendation. The band approach counsel prefers needs survey infrastructure that does not yet exist [D9p2]. The internal minute warns that enforcement relies on complaints, so it would miss the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement and the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not find any design that protects that group first [D10p3]. I cannot answer this fully. What I can say is that it argues for sequencing, and for making written tenancies the first deliverable, because a register and a written agreement are what let a band be enforced at all.

Tenure security is the strongest lever. In the NGO survey of 440 households, 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan says the same: he names the waiting list, not rent, and would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. His landlord ended a previous tenancy on two weeks' notice after he asked for a repair [D3p1]. Security of tenure would also reduce the fear that stops tenants from complaining. The NGO further notes that enforcement spent policing rents is not spent on fire and ventilation inspections, where harm is documented [D11p2].

So the recommendation is a floor standard, tenure security and a light, reviewable band, which matches the direction the consultation is expected to take [D12p1]. This accepts a small, bounded contraction risk in exchange for protection that tenants say they value. A hard cap would give a visible win to current tenants and hand the cost to the newest arrivals, the very group in the brief's first passage [D1p2].
