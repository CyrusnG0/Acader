**The Case Against a Hard Cap on SDU Rents**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units through a hard cap on initial rents. The archive supports a narrower package—tenure security plus a light, band-based limit—but the evidence against a first-generation cap is strong enough to make the case against it decisive. The core problem is that a hard cap targets the price of a symptom while leaving the actual constraint—the public-housing queue—untouched, and it does so in a segment where the households most in need would bear the cost of contraction.

The most important distinction in the archive is between first- and second-generation controls. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents untouched [D1p3]. The stock index shows continued growth through that period, from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this stability is not evidence that tighter control is safe. The working paper explains that first-generation controls show consistent contraction effects, while second-generation controls show small or null effects—and the Hong Kong ordinance is second-generation [D6p2]. The visible stability is thus an artifact of a non-binding instrument. A binding cap on initial rents would behave differently: the reviewed elasticity band suggests a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1].

The distributional consequence is the decisive point. The working paper's model of a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. These are precisely the populations named in the primer as the segment's core tenants [D1p2]. A policy that helps those already inside while shutting out those still outside is regressive in the specific sense that matters here. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

The enforcement evidence reinforces the case. Current inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. More damning, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals—so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A cap that cannot be enforced against the worst landlords is not a cap; it is a signal.

The revealed preferences of tenants themselves undercut the case for a hard cap. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, does not name rent—he names the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not price level. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap, but her own account contains the contradiction the archive flags: she reports two flats converted to non-residential storage after the ordinance while still believing landlords cannot exit [D4p3]. The Landlords Association's threat to exit [D5p2] is self-interested and its data thin—no margin data, a 34 percent response rate on self-reported costs [D5p3]—but the academic elasticity estimates do not depend on taking the Association at its word.

What should Hong Kong do instead? The legal opinion points to a defensible middle instrument: a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, framed as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. The NGO report supports exactly this—tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, opposing a hard cap [D11p3]. The consultation document already appears to be floating a "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap [D12p1]. This is the right direction: it addresses insecurity, which tenants name as the real harm [D3p1][D11p1], without triggering the contraction arithmetic that would fall on new arrivals.

The strongest counterargument is that the 2021 ordinance's stability shows capacity for more control [D12p2]. But that argument misreads the evidence: the instrument was non-binding at first-rent, which is exactly why stock stayed stable [D6p2]. Tightening to a binding cap would change the regime, not extend it. The uncomfortable welfare arithmetic of contraction [D6p3] and the enforcement gap [D10p2–p3] together make a hard cap a poor instrument for a real problem. Hong Kong should secure tenure, publish bands, and above all shorten the queue—because as Mr Chan's answer shows, the rent is not what tenants most need fixed.
