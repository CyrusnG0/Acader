# The Case Against a Hard Cap on SDU Rents

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units through a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead pursue a narrower package: security of tenure plus a light, indexed rent band, coupled with enforcement investment and a serious attack on the public-housing queue. The archive supports this position on three grounds: the elasticity evidence, the enforcement evidence, and the revealed preferences of tenants themselves.

**The elasticity evidence cuts against a hard cap.** The working paper's central finding is that first-generation controls—hard caps on rent levels—show consistent supply contraction, while second-generation controls—caps on increases within tenancies—show small or null effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which explains why stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 even as the ordinance operated [D2p1][D6p2]. That stability is real but misleading as a precedent: it reflects a non-binding instrument. The same paper estimates that a binding cap removing 10 percent of rent would remove 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1], and models a 15 percent cut in initial rents as transferring about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while pushing 3,000 to 4,000 households per year out of the segment entirely—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. The tenants a hard cap protects are those already inside; the bill goes to those still outside.

Berlin illustrates the mechanism in practice. When the Mietendeckel capped rents, classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats [D7p1]. When the law fell, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is explicit: caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those outside [D7p3]. New York's second-generation scheme shows the same asymmetry from the other direction—stock contraction is modest precisely because the scheme is light at first-rent, the exact point Hong Kong's ordinance left untouched [D8p2]. Tightening the first rent would move Hong Kong from the New York model toward the Berlin one.

**Enforcement capacity makes a hard cap largely illusory.** Inspection covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement—like Mrs Wong, who has never received one and reports the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]—or the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A cap that cannot be enforced against the landlords who most need regulating is not tenant protection; it is a signal.

**Tenants themselves prioritize differently.** Mr Chan, facing a rent of 5,800 dollars on a third of household income, does not name rent when asked what would help most—he names the waiting list, and says he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap, but her own account undercuts the exit-fear argument: she reports two flats in her stairwell converted to storage after the ordinance while still believing landlords cannot exit [D4p3]—a contradiction that shows how unreliable anecdotal confidence in landlord behavior is, in either direction.

The landlords' submission deserves skepticism—it concedes no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures rest on a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. But the legal opinion confirms that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream would attract compensation claims and judicial review, with a litigation timeline outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. The middle instrument—an indexed, reviewable per-square-foot band—is defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2].

**Conclusion.** The evidence does not support a hard cap. It supports tenure security, a light band on initial rents, investment in the register and inspection regime the band requires, and above all a shorter queue—the quantity problem beneath the price symptom [D11p2]. Hong Kong should regulate the SDU segment the way New York does, not the way Berlin tried.
