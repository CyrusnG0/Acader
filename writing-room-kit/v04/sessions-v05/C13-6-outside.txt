# Tighten, but Not With a Hard Cap: A Banded, Enforced Middle Path for SDU Rent Control

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs)—but not with a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points to a narrow, defensible package: a published per-square-foot rent band on initial rents, paired with genuine tenure security and a credible enforcement register. A hard cap would score a political win while handing the bill to the households least able to pay it.

The case against a hard cap rests first on the economics. The working paper estimates long-run supply elasticity in marginal housing at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Its modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while pushing 3,000 to 4,000 households per year out of the segment entirely, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. That is the population the policy is meant to protect [D1p2]. Berlin confirms the mechanism in practice: initial-offer rents fell, but classified-listing supply contracted within a year as owners rerouted flats to uncontrolled segments [D7p1]. The bill for protecting insiders was handed to those still outside [D7p3].

The counter-argument—that the stable SDU stock under the 2021 ordinance proves regulation is harmless [D2p1]—misreads the evidence. The working paper is explicit that the 2021 ordinance is a second-generation instrument capping increases within tenancies, and that such controls show small or null stock effects precisely because they leave initial rents untouched [D6p2]. The visible stability is the artifact of a non-binding instrument; a binding cap behaves differently [D12p2]. New York reinforces the point: its light first-rent regulation produces modest contraction [D8p2], but the 2019 tightening is associated with a measurable fall in available stabilised listings [D8p3].

Nor is a hard cap enforceable in the way its advocates assume. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the enforcement design systematically misses the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals, are least able to complain [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A cap that cannot reach the people it targets is not protection; it is symbolism.

The legal route compounds the problem. A hard cap framed as deprivation of rental income would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's property protections, with litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. The Association's submission previews exactly this argument [D5p3], and while its margin data is thin and self-reported [D5p3], the litigation risk is not.

What, then, should be done? The archive converges on a middle instrument. Counsel proposes capping initial rent by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band—defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. The NGO report supports precisely this: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, opposing a hard cap on both the contraction arithmetic and its own survey's revealed preference [D11p3]. That survey is the strongest evidence in the archive: 61 percent of tenants would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan says it plainly—if the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p3]. The binding constraint is quantity, not price [D11p2].

A banded approach also has political legs: the consultation document already floats a "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap [D12p1], and listed developers hold little SDU exposure, so opposition concentrates among small owners the Association represents [D12p3].

The honest caveat: a band requires survey infrastructure that does not yet exist [D9p2], and enforcement will still lag without a register [D10p1]. But these are buildable problems. A hard cap's contraction is not.

Hong Kong should tighten—banded, indexed, enforced, and paired with tenure security and a shorter queue. Protect the incumbents without pricing out the newcomers.
