Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in the second-generation form it already uses: caps on increases within tenancies, not hard caps on rent levels. The evidence in the course archive shows that tenant-side pressure justifies doing more, while the classic objection about supply collapse applies mainly to a different kind of control than the one Hong Kong has.

The case for tightening starts with the tenants' position. About 110,000 households live in SDUs, small cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms [D1p1]. Median SDU rent is roughly 30 percent below mainstream flats, but per square foot SDU tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent, because space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. That premium is the clearest sign of bargaining weakness: tenants are not paying less because the housing is worse in some neutral sense; they are paying more per unit of space because they have nowhere else to go. A rent control regime that leaves this premium intact is not doing its job.

The strongest counter-argument is supply contraction. Standard theory predicts that binding rent ceilings shrink supply, and the reviewed literature estimates that in marginal stock like SDUs, a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That is a real cost, and it should shape policy design rather than be dismissed.

But the same source draws a distinction that matters for Hong Kong. First-generation controls, which are hard caps on rent levels, show consistent contraction effects; second-generation controls, which cap increases within tenancies, show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The 2021 Hong Kong ordinance is second-generation, which explains the stable stock in the D2 index [D6p2]. In other words, the supply objection is strongest against exactly the policy Hong Kong has not adopted. Tightening within the second-generation framework therefore targets the tenant-side problem while avoiding the mechanism that produces the contraction.

Berlin is sometimes cited as a warning, and it is a genuine one, but it is a warning about first-generation design. Berlin capped rents on about 1.5 million flats in 2020; initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply, and classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. The Berlin mechanism was a hard cap on levels, and the response was withholding and rerouting. That is precisely the first-generation pattern the elasticity literature flags [D6p2]. It does not show that capping increases within an existing tenancy produces the same result.

So the fair reading of the archive is this: the tenant-side numbers are bad enough to justify tightening [D1p1], the supply cost of tightening in the second-generation form is small or null [D6p2], and the dramatic contraction story belongs to a policy design Hong Kong has not chosen [D7p1]. The sensible position is to tighten the second-generation controls — for example, by making the increase caps stricter and enforcement stronger — while monitoring stock and resisting any drift toward hard caps on levels. That combination addresses the per-square-foot premium without triggering the withholding and rerouting that Berlin experienced.

One caveat: the elasticity estimates are for SDU-type supply generally, not for Hong Kong specifically [D6p1], so the 3 to 5 percent figure should be treated as a guide, not a prediction. But even taking it at face value, it describes first-generation effects, and the second-generation evidence is reassuring [D6p2]. On balance, tightening is justified.
