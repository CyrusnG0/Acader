# The Case Against a Hard Cap on Subdivided-Unit Rents

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) by imposing a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead pursue a narrow package: security of tenure plus a light, indexed band on initial rents, coupled with a serious expansion of the public-housing pipeline. The archive supports this position on three grounds: the evidence that binding caps shrink the marginal stock, the mismatch between what tenants say they need and what a hard cap delivers, and the enforcement and legal realities that would make a hard cap both costly and regressive in practice.

The central economic question is supply elasticity. The working paper estimates the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Its modelling of a tightened cap for a Hong Kong-like market is stark: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. The 2021 ordinance's apparent success in stabilising stock is not evidence that a harder cap is safe. The paper explicitly distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases), which show small or null effects—and the 2021 ordinance is second-generation [D6p2]. The stable stock in the index [D2p1] is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument, exactly as one think-tank piece argues [D12p2]. The Berlin case reinforces the point: when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The bill for protecting incumbents is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

The tenants themselves do not prioritise rent levels. The NGO survey of 440 households finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan is explicit: asked what would help most, he names the waiting list, not the rent [D3p3]. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap, but her own account undercuts it—she reports two flats in her stairwell converted to storage after the ordinance while still believing landlords cannot exit [D4p3]. That contradiction is the constituency problem in miniature.

Enforcement and law make a hard cap worse still. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based register would take 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]—and the tenants least able to complain are precisely those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1]. Legally, a hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream invites compensation claims and judicial review, with a timeline outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. A band-based instrument, by contrast, regulators can defend as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2].

The counter-argument—that landlords would not exit because SDU income is their pension [D4p2]—is asserted, not evidenced. The Landlords Association concedes it has no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures rest on a 210-member survey with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported [D5p3]. That cuts both ways: we should not design policy on the Association's confident exit threats, but neither should we dismiss the elasticity evidence [D6p1] on the strength of one tenant's intuition.

The strongest objection to my position is moral: the status quo lets landlords extract a 15 to 25 percent per-square-foot premium from desperation [D1p1], and Mrs Wong's experience shows the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. That is a real harm. But the remedy must target the quantity problem, not the price symptom. The segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2]; every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire, partition and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

The defensible policy is therefore the one the consultation is reportedly floating: a floor-standard plus rent band, with tenure security, indexed and reviewable [D12p1][D9p2]. It protects incumbents without pretending to protect entrants it would actually exclude, and it keeps the state's scarce capacity aimed at the queue—the thing Mr Chan says he would pay any rent to escape [D3p3]. A hard cap would feel like action while handing the bill to the households least able to pay it.
