# Tighten, But Not With a Hard Cap: A Targeted Case for Banded Initial Rents and Tenure Security

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs)—but not with the hard initial-rent cap that dominates public debate. The evidence in this archive points to a narrower, better-defended package: a published per-square-foot band on initial rents, paired with genuine tenure security and enforcement capacity. A hard cap would help some incumbents while shrinking entry for the households the policy exists to protect.

The case against a hard cap rests first on supply economics. The academic literature estimates long-run supply elasticity in marginal housing at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Modelled for a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers roughly 900 dollars monthly to incumbents but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. That is precisely the population named in the policy brief as the residual users of SDUs [D1p2]. The Berlin precedent confirms the mechanism: listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

The strongest counter-argument—that the 2021 ordinance produced stable stock—actually undermines the hard-cap case. Stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, with no visible exit after the ordinance [D2p1]. But that ordinance was second-generation: it capped increases within tenancies, not initial rent levels [D1p3][D6p2]. The literature shows second-generation controls have small or null stock effects, while first-generation hard caps show consistent contraction [D6p2]. As one think-tank piece argues, the visible stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument; a binding one behaves differently [D12p2]. Citing 2021 stability to justify a hard cap is a category error.

The tenants' own testimony is striking. Mr Chan, facing a 5,800-dollar rent on a third of household income, names not rent but the waiting list: at three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p1][D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not price. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap, but her own account—no written tenancy, a landlord pricing to "whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears"—shows the problem is enforcement and documentation, not the ceiling's height [D4p1]. She also reports two stairwell flats converted to storage after the ordinance while believing landlords cannot exit [D4p3]; her confidence is not evidence.

Enforcement is the decisive practical objection. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement or the newest arrivals—would be missed first, so enforcement would systematically fail the most exploited tail [D10p2][D10p3]. A hard cap that cannot be enforced is a slogan, not a policy.

What should be done? First, adopt the legal opinion's middle instrument: a published, indexed, reviewable per-square-foot band on initial rents, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation and less exposed to Basic Law compensation claims and judicial review [D9p1][D9p2]. The Association's exit threat [D5p2] deserves weight but is weakened by its own concessions—no data on actual margins, and compliance costs from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Second, pair the band with tenure security, the reform tenants actually name [D11p1]. Third, invest in the register and inspection capacity, because without it any instrument is nominal [D10p1]. Fourth, treat the public-housing queue as the real lever: every year cut from the five-and-a-half-year wait reduces SDU demand more than any rent ceiling [D1p2][D3p3].

The NGO report is right that caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2]. But that does not counsel doing nothing—it counsels doing the narrow thing well. A banded initial-rent cap plus tenure security protects incumbents, preserves entry, and survives legal challenge. A hard cap would deliver a headline, a lawsuit, and fewer units for the people it claims to help. Hong Kong should tighten—carefully, and not with the blunt instrument.
