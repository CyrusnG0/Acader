Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs). The current regime has slowed rent growth, but it has not gone far enough: SDU tenants still pay a large per-square-foot premium, their rent burden has actually worsened under the existing ordinance, and the stock of units has kept growing, so there is no evidence that stronger controls would cause landlords to exit the market. Tightening is the fair and workable next step.

First, the background matters. Around 110,000 households live in SDUs, which are flats carved into small cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms [D1p1]. The median SDU rent is about 30 percent below mainstream private flats, but per square foot tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent, because space is "priced at its most desperate margin" [D1p1]. That premium is the clearest sign that the market is not working for tenants. People are not paying more per square foot because SDUs are better; they are paying more because they have nowhere else to go. A rent control regime that leaves this premium intact is not doing its job.

Second, the existing ordinance has helped but only modestly. Median SDU rent per square foot rose 6.8 percent in 2019–2021, then 3.1 percent in 2021–2023 under the ordinance, compared with general private rent growth of 2.4 and 1.7 percent in the same windows [D2p2]. So SDU rents did slow down after regulation, which is a real achievement and shows that controls are not automatically counterproductive. But SDU rents still grew faster than general private rents, and the real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. In other words, the ordinance bent the curve but did not break it. If the goal is to protect tenants, the current settings are too loose.

Third, the usual objection to tighter rent control — that landlords will withdraw units and shrink supply — is not supported by the evidence here. Estimated SDU stock rose from 100,800 units in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. Growth continued through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, which suggests moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit [D2p1]. This is important because it means the government can tighten rules without fearing a collapse in supply. Landlords are clearly still finding it worthwhile to operate SDUs even under controls, so the trade-off that critics warn about is, at least in this market, mostly hypothetical.

Fourth, the people affected are exactly the ones policy should protect. SDU tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households and residents waiting for public housing, where the average wait has hovered around five and a half years [D1p2]. For these households the SDU is not a choice but a residual option while the queue moves [D1p2]. When tenants have no bargaining power and no exit option, a lightly regulated market will keep extracting the premium described in [D1p1]. Tighter controls — for example, a lower cap on per-square-foot increases and stronger enforcement against evasion — would target that extraction directly.

One honest caveat: tighter controls will not fix the root cause, which is the public housing queue and overall land supply. But that is an argument for doing more on supply, not for doing less on rent control in the meantime. The evidence shows controls can slow rents [D2p2] without shrinking stock [D2p1], and that tenants remain under serious pressure [D1p2]. Tightening rent control on SDUs is therefore justified: it is proportionate, it is backed by the archive's own numbers, and it protects the households with the least ability to protect themselves.
