Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but not with a hard cap on initial rents. The defensible move is a narrow one: security of tenure first, then a light, indexed rent band, introduced only as enforcement capacity is built. The evidence in the archive supports acting, and it also shows why the most obvious form of action would backfire.

The status quo is not neutral. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they pay a per-square-foot premium of 15 to 25 percent [D1p1]. Even under the 2021 ordinance, their rent-to-income burden rose from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. The ordinance capped increases within a tenancy but left the initial rent untouched [D1p3], and Mrs Wong, nine years in her unit with no written agreement, says it changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. Mr Chan's worst experience was not price but a two-week eviction notice after he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. Doing nothing leaves tenants exposed on exactly the points that hurt most.

A hard cap, however, would hit the wrong people. The working paper estimates supply elasticity at 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. It also explains why Hong Kong's stock kept growing after 2021 [D2p1]: the ordinance is a second-generation control, and those show small or null stock effects, while hard caps show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The think-tank argument that stable stock proves capacity for more control therefore confuses a non-binding instrument with a binding one [D12p2]. For a 15 percent cut in initial rents, the paper models a transfer of about 900 dollars a month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households a year unable to enter, mostly new arrivals [D6p4]. Berlin shows the same split: protection for those inside, with the bill handed to those outside [D7p3], after listings contracted and rents rebounded above trend [D7p1, D7p2].

The exit threat is credible even though the landlords' own case is weak. The Association admits it has no data on members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. But tenants describe the behaviour independently. Mr Chan has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked [D3p2], and Mrs Wong reports two flats in her stairwell converted to storage, even as she insists landlords cannot leave [D4p3]. Much of this stock belongs to small individual owners for whom the unit is a pension [D4p2, D12p3], and these are the owners least likely to absorb a loss. A hard cap would also invite compensation claims and judicial review that would outlast a Council term [D9p1, D9p3].

The narrower instrument has better support. Counsel considers a per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. Tenants say what they want: in the NGO survey, 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan says the same, naming the waiting list rather than rent [D3p3]. The NGO's own recommendation is tenure security plus a light band, and it opposes a hard cap [D11p3]. The consultation is reportedly heading the same way, toward a floor standard plus a rent band [D12p1]. New York's light-touch second-generation scheme shows modest stock contraction [D8p2], though it also shows costs in maintenance and available listings [D8p3], which is a reason to keep the band light.

The strongest objection to my own position is enforcement. Inspection covers under 6 percent of stock a year, and a certifiable tenancy register would take an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. The subcommittee's draft warns that complaint-driven enforcement misses tenants with no written agreement and the newest arrivals [D10p2], and it could not identify a design that protects that group first [D10p3]. Those are the households the policy exists for [D1p2]. This is why tenure security must lead: a written tenancy and protection against retaliatory eviction give tenants something to complain with, and they cost far less than policing rent levels. The NGO adds that enforcement spent on rents is not spent on fire, partition and ventilation hazards where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

The evidence has limits. Post-2023 stock figures rest on survey extrapolation [D2p3], the NGO is an advocate, and the elasticity range comes from other markets. These uncertainties argue for a staged, reviewable band rather than a permanent rule.

My answer is yes to tightening, no to a hard cap. Legislate tenure security and written tenancies now, build the survey and register infrastructure, and bring in an indexed band only once it can reach the tenants who cannot complain. The root problem is the queue [D1p2, D11p2], and no rent rule fixes that.
