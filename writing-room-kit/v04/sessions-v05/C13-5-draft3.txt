# Tighten, but Target the Right Instrument: A Case for a Band-Based Cap on Initial SDU Rents

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units—but not with a hard cap. The archive points toward a narrower instrument: a published, indexed per-square-foot band on initial rents, paired with tenure security and a serious enforcement register. This design is not invented here. The legal opinion assesses a "middle instrument": cap initial rent "by reference to a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable," which regulators can defend as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. The NGO report independently endorses "a narrow tightening—tenure security plus a light band on initial rents" while opposing a hard cap [D11p3]. Two separate sources converge on the same design, which is why it anchors this essay.

**The incumbent-versus-entrant trade-off.** A hard cap on rent levels would help incumbents while shrinking entry for the very households the policy aims to protect. The working paper's model of a Hong Kong-like market makes the arithmetic explicit: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. The paper calls this "uncomfortable": contraction removes units at the bottom, and the households that lose them are the same population the cap intends to protect [D6p3]. A hard cap thus redistributes toward those already inside and away from those still outside the door.

**Why the 2021 stock stability proves nothing about a binding cap.** The academic literature distinguishes two families of control. First-generation controls—hard caps on rent levels—consistently produce supply contraction in the reviewed evidence; second-generation controls, which cap increases within an existing tenancy, show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The estimated elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The stable SDU stock through the 2021 ordinance—100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025—is therefore not proof that a binding cap is safe; it is evidence that a non-binding, second-generation instrument did not bite [D2p1][D6p2]. One think-tank piece reads the stable stock as room for more control, but the other correctly calls it an artifact of a non-binding instrument [D12p2].

**Berlin shows the mechanism in the wild.** The 2020 Mietendeckel capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats. Initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply, but classified-listing supply then contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. When the constitutional court struck the law down in 2021—on federal-competence grounds, not economic ones—a backlog of suppressed increases released into the market, and rents for new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The lesson is precise: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents; the tenants the cap protects are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

**Why inaction is indefensible.** SDU tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats, priced at the most desperate margin [D1p1]. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents unregulated [D1p3], and Mrs Wong's experience shows what that means: no written agreement, rents raised by "whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears," and no visible change from the ordinance [D4p1]. Real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the current regime [D2p2]. Doing nothing is not neutral—it ratifies the extraction.

**Why a band is the defensible middle.** A hard cap framed as deprivation of an income stream would attract compensation claims and judicial review under the Basic Law's property protections, while a cap framed as contractual-term regulation sits on firmer ground but leaves initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated [D9p1]. A published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, can be defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. This matters because the Association's exit threat—self-interested and thinly evidenced, with no data on actual margins and compliance costs from a 210-member survey at 34 percent response, self-reported [D5p3]—would gain legal traction against a confiscatory framing. A band blunts that argument.

**Two caveats must be built in, not bolted on.** First, enforcement. Current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail—tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design protecting the newest-arrival tail first rather than last [D10p3]. Any tightening must be sequenced behind register infrastructure, or it is theatre.

Second, rent is not the binding constraint tenants name. Sixty-one percent of surveyed households would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security; 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan does not name rent—he names the waiting list, and would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. His fear that landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants is not hypothetical: he has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]. Mrs Wong's own stairwell contains two flats converted to non-residential storage—a contradiction she does not see [D4p3]. This is why the band must be light and paired with tenure security [D11p3].
