# Tighten, but Not the Way the Debate Suggests

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs)—but not with the hard initial-rent cap that dominates public debate. The evidence supports a narrow tightening: a light, indexed band on initial rents combined with security of tenure, backed by the inspection and registration infrastructure the current regime lacks. A hard cap would help incumbents while shrinking entry for the very households the policy names as its beneficiaries.

The case for doing something is strong. SDUs house roughly 110,000 households, and tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats for the worst space in the market [D1p1]. They are disproportionately new arrivals, single parents, and public-housing applicants facing a five-and-a-half-year queue [D1p2]. Real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2], and Mrs Wong's experience—nine years without a written tenancy, rents rising "by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears"—shows the existing second-generation controls leave the actual pain point untouched [D4p1][D9p1]. Mr Chan's story adds the deeper harm: insecurity, with two weeks' notice after requesting a repair [D3p1].

But the instrument matters enormously. The academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies). First-generation controls show consistent stock contraction; second-generation controls show small or null effects—which explains why SDU stock grew steadily through the 2021 ordinance [D6p2][D2p1]. The apparent stability of the 2021 regime is therefore evidence that a *non-binding* instrument is safe, not that a binding one would be [D12p2]. The estimated elasticity of 0.31–0.48 implies a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled 15 percent cut yields about $900 monthly to incumbents but leaves 3,000–4,000 households per year unable to enter—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin confirms the pattern: caps protect those already inside while the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3], and when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p2].

The landlord association's exit threat deserves skepticism. It concedes it holds no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures rest on a 210-member survey with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation cuts the other way too: units are landlords' pensions, and "where else would the money come from?" [D4p2]. Yet her own stairwell contains two flats converted to storage after the ordinance—a contradiction she does not see [D4p3], and one that suggests exit is real at the margin even if the association overstates it.

The decisive problem with a hard cap is enforcement. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, and the newest arrivals—are precisely those enforcement would miss [D10p2]. The subcommittee could identify no design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would thus be strongest on paper and weakest where harm is greatest.

What would work? The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan himself names the queue, not the rent: at three years instead of five and a half, "he would accept any rent to leave" [D3p3]. Tenure security is enforceable through the existing ordinance framework, and a light indexed band—defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation—avoids the compensation and judicial-review risk of a hard cap [D9p2]. The legal opinion warns that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income would almost certainly attract Basic Law claims, with litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. The consultation's expected "floor-standard + rent band" package points in exactly this direction [D12p1].

The objection is that a light band leaves incumbents like Mrs Wong exposed. That is a real cost, but the alternative trades their modest gain for excluding thousands of households annually from any unit at all [D6p4]. The queue, not the price, is the quantity problem [D11p2]. Tighten—yes. But tighten tenure and enforce conditions first, cap initial rents only lightly, and spend the enforcement capacity where the harm is documented and immediate.
