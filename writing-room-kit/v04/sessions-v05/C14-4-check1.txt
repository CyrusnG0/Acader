# Tighten, but Not With a Hard Cap: A Targeted Case for Tenure Security and Rent Bands

Hong Kong should tighten regulation of subdivided units (SDUs)—but it should not impose a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points to a narrower, better-targeted package: security of tenure plus a light, indexed rent band. A hard cap would help some incumbents while shrinking the residual stock that the most vulnerable households depend on, and its enforcement would systematically fail the tenants it is meant to protect.

The case for *some* tightening is strong. SDU tenants already pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats, precisely because they are priced at the most desperate margin [D1p1]. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs from key provisions [D1p3]—so the actual pain point went unregulated. Mrs Wong's experience confirms this: her landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears," and she has never had a written agreement [D4p1]. Real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Doing nothing is not neutral.

Yet a hard cap on initial rent levels is the wrong instrument, for three reasons.

First, the stock evidence cuts against the "regulation is free" reading. The SDU stock grew steadily through 2021, from 100,800 to 111,200 units by 2025 [D2p1]—but the 2021 ordinance was *second-generation* regulation, capping increases rather than levels, and the academic literature finds such controls have small or null stock effects, whereas first-generation caps on levels show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The visible stability is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument, not proof that a binding cap is safe [D12p2]. The estimated elasticity of marginal supply is 0.31–0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. In the modelled Hong Kong scenario, a 15 percent initial-rent cut transfers about $900 a month to incumbents but leaves 3,000–4,000 households a year unable to enter—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin is the cautionary case: caps protected those already inside, and "the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3]. Mr Chan's fear that landlords would rather lock units as storage than let them to regulated tenants is not irrational—he has watched it happen [D3p2], and Mrs Wong reports the same on her stairwell even while believing exit is impossible [D4p3].

Second, the enforcement arithmetic is damning. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based cap needs a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. The tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement [D4p1] and the newest arrivals—would be missed first, so enforcement would "systematically miss the most exploited tail" [D10p2]. The subcommittee could identify no design protecting the newest-arrival tail first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would thus be strongest on paper and weakest where harm is greatest.

Third, the revealed preferences of tenants themselves point elsewhere. In a 440-household survey, 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, "does not name rent. He names the waiting list" [D3p3]. The binding constraint is a quantity problem—queue length and insecurity—not price [D11p2].

What should be done? A package of (a) statutory security of tenure, addressing the two-week-notice insecurity Mr Chan describes [D3p1]; (b) a light initial-rent band referenced to a published per-square-foot range, indexed and reviewable, which counsel judges defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation and less exposed to Basic Law compensation claims than a hard cap [D9p1–D9p2]; and (c) investment in the tenancy register and inspection capacity that any regime requires [D10p1]. This aligns with the consultation's expected "floor-standard + rent band" direction [D12p1] and the NGO's narrow-tightening recommendation [D11p3], while heeding the landlords' exit threat [D5p2]—not by trusting their self-reported margins [D5p3], but by keeping returns above the exit threshold.

The honest concession: a band leaves initial rents partly unregulated, and incumbents gain less than under a hard cap. But a cap that empties units helps no one who loses one, and a rule that cannot be enforced protects no one it targets. Tighten—targeted, enforceable, and tenure-first.
