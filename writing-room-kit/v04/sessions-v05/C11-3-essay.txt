Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only through second-generation controls that cap rent increases within tenancies rather than imposing hard caps on rent levels. The evidence in the course archive shows that SDU tenants are genuinely vulnerable and need stronger protection, and it also shows that the form of control matters enormously for whether the policy backfires.

The case for stronger protection starts with the conditions themselves. Around 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and although the median SDU rent is roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats, tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot because space is carved up and priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. This is the key point: SDU tenants are not getting a bargain. They are paying more per unit of space precisely because they have nowhere else to go. The archive also shows who these tenants are. They are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households, and residents waiting for public housing, where the average wait has hovered around five and a half years [D1p2]. For these households the SDU is "not a choice but a residual option while the queue moves" [D1p2]. That combination — high per-square-foot costs, weak bargaining power, and a long queue for the alternative — is exactly the situation in which tenants are exposed to sharp rent hikes. A landlord knows the tenant cannot easily move without losing proximity to work, school, or support networks, and cannot move into public housing for years. In that setting, leaving rents entirely to the market means the weakest households absorb the pressure.

The obvious counter-argument is that rent ceilings shrink supply, and the archive does support this for one type of control. Standard theory predicts supply contraction under binding rent ceilings, and in marginal stock like SDUs the long-run elasticity of supply to controlled rent levels is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That is a real cost, and it is why I would not defend first-generation hard caps on rent levels. If the policy pushed rents down sharply, some landlords would exit the SDU market, and the tenants who need these units most could find fewer of them available.

But the same source draws a distinction that changes the conclusion. First-generation controls, which cap rent levels, show consistent contraction effects; second-generation controls, which cap increases within tenancies, show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The 2021 Hong Kong ordinance is second-generation, and the stable stock in the D2 index is consistent with that [D6p2]. This is the decisive evidence for my position. The supply objection is strongest against the policy I am not proposing. A cap on increases within a tenancy protects sitting tenants from sudden hikes without forcing the rent level below market, so it does not create the same incentive for landlords to withdraw stock. The estimated 3 to 5 percent stock loss applies to level caps, not to the within-tenancy approach [D6p1][D6p2].

Putting the two strands together, the archive supports a clear position. The living conditions and tenant profile show that protection is needed: tenants pay a per-square-foot premium [D1p1], they are the households with the least ability to absorb increases, and they face a five-and-a-half-year wait for public housing [D1p2]. The supply evidence shows that the design of the protection determines whether it helps or hurts them. Tightening rent control in the second-generation sense — stronger limits on increases within tenancies — targets the actual vulnerability, which is the risk of a sharp hike, while avoiding the contraction associated with hard caps [D6p2]. A policy that caps increases therefore protects tenants without shrinking the stock they depend on.

For these reasons, Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but it should do so by strengthening within-tenancy increase caps rather than by imposing hard rent ceilings. The evidence on conditions justifies stronger protection [D1p1][D1p2], and the evidence on supply tells us which kind of protection to choose [D6p1][D6p2].
