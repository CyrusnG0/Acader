# The Case for a Narrow Tightening, Not a Hard Cap

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs), but only through a narrow instrument combining tenure security with a light, indexed band on initial rents. A hard cap on initial rent levels would likely harm the very households it aims to protect. The archive supports this middle path on three grounds: the evidence on supply contraction, the enforcement realities facing the newest and most vulnerable tenants, and the revealed preferences of tenants themselves.

**The contraction risk is real but instrument-dependent.** The academic literature estimates the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Crucially, this contraction is associated with first-generation controls—hard caps on rent levels—while second-generation controls capping increases within tenancies show small or null stock effects [D6p2]. The stable SDU stock from 2019 to 2025, growing from 100,800 to 111,200 units through the 2021 ordinance [D2p1], is consistent with this distinction: the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched [D1p3][D6p2]. The stability is therefore evidence that *moderate* regulation does not trigger exit—not that a binding hard cap would be equally benign. The think-tank argument that visible stability proves "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2] misreads the instrument. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents would transfer about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while pushing 3,000 to 4,000 households per year out of the segment entirely, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4].

**Enforcement would protect the wrong tail first.** A hard cap is only as real as its inspection regime, and current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually [D10p1]. The tenants least able to complain are precisely those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], and the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would thus create a paper right that the most exploited households cannot exercise.
