# Tighten, but Target the Right Instrument: A Case for a Band-Based Cap on Initial SDU Rents

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units—but not with a hard cap. The evidence in this archive points toward a narrower, better-targeted instrument: a published, indexed per-square-foot band on initial rents, paired with tenure security and a serious enforcement register. A hard cap on rent levels would help incumbents while shrinking entry for the very households the policy is meant to protect; doing nothing leaves the actual pain point—unregulated initial rents—untouched. The band approach threads between these failures.

**The case against a hard cap is strong but not absolute.** The academic literature is clear that first-generation controls—hard caps on rent levels—produce consistent supply contraction, while second-generation controls on increases within tenancies show small or null effects [D6p2]. The stable SDU stock through the 2021 ordinance (100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025) is therefore not proof that a binding cap is safe; it is evidence that a non-binding instrument did not bite [D2p1][D6p2][D12p2]. The modelled tightened-cap scenario is sobering: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers roughly 900 dollars monthly to incumbents but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism in the wild: new-listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is precise—caps protect those already inside and hand the bill to those still outside [D7p3].

**Yet the status quo is indefensible.** SDU tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats, priced at the most desperate margin [D1p1]. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents unregulated [D1p3], and Mrs Wong's experience shows what that means in practice: no written agreement, rents raised by "whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears," and no visible change from the ordinance [D4p1]. Real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the current regime [D2p2]. Doing nothing is not neutral—it ratifies the extraction.

**The band instrument is the defensible middle.** Counsel assesses that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream would attract compensation claims and judicial review, while a cap framed as contractual-term regulation sits on firmer ground but leaves initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated [D9p1]. A published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, can be defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. This matters because the Association's exit threat, while self-interested and thinly evidenced—no data on actual margins, compliance costs from a 210-member survey with 34 percent response, self-reported [D5p3]—would gain legal traction against a confiscatory framing. A band blunts that argument.

**Two caveats must be built in, not bolted on.** First, enforcement. Current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail—tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design protecting the newest-arrival tail first rather than last [D10p3]. Any tightening must therefore be sequenced behind register infrastructure, or it is theatre.

Second, rent is not the binding constraint tenants name. Sixty-one percent of surveyed households would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security; 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan does not name rent—he names the waiting list, and would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. His fear that landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants is not hypothetical: he has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]. Mrs Wong's own stairwell contains two flats converted to non-residential storage—a contradiction she does not see [D4p3]. This is why the band must be light and paired with tenure security, as the NGO report recommends [D11p3].
