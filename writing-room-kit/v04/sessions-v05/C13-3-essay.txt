# Tighten, but Not With a Hard Cap: A Case for Tenure Security and a Light Rent Band

Hong Kong should tighten regulation of subdivided units (SDUs), but it should not impose a hard cap on initial rents. The archive points toward a narrower package: enforceable security of tenure plus a published, indexed rent band. A hard cap would help some incumbents while shrinking the bottom of the market and failing the very households it is meant to protect. The evidence on stock, enforcement and tenant preferences all points the same way.

The first question is whether tightening would destroy supply. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance offers a natural experiment: SDU stock rose from 107,300 units in 2021 to 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025, suggesting moderate regulation did not trigger visible exit [D2p1]. But this stability is misleading as a guide to a harder instrument. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within tenancies, not initial rents [D1p3], and the academic literature distinguishes exactly these two designs: first-generation controls on rent levels show consistent contraction, while second-generation controls on increases show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The stable stock in the index is therefore evidence that a non-binding instrument does not bind, not that a binding one would be harmless.

How large would contraction be? The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households a year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those losers are precisely the population the policy intends to protect [D1p2]. The Berlin case confirms the mechanism: when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2]. The bill for protecting insiders is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Tenant evidence complicates the story but does not rescue the hard cap. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap and believes her landlords would never exit, yet she also reports two flats in her stairwell converted to storage after the ordinance—a contradiction she does not see [D4p3]. Mr Chan is more clear-eyed: a cap would free perhaps 800 dollars a month, but he fears landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants, and he has seen units locked for a year [D3p2]. Asked what would help most, he does not name rent; he names the waiting list [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households reinforces this: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not price.

Enforcement seals the argument. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], and the newest arrivals—would be missed first, so enforcement would systematically fail the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would also invite compensation claims and judicial review, with a litigation timeline outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

The alternative is a light band on initial rents, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation, paired with real tenure security [D9p2]. The NGO report endorses exactly this narrow tightening and opposes a hard cap on both contraction arithmetic and revealed preference [D11p3]. The government's expected "floor-standard + rent band" package moves in this direction [D12p1]. The objection that the 2021 ordinance's stability proves capacity for more control [D12p2] misreads a non-binding instrument; the counter that a binding cap behaves differently [D12p2] is correct, which is why the band must be indexed, reviewable and backed by an inspection regime before it is imposed.

Tighten, then—but tighten tenure first, and price second. A hard cap protects today's tenants at tomorrow's tenants' expense. A band plus security of tenure protects the tail the current market abandons, without pretending the queue is a rent problem.
