Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units beyond the current second-generation framework, because the evidence suggests stricter caps would deliver only modest tenant relief while risking a slow contraction of the very stock low-income households depend on. The case for tightening is understandable but ultimately weaker than it first appears.

The case for tightening rests on real hardship. About 110,000 households live in SDUs, and while median SDU rent is roughly 30 percent below mainstream flats, tenants pay a per-square-foot premium of 15 to 25 percent because space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. These tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households and residents waiting for public housing, where the average wait has hovered around five and a half years [D1p2]. For these households the SDU is a residual option, not a choice [D1p2]. On this reading, tighter caps look like straightforward protection.

But the supply evidence complicates that story. Standard theory predicts supply contraction under binding rent ceilings, though the elasticity is an empirical question [D6p1]. In marginal stock like SDUs, the long-run elasticity of supply to controlled rent levels is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That is not catastrophic, but it is real, and it falls hardest on the households with the fewest alternatives.

The counter-argument is that the 2021 ordinance did not shrink stock. Estimated SDU stock rose from 100,800 units in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025, with growth continuing through the ordinance [D2p1]. This is genuine evidence that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit [D2p1]. However, the mechanism matters: the literature distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies). First-generation controls show consistent contraction effects, while second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock — and the 2021 Hong Kong ordinance is second-generation, which explains the stable stock [D6p2]. So the stability of stock under the 2021 rules is evidence for keeping a second-generation framework, not evidence that tightening toward harder caps is harmless. Tightening would move policy closer to the first-generation end of the spectrum, where contraction effects are consistent [D6p2].

This is why I would not tighten. The current ordinance already caps increases within tenancies, and the stock data show it did not cause visible exit [D2p1][D6p2]. Stricter caps would push toward level controls with a known contraction record [D6p2], while the per-square-foot premium that actually burdens tenants [D1p1] is driven by scarcity and carving, not merely by the size of annual increases. A tighter cap does not create space; it mainly redistributes the existing marginal stock and, at the margin, reduces the incentive to keep providing it [D6p1].

What should happen instead is enforcement and supply-side policy. The premium persists because tenants have nowhere else to go while the public housing queue moves [D1p2]; the durable fix is faster turnover of that queue and more transitional supply, not a harder ceiling on a stock that is already growing slowly [D2p1]. Tightening rent control treats the symptom while risking the stock. Hong Kong should hold the second-generation framework, enforce it properly, and address the underlying shortage.
