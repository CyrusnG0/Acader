Hong Kong should not adopt a hard cap on initial rents for subdivided units, but it should tighten in a narrower way: a floor standard, security of tenure, and a light, indexed rent band. The strongest case against a hard cap is that its costs fall on the very people it is meant to help, and the evidence in the archive points consistently in that direction.

Start with who lives in these units. About 110,000 households occupy SDUs, and they are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households and people waiting for public housing, where the wait averages around five and a half years [D1p1][D1p2]. For them the SDU is a residual option while the queue moves. That matters because any policy that shrinks the stock lands hardest on exactly this group.

Does a cap shrink the stock? The working paper says the answer is empirical, and its estimate is that the long-run supply elasticity in marginal housing is 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The paper's distinction between generations of control is the key. First-generation controls, which cap levels, show consistent contraction. Second-generation controls, which cap increases within tenancies, show small or null effects, and the 2021 Hong Kong ordinance is of the second kind [D6p2]. This explains why stock kept growing from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. Supporters of tighter control read that stability as proof of spare capacity, but the better reading is that a non-binding instrument tells us little about a binding one [D12p2]. The paper models a 15 percent initial-rent cut: about 900 dollars a month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households a year shut out [D6p4].

That is not just theory. Berlin's rent cap was followed by a measurable contraction in listings within a year, and after the court struck it down, suppressed increases were released and new-contract rents rose above the earlier trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The lesson drawn there is that the tenants a cap protects are those already inside, while the bill goes to those outside [D7p3].

The landlord side confirms the exit risk, though it should be discounted. The Association says members will leave if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2], but it admits it has no data on actual margins and relies on a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. The testimony of tenants is more persuasive. Mr Chan fears landlords would convert cubicles to storage rather than accept regulated tenants, and he has watched two units on his floor emptied [D3p2]. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap and insists landlords cannot leave, yet she reports two flats in her stairwell converted to storage after the ordinance [D4p2][D4p3]. Her own observation undercuts her belief.

Two further constraints weaken the case for a hard cap. The first is legal. Counsel judges that a hard cap would almost certainly draw compensation claims and judicial review, with litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. The second is enforcement. Inspection covers under 6 percent of stock a year, and a band-based cap would need a tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the subcommittee recognised that enforcement relies on complaints, so it would miss the tenants with no written agreement and the newest arrivals, and it could not find a design that protects that tail first [D10p2][D10p3]. A cap that fails the most exploited households is a weak instrument for protecting them.

What tenants actually want points elsewhere. In the NGO survey of 440 households, 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, names the waiting list rather than rent [D3p3]. Rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2], so the pain is real, but the NGO is right that spending scarce enforcement capacity on rents diverts it from fire and ventilation inspection, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

The defensible tightening is therefore the package the NGO supports and the government is reportedly floating: tenure security plus a light, published per-square-foot band on initial rents, reviewable and indexed so it can be defended as rate regulation rather than confiscation [D9p2][D11p3][D12p1]. This should be introduced only alongside the survey and register infrastructure it needs, with data collection made mandatory, since current figures already rely on extrapolation [D2p3]. It should come with a push to shorten the queue, because the segment is tight chiefly because the queue is long. Tighten, yes, but in the way that protects people still waiting outside.
