# Tighten, But Not With a Hard Cap: A Targeted Case for Rent Bands and Tenure Security

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) — but not with the hard initial-rent cap that dominates the debate. The archive points toward a narrower instrument: a published per-square-foot band on initial rents, paired with enforceable tenure security. The case rests on three findings: moderate regulation has not shrunk the stock, a hard cap would predictably do so, and the harm tenants most fear is insecurity rather than price.

The first question any tightening must answer is whether it shrinks the stock. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance offers a natural experiment, and the answer is reassuring: estimated SDU stock rose steadily from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, growth that continued through the ordinance [D2p1]. The academic literature explains why. Second-generation controls — caps on increases within tenancies — show small or null effects on stock, and the 2021 ordinance is precisely that type [D6p2]. The stability is real, but it is also partly an artifact: the ordinance capped increases within an existing tenancy without touching the initial rent, which is where the pain actually sits [D1p3]. A binding cap would behave differently. The reviewed elasticity of SDU-type supply is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is stark: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households a year unable to enter [D6p4]. Berlin shows the same mechanism in the wild — initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2].

The second question is who the cap actually protects. Berlin's lesson is that caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents: the tenants protected are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3]. In Hong Kong the outside population is not abstract — it is new arrivals and residents waiting five and a half years for public housing, for whom the SDU is a residual option while the queue moves [D1p2]. The enforcement minute makes the point uncomfortably concrete: the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement or the newest arrivals, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

Yet the status quo is indefensible. Mrs Wong has paid rent for nine years without a written tenancy agreement, and the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. Mr Chan's worst experience was not price but a two-week notice period when he asked to repair a leaking window [D3p1]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan himself names the waiting list, not rent, as what would help most [D3p3]. The rent burden is worsening regardless — rent-to-income rose from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2] — so doing nothing is not neutral.

The middle instrument fits the evidence. Counsel assesses a cap on initial rent by reference to a published, indexed, reviewable per-square-foot band, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation and therefore less exposed to Basic Law compensation claims than a hard cap [D9p1][D9p2]. The NGO report endorses exactly this combination — tenure security plus a light band — while opposing a hard cap on both the contraction arithmetic and its own survey's revealed preference [D11p3]. The government's expected consultation package already floats a "floor-standard + rent band" design [D12p1].

Two caveats must be faced honestly. The band needs infrastructure that does not exist: a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years, against inspection capacity covering under 6 percent of stock per year [D10p1]. And the landlords' exit threat deserves scrutiny rather than dismissal: the Association represents 18,000 units and commits to exit below mortgage cost, but concedes it holds no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures rest on a 210-member self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p2][D5p3]. A reviewable band, unlike a hard cap, gives regulators room to calibrate against verified cost data.

The quantity problem behind the price problem — the queue — must be addressed in parallel; no rent instrument shortens it. But within housing policy, the choice is not between a hard cap and nothing. It is between an instrument that protects incumbents while shutting out entrants, and one that stabilises the segment without shrinking it. Hong Kong should tighten — through bands and tenure security, not a hard cap.
