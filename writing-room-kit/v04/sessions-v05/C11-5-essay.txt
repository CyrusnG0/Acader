Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units. While the impulse to protect tenants is understandable, the evidence in the course archive suggests that tighter caps would hurt the very people they aim to help, and would distract from the deeper problems of supply and security that tenants themselves identify as most urgent.

First, it helps to be clear about what is at stake. Around 110,000 households live in subdivided units (SDUs), flats carved into small cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms. Median SDU rent is roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats, but per square foot tenants actually pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent, because space is carved and priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. That premium is a real injustice, but it is a symptom of scarcity, not of greedy pricing alone, and that distinction matters for policy.

The clearest evidence on what hard caps actually do comes from Berlin. The city capped rents on about 1.5 million flats at 2020 levels. Reported initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply, but classified-listing supply then contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. In other words, the cap did not create more affordable homes; it made some homes disappear from the controlled market. When the constitutional court struck the law down in 2021, a backlog of suppressed increases released into the market and rents for new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The lesson is precise: caps bind differently on new entrants than on incumbents, protecting those already inside while handing the bill to those still outside [D7p3]. In Hong Kong, where SDU tenants are highly mobile and turnover is frequent, a large share of tenants would be "still outside" at any given moment.

There is also a supply-side objection specific to Hong Kong. The Association representing owners of roughly 18,000 SDU units argues that a hard initial-rent cap is confiscatory, since many owners hold thin margins after mortgage payments, rates and the 2021 compliance costs of windows, wiring and fire doors [D5p1]. If those owners exit or withhold units, the segment tightens further, and the tenants who lose are again the ones looking for a unit now.

Most importantly, the tenants themselves point elsewhere. A survey of 440 SDU households finds that the binding constraint named by tenants is not rent level but queue length and tenancy insecurity: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for a three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The same report argues that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem, and that every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting conditions—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. That is a strong argument: a tighter cap would consume scarce regulatory attention while leaving the dangerous conditions untouched.

A defender of tighter controls might reply that Berlin's cap did lower offered rents while it lasted, and that SDU tenants paying a 15 to 25 percent per-square-foot premium deserve immediate relief [D1p1]. That is fair as far as it goes, but the relief is temporary and misdirected: it protects incumbents, shrinks the listing pool, and, on the Berlin evidence, sets up a rebound when the measure is lifted or struck down [D7p2][D7p3]. Given the thin margins owners describe [D5p1], the risk of withdrawal is real rather than theoretical.

The better path is to attack the quantity problem directly: build more public housing to shorten the queue, legislate security of tenure, and redirect enforcement toward fire, partition and ventilation standards. Those measures address what tenants actually say they need [D11p1] and avoid the documented side effects of caps [D7p1][D7p3]. Tightening rent control on SDUs would feel like action, but on this evidence it would mostly relocate the harm.
