**The Case for a Band, Not a Cap**

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs), but not with a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points to a narrow, defensible middle path: a published per-square-foot rent band on initial rents, paired with stronger tenure security, backed by a certifiable tenancy register. A hard cap would help incumbents while quietly shrinking the stock that houses the people the policy claims to protect.

The strongest argument for doing something is that the status quo protects almost no one. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent untouched [D1p3], and that is precisely where the pain sits. Mrs Wong, nine years in her unit with no written agreement, says the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. The data agree: median SDU rent per square foot rose 6.8 percent in 2019–2021, then still 3.1 percent under the ordinance, against general private rent growth of 2.4 and 1.7 percent [D2p2]. Real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. The instrument is non-binding at the point that matters, so the visible stock stability [D2p1] proves little—a point the second think-tank piece makes directly [D12p2].

But the case for a *hard cap* is weak, and the archive is unusually clear about why. The academic working paper distinguishes first-generation controls (caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases), finding consistent contraction under the former and small or null effects under the latter [D6p2]. Its elasticity estimate for marginal stock is 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Modelled for a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households a year unable to enter the segment—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those are exactly the populations D1 identifies as SDU tenants [D1p2]. Berlin shows the mechanism in the wild: listings contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. Mr Chan has already seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]. The Landlords Association promises exit below mortgage cost [D5p2]—self-interested and undocumented [D5p3], but consistent with the elasticity evidence.

A band avoids the confiscation problem and the worst of the contraction. Counsel advises that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream invites compensation claims and judicial review, while a cap framed as contractual-term regulation sits on firmer ground—and a published, indexed per-square-foot band can be defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p1][D9p2]. This is also the direction the consultation is reportedly taking [D12p1], and it mirrors New York's second-generation scheme, where stock contraction is modest precisely because first-rent regulation is light [D8p2].

The honest objection is enforcement. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, and the newest arrivals—would be missed first [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design protecting the newest-arrival tail first rather than last [D10p3]. That is a reason to build the register before the band, and to sequence enforcement toward the most exploited tail, not a reason to abandon the instrument.

Finally, rent is not the binding constraint tenants name. Mr Chan, offered a cap, names the waiting list: three years instead of five and a half and he would accept any rent [D3p3]. The NGO survey finds 61 percent would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Tenure security is cheap to enforce relative to a rent band and addresses the insecurity Mr Chan calls "the worst part" [D3p1].

The recommendation follows: tighten, but tighten the right thing. Enact a light, indexed initial-rent band [D9p2], fund the tenancy register first [D10p1], pair it with statutory tenure security [D11p3], and treat the public-housing queue as the real supply instrument [D11p2]. A hard cap would buy incumbents 800 dollars a month [D3p2] and charge it to the households still outside the door [D7p3]. Hong Kong can do better than that trade.
