# Tighten, but Not With a Hard Cap

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units — but it should tighten tenure security and regulate initial rents through an indexed band, not impose a hard cap on rent levels. The evidence in this archive points consistently in one direction: the instrument that helps incumbents most is the one most likely to harm the households still outside, and the harm falls hardest on the population the policy is meant to protect.

The case for doing nothing is weak. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched, and the result is visible in the data. Median SDU rent per square foot rose 3.1 percent in 2021–2023 under the ordinance, against general private rent growth of 1.7 percent, and real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Mrs Wong's experience matches the aggregate: her landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears," and the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. The 2021 instrument was second-generation by design, and second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock [D6p2] — but they also leave the actual pain point, the initial rent, unregulated [D9p1]. The status quo regulates the margin while the entry price runs free.

Yet the case for a hard cap fails on its own arithmetic. The reviewed literature estimates the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rents at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The working paper's Hong Kong-like model is starker still: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields about $900 a month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households a year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those are precisely the households D1 identifies as the segment's core population [D1p2]. Berlin confirms the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year, and when the law fell the suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line — the bill handed to those still outside [D7p1][D7p3]. Mr Chan, who would gain $800 a month from a cap, already fears exactly this: he has watched two units on his floor "emptied and locked for a year" [D3p2]. His fear is not hypothetical; it is the elasticity estimate wearing a face.

The counter-argument — that the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance proves landlords will not exit [D2p1] — misreads the evidence. That stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument [D12p2]. The Landlords Association's exit threat [D5p2] should be discounted, but not dismissed: it concedes it has no data on members' actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures rest on a 210-member survey with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported [D5p3]. The threat is unproven, but so is the claim that landlords are bluffing. Mrs Wong's building, where four landlords treat units as their pension, suggests some owners would stay [D4p2]; the stairwell flats converted to storage suggest others would not [D4p3].

A band-based cap on initial rents, indexed and reviewable, threads this needle. Counsel judges it defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation, avoiding the compensation and judicial-review exposure a hard cap would invite [D9p1][D9p2]. It binds where the pain is — the entry price — while leaving enough return to blunt exit. It must be paired with the measure tenants actually name. Mr Chan does not ask for rent relief; he asks for the queue, saying he would "accept any rent to leave" if waiting time fell to three years [D3p3]. The NGO survey confirms this revealed preference: 61 percent would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security, 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Tenure security is cheap to deliver and attacks the insecurity Mr Chan calls "the worst part" [D3p1].

The honest caveat is enforcement. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock a year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain — those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], and the newest arrivals — are exactly those enforcement would miss, so the most exploited tail is protected last [D10p2]. Any tightening must therefore be sequenced: build the register and inspection capacity first, then bind the band. A cap that cannot be enforced is a press release, not a policy.

Tighten — but tighten tenure and entry pricing, not the level. The archive's lesson is consistent: protect the incumbent cheaply, or the outsider pays for it.
