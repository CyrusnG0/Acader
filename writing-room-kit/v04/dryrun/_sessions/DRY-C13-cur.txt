# Tighten, but Not With a Hard Cap: A Targeted Case for Tenure Security and Indexed Rent Bands

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs), but it should reject the instrument most often demanded—a hard cap on initial rents—in favour of a package that addresses the actual binding constraint named by tenants: insecurity and the public-housing queue. The archive points consistently in one direction: a hard cap would help some incumbents while shrinking entry for the households the policy is meant to protect, whereas a lighter, better-enforced instrument can deliver real relief without triggering the contraction dynamics the literature predicts.

The case for doing *something* is strong. SDU tenants already pay a per-square-foot premium for the most marginal space in the market: median SDU rent sits roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats in absolute terms, but on a per-square-foot basis tenants pay 15–25 percent *more*, because carved space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs from key provisions [D1p3]. That gap is the actual pain point: Mrs Wong reports her landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears," and she has never received a written agreement [D4p1]. A regime that regulates increases but not entry prices regulates the wrong margin—and the burden has kept climbing regardless. The index records real rent burden (rent to income) for SDU households worsening from 31 to 34 percent across the period, even with the ordinance in force [D2p2].

Yet the *form* of tightening matters enormously. The academic working paper separates first-generation controls—hard caps on rent *levels*—from second-generation controls—caps on increases *within* tenancies—and finds that first-generation controls show consistent supply contraction while second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. That distinction explains the 2021 record. Estimated SDU stock rose steadily from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 by 2025, continuing through the ordinance [D2p1]; but because the 2021 instrument was second-generation and never bound initial rents, that stability reflects a non-binding instrument, not proof that a binding cap is safe—precisely the point the think-tank critique makes when it calls the visible stability an artifact [D12p2]. The working paper's modelled tightening scenario shows the stakes: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about $900 per month to incumbent tenants, but leaves 3,000–4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]—the very population D1 identifies as the core SDU constituency [D1p2]. Berlin reinforces the pattern: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, and "the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3].

The landlord association's threat of exit [D5p2] should be discounted—its margin data is self-reported from a 34 percent response rate [D5p3], and Mrs Wong's observation that units are landlords' pensions cuts against mass exit [D4p2]. But Mr Chan's counter-evidence—two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]—shows exit is real at the margin, and the elasticity estimate of 0.31–0.48 implies a 10 percent cap removes 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That is a cost worth avoiding if a cheaper instrument exists.

It does. The legal opinion identifies a defensible middle path: cap initial rent by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band, framed as rate-capping rather than confiscation, avoiding the compensation and judicial-review risk of a hard cap [D9p2]. The NGO survey supports precisely this: 61 percent of tenants would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]; the report endorses a narrow tightening of tenure security plus a light band, opposing a hard cap [D11p3]. Mr Chan himself names the queue, not rent, as what would help most [D3p3]. The consultation's expected "floor-standard + rent band" package [D12p1] is therefore the right direction.

The decisive caveat is enforcement. Current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, the newest arrivals—would be missed first [D10p2], and the subcommittee could not identify any design protecting that tail first rather than last [D10p3]. Any tightening must therefore be sequenced: build the register and inspection capacity first, pair the band with mandatory written tenancies and tenure security, and spend enforcement resources on conditions—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is immediate [D11p2].

**Conclusion.** Tighten, yes—but not with a hard cap. A hard cap on initial rents would deliver visible relief to incumbents while contracting entry for the very households the policy targets, as the elasticity literature [D6p1–D6p4] and Berlin's experience [D7p3] both warn. An indexed rent band plus tenure security, backed by a real register and inspection regime, captures most of the benefit at a fraction of the supply risk, and addresses what tenants actually name as binding [D11p1][D3p3]. The queue remains the root cause; rent control can only treat the symptom, and it should do so with the lightest instrument that works.
