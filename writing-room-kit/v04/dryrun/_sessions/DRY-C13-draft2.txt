# Tighten, but Not With a Hard Cap: A Targeted Case for Tenure Security and Indexed Rent Bands

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs), but it should reject the instrument most often demanded—a hard cap on initial rents—in favour of a package that addresses the actual binding constraint named by tenants: insecurity and the public-housing queue. The evidence in this archive points consistently in one direction: a hard cap would help some incumbents while shrinking entry for the households the policy is meant to protect, whereas a lighter, better-enforced instrument can deliver real relief without triggering the contraction dynamics that the literature predicts.

The case for doing *something* is strong. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium for the most marginal space in the market [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs from key provisions [D1p3]. That gap is the actual pain point: Mrs Wong reports her landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears," and she has never received a written agreement [D4p1]. A regime that regulates increases but not entry prices regulates the wrong margin.

Yet the archive is equally clear that the *form* of tightening matters enormously. The academic working paper distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies): the former show consistent supply contraction, the latter small or null effects [D6p2]. The stable SDU stock through 2021—from 100,800 to 111,200 units by 2025 [D2p1]—is therefore not evidence that a binding cap is safe; it is evidence that a *non-binding* instrument was in place, exactly as the working paper's critics of the "capacity exists" argument note [D12p2]. The modelled tightening scenario is sobering: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about $900 per month to incumbents but leaves 3,000–4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. That is the population D1 identifies as the core SDU constituency [D1p2]. Berlin reinforces the point: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, and "the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3].

The landlord association's threat of exit [D5p2] should be discounted—its margin data is self-reported from a 34 percent response rate [D5p3], and Mrs Wong's observation that units are landlords' pensions cuts against mass exit [D4p2]. But Mr Chan's counter-evidence—two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]—shows exit is real at the margin, and the elasticity estimate of 0.31–0.48 implies a 10 percent cap removes 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That is a cost worth avoiding if a cheaper instrument exists.

It does. The legal opinion identifies a defensible middle path: cap initial rent by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band, framed as rate-capping rather than confiscation, avoiding the compensation and judicial-review risk of a hard cap [D9p2]. The NGO survey supports precisely this: 61 percent of tenants would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]; the report endorses a narrow tightening of tenure security plus a light band, opposing a hard cap [D11p3]. Mr Chan himself names the queue, not rent, as what would help most [D3p3]. The consultation's expected "floor-standard + rent band" package [D12p1] is therefore the right direction.

The decisive caveat is enforcement. Current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, the newest arrivals—would be missed first [D10p2], and the subcommittee could not identify any design protecting that tail first rather than last [D10p3]. Any tightening must therefore be sequenced: build the register and inspection capacity first, pair the band with mandatory written tenancies and tenure security, and spend enforcement resources on conditions—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is immediate [D11p2].
