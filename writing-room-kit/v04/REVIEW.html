Process-Evidence Room

A study workspace where AI helps students think but never writes for them, and a short report that shows a teacher where submitted work has no earlier trace. This page explains the idea, the test we ran on 30 simulated students, and what the numbers do and do not show.

In one minute

What we built. A governed AI study assistant (the Room) and a coherence report. The assistant answers questions about sources and structure but never hands over an argument or essay text. When a student asks it to write, it turns the request into a question and offers options instead. The report compares the final essay with everything the student did in the Room and lists the places where the essay has no earlier trace, each phrased as a question for a person to ask.

What we tested. 30 simulated students in five behaviours: honest, lazy but honest, everything outsourced to an outside AI, partly outsourced, and partly outsourced while narrating the borrowed parts into the Room. Students knew nothing about a fictional topic, so they could only learn through the Room.

What happened. The report ranked honest work above every other behaviour, with no overlap between honest work and the lazy, outsourced and partly-outsourced runs. It was weaker against students who narrated the borrowed parts (AUC 0.89, wide interval). It proves nothing about understanding. It makes a short live conversation sharper, and that conversation stays the real gate.

Honest vs outsourced AUC 1.00 Honest vs partly outsourced AUC 1.00 (earlier version: 0.33) Honest vs narrated AUC 0.89 (95% CI 0.58 to 1.00) Assistant gave essay-ready argument in 1.4% of replies

AUC = the chance that a randomly chosen honest run scores higher than a randomly chosen run from the other behaviour. 0.50 is a coin flip, 1.00 is perfect separation. With six runs per behaviour, 1.00 means "no overlap in these runs", not a measured accuracy. Everything here is simulated; no real student took part.

The problem and the idea

Any text a student submits can now be written by an AI. Tools that try to spot AI text, or that record typing, lose that arms race: an outside AI can write the essay, and a student can retype it. We take a different route. Instead of judging the final text, we make the working process itself the evidence, and we check whether the final work fits that process.

The five-line design

1

The Room is the best place to do the work, not a requirement. Research outside is free. A student who skips the Room simply leaves less evidence.

2

Every tool runs under a policy. We ship defaults; the teacher can tune them (how many clarifying questions, which tools are on).

3

Two ways to work. With the assistant (every step is discussed first), or in confident mode: the student states their claim and argument themselves, nobody judges it, and a Coach asks one question to check it holds together.

4

We aid thinking and never give the direct answer. Where an outside AI gives one answer, the Room gives options with descriptions (sources, structure tracks, opposing views). The choice and the reason the student gives for it are the evidence.

5

Where the work doesn't fit the process, the platform asks. A mismatch becomes a question ("Why did you move from A to B?"), never a verdict. A human decides what, if anything, follows.

The agreed limit. Anything typed into the Room can also be typed by an AI that has the same context, including progress reports and answers to the Room's questions. So the report shows work that skipped the Room. It cannot show work that was faked through the Room. Only a live moment, a short conversation with the student, can do that. That step belongs to the school, and the report's job is to make it sharp: it hands the teacher the questions to ask.

The role is the same as Turnitin or VeriGuide: we produce a report next to the submission. We verify and ask. We do not judge the work or the student.

How the Room behaves

The Room is an AI assistant wrapped in policies, small modules that decide what the assistant may do with each kind of request. Two rules are enforced in code, not only in the prompt.

Guided source requests

A student asks for a source. The assistant asks what the source must show and for which claim, repeating until it has its own view of what would help (default cap: 3 questions). It then serves a shuffled shortlist of 3 to 5 documents with one-line descriptions, with no ranking. The student picks and gives a reason. Picking and the reason are logged.

In code: while clarifying, the assistant's prompt contains no document text, titles or IDs, so it cannot leak a source by accident. Our tests assert this.

"Write it for me" is never served

It becomes a question: "What's your idea for this paragraph? One line is enough." Once the student gives an idea, the assistant offers 2 or 3 shapes (structure only, no facts or prose). Each request is counted.

In code: a global rule forbids more than two consecutive sentences that could be pasted into the essay as the student's argument.

Opposing claims and reading help

The assistant raises the strongest opposing position, attributed to a named source, and asks "How do you answer this?". It never says the student is wrong. Reading help maps a document and explains terms but does not summarise its argument.

Confident mode

The student states a claim and argument unaided. The Coach replies with exactly one question about whether it holds together with what the student said earlier and the sources unlocked. No help, no judgement.

The actual rules the assistant runs under (prompt text)

Global rules, applied to every reply

You are the study assistant inside a course writing room. The student is working on a graded essay.
Your job is to help the student think, never to think for them.
Rules that always apply:
- Never write the student's essay content. Never produce more than 2 consecutive sentences that could be pasted into the essay as the student's argument.
- Never invent an argument for the student. You may point to positions that a named source takes, or group things the student has already said, quoting them.
- Never rank or say which choice is best. Offer options and ask the student to choose and say why.
- Never tell the student they are wrong. Ask a question instead.
- Keep replies short: 2 to 5 sentences. Plain language. No lists longer than 5 items.

When the student asks it to write

The student asked you to write something for them. You never do that.
If the student has not yet given an idea for this paragraph: say you won't write it, and ask what their idea is. One line is enough.
If the student has given an idea earlier: offer 2 or 3 SHAPES the paragraph could take (for example claim, evidence, reply; or problem, cause, response). One short line each: the name of the shape and what it is good for. Mention no facts, documents, numbers or positions at all, and put no words in the student's mouth. You may point at the student's own earlier words by quoting them.
End by asking which shape they want to try and what their first line would be. Plain text, no markdown, at most 5 sentences.

While clarifying a source request

The student has asked for a source. You do not have any documents in front of you and you must not name or guess any.
Ask ONE short question that helps the student say what a source here must do for their essay: what it should show (evidence, a counter-view, a definition, a case, a first-hand account) and for which claim they are making.
If they have already said some of this, build on their words and ask about what is still unclear.
Do not suggest titles, authors, document types or facts. Ask one question only.

Policies in this test: source requests, structure tracks, argument options (limited to attributed source positions or the student's own earlier words), write requests, opposing claims, reading help, summaries, citation formatting, claim check-ins, and confident mode.

The coherence report

When the work is submitted, the report asks one question of every claim, source and change: is there an earlier moment in the Room where the student stated it, chose it, or defended it in their own words?

ComponentWhat it measuresWeight
Claim lineageShare of the essay's claims (typically 8 to 20) that a student's own earlier turn states, chooses or defends. Agreeing with the assistant counts only if the student restates the point. Judged by an AI that is never told which behaviour the run came from.0.35
Source lineageShare of cited documents the student chose in the Room, with a reason of at least three words.0.20
Citation checkDoes the cited passage actually support the sentence that cites it? Checked against a hand-readable answer key of the facts each passage holds.0.15
Change lineageParagraphs that are new or rewritten between saved drafts: was the change discussed in the Room between those saves?0.15
StructurePasted-versus-typed pattern. Set by the arm's rule in this test, so it is never used as evidence of anything beyond a sanity check.0.15

The output is not a score to act on. It is a list of questions for the teacher, for example: "Paragraph 3 says X. This point doesn't appear earlier in your work in the Room. Where did it come from?" The report uses no verdict words.

The experiment

We needed a test without real students. So we simulated them, with a labelled answer: we know which behaviour produced each run, and we ask whether the report can tell them apart.

The world

A fictional course archive of 12 documents (policy brief, rent data, tenant interviews, a landlords' submission, a working paper, comparative cases, a legal opinion, an internal minute, an NGO report, news), about Hong Kong subdivided-unit rent control. Being fictional, no AI knows it in advance.

Essay question: Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? Take a position and defend it with evidence from the course archive.

Who plays whom

Student: a fresh Claude Sonnet worker per run that knows nothing about the topic and sees only the question and its own instructions.
Assistant, outside AI and judge: DeepSeek deepseek-chat. The outside AI sees the whole archive but never the Room. The judge never sees which behaviour or run id it is reading.

ArmWhat the simulated student does
C1 Honest15 to 30 turns. Asks for sources, picks with real reasons, reads, states claims in its own words, answers opposing claims, saves drafts, then writes the essay itself.
C2 Lazy honest4 to 8 turns, vague requests, 3 to 6 word reasons, one or two drafts. Still writes its own (shorter) essay.
C3 All outside2 or 3 turns for show. An outside AI writes the whole essay, submitted unchanged.
C4 Partly outsideA lighter honest process and its own draft, then replaces about half the paragraphs (at least two) with an outside AI's, retyped, and never discusses them in the Room.
C5 Partly outside, narratedAs C4, but before submitting it explains each borrowed paragraph's claim to the assistant, requests and picks the sources it cites, and answers the opposing claims.

Six runs per arm, 30 in total. Arm instructions never mention scoring, lineage or what is measured.

What the numbers say

  • Separation comes from the Room's trail, not from typing. The partly outside arm is retyped, so the paste pattern gives nothing. It was separated because its borrowed paragraphs had no earlier trace: claim lineage 42 against 91 for honest work, and the sources it cites were never chosen in the Room (46 against 100).
  • Correct citations prove nothing. The outside AI cites real passages accurately (citation check 87 for all-outside against 97 for honest). Source and claim lineage carry the result.
  • The narrated arm lands in between (82), and we predicted otherwise. Narrating helped most through source lineage (100) and change lineage (96). Claim lineage stayed lower (66). These students were ordinary workers told to narrate: several paraphrased loosely, left the assistant's last question unanswered, or submitted essays whose opening and closing disagreed. A dedicated narrator, an AI holding the outside essay and answering for the student, was not tested. That is the attack the agreed limit already names.
  • Lazy honest and partly outside tie. They score the same but would draw different questions: the lazy student's essay claims were never stated in the Room (claim 31, change 21), while the partly outside student's borrowed paragraphs and their sources never passed through it.
  • Honest students still get questions (5 on average). Most are about figures they read in a passage and quoted without restating. A design choice is open on whether reading a passage should count as the origin of a claim that quotes it.

What the report can and cannot claim

Can claim

  • Where the final work has no earlier trace in the Room, as questions for a human to ask.
  • In this simulation, it ranked honest, lazy honest, partly outside and fully outside work in that order, with no overlap between honest work and the lazy, outsourced and partly-outsourced runs.
  • The assistant guided without handing over arguments or leaking sources (1.4% flagged, 0 leaks).

Cannot claim

  • That a student understood anything.
  • That text typed into the Room was the student's own thinking. An AI with the same context can type it.
  • That an honest student will not be asked questions.
  • Anything about real students, anxious students, or writers in a second language. That risk is untested and is the main fairness question for a pilot.
  • Accuracy or readiness for use.

Caveats

  • Simulated students, six per arm. The narrated arm's interval spans 0.58 to 1.00.
  • One vendor plays three roles (assistant, outside AI, judge: DeepSeek). The same team designed the test and the product.
  • The partly-outside arms differ from honest work partly by construction: borrowed paragraphs cite sources the student never requested. A student who requests those sources first is the narrated arm.
  • Worker deviations, all kept in: essay lengths outside the brief in four runs, one honest run with 14 Room turns instead of 15, and two runs that were finalised more than once (the last file is scored). No run was excluded; no honest run cited a passage it never read. We did not review worker transcripts for forbidden-file reads, and no worker reported any.
  • Two analysis changes after the runs, both disclosed: the source-leak check no longer counts a document id the student had just typed themselves (raw count 9, all such echoes; refined count 0); and the five flagged assistant replies are all attributed opposing-claim answers, which are by design, so the raw rate is reported.

Tuning we made before the runs (all logged in the pre-registration)

  • A shortlist is served once 2 of the top 3 candidate sources repeat between turns (instead of all 3). With a language-model picker, strict repeats meant every request ran to the cap.
  • Source descriptions state the kind of material and who is speaking, never a finding or number.
  • Shape options for a write request mention no facts, documents or positions.
  • Opposing claims state the position only, with no figures, so the student must request and read the source.

Open items

  1. Hand audit. The founder grades six blind reports (each claim: was the earlier trace real?). This checks the judge itself.
  2. Design choice. Should reading a passage count as the origin of a claim that quotes it? It would lower the number of questions honest students receive.
  3. The untested attack. A dedicated narrator arm (an AI holding the outside essay and answering the Room's questions).
  4. Real students. A pilot, with accessibility alternatives to typing and attention to students who work slowly or in a second language.
  5. Open design questions in the design document: professor visibility, practice mode, and the final live exchange.

Glossary

Room
The workspace where students do graded work with the governed assistant.
Policy
A small module that sets what the assistant may do for one kind of request (find sources, write, argue, structure, and so on).
Coach
The voice that only asks questions, as opposed to the Helper, which answers. In confident mode the Coach checks that the student's own claim holds together.
Claim lineage
The link from a claim in the final work back to an earlier moment where the student stated or chose it.
Arm
One of the five simulated student behaviours tested.
AUC
Chance that a random honest run outscores a random run of another behaviour; 0.5 = chance, 1.0 = perfect separation.
Pre-registration
Writing the test, thresholds and predictions down before running, so results can't be read after the fact to fit.
Viva
A short oral conversation about one's own work; the live gate the report is meant to aim.
