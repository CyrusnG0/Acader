**The Case for a Narrow Tightening, Not a Hard Cap**

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs), but only through a narrow instrument: a published per-square-foot band on initial rents combined with stronger tenure security. A hard cap on initial rent levels would be economically risky and legally fragile, and the archive suggests it would hurt the very households it aims to protect.

The case for doing nothing is weak. SDU tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium per square foot precisely because they are desperate [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs [D1p3]. Mrs Wong's experience shows the consequence: without a written agreement, the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. The status quo regulates the wrong margin.

Yet a hard cap on initial rents is the wrong correction. The academic evidence distinguishes first-generation controls, which show consistent stock contraction, from second-generation controls, which show small or null effects [D6p2]. The stable SDU stock from 2019 to 2025 [D2p1] reflects a non-binding instrument, not proof that binding controls are safe [D12p2]. The estimated elasticity of 0.31 to 0.48 implies a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1], and a modelled 15 percent cut would transfer roughly $900 monthly to incumbents while locking out 3,000 to 4,000 households annually, mostly new arrivals [D6p4]. Berlin confirms the pattern: supply contracted within a year, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. Mr Chan's fear that landlords would rather lock units than accept regulated tenants [D3p2] is not irrational; the Landlords Association explicitly commits to exit below mortgage cost [D5p2], though its margin data is self-reported and thin [D5p3].

The legal route matters too. A hard cap framed as depriving rental income would invite compensation claims and judicial review lasting beyond a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. A band indexed and reviewable can be defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2].

Most importantly, rent is not the binding constraint tenants name. Mr Chan would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey finds 61 percent would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2].

A band-based tightening therefore offers the best trade-off: it addresses the initial-rent pain point the 2021 ordinance ignored, while avoiding the contraction arithmetic of a hard cap. But two caveats must shape the design. First, enforcement capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a certifiable register needs 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. Second, the tenants least able to complain—those without written agreements or newest arrivals—would be missed first [D10p2]; the subcommittee could not identify any design protecting that tail first [D10p3]. Any tightening must therefore be sequenced: build the register, pair the band with mandatory written tenancies, and redirect enforcement savings toward fire and ventilation inspections [D11p2].

The honest conclusion is that rent control is a second-best instrument for a quantity problem. Tighten narrowly, enforce visibly, and treat the public-housing queue as the real policy lever.
