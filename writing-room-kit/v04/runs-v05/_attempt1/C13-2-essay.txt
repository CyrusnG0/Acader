# The Case Against a Hard Cap on SDU Rents

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units through a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points toward a narrower package—tenure security plus a light, band-based limit on initial rents—rather than the confiscatory instrument that dominates public debate. A hard cap would likely shrink the very stock it aims to protect, would be difficult to enforce against the tenants who most need protection, and would misdiagnose a quantity problem as a price problem.

The strongest argument for tightening is the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance. Estimated SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, continuing through the ordinance [D2p1], which suggests moderate regulation did not trigger visible exit. But this stability is precisely what the academic literature predicts for a second-generation instrument. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched [D1p3], and the working paper finds second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock, while first-generation controls—hard caps on levels—show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The visible stability is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and a binding one would behave differently [D12p2].

The contraction arithmetic is uncomfortable. The elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. In a modelled tightened-cap scenario, a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those losers are the same population the cap intends to protect [D6p3]. The Berlin case illustrates the mechanism: capped initial-offer rents fell, classified-listing supply contracted within a year as owners rerouted flats to non-controlled segments, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Enforcement is the second problem. Current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. More damningly, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement—like Mrs Wong, who has never received one and says the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]—or the newest arrivals. Enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail, and the subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p2][D10p3].

The landlord threat deserves scrutiny but cannot be dismissed. The Association represents roughly 18,000 units and commits that members will exit if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1][D5p2]. Its evidence is weak—no data on actual margins, and compliance costs from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Yet Mrs Wong's own building shows the contradiction: she reports two flats converted to non-residential storage after the ordinance, while still believing landlords cannot exit [D4p3]. Mr Chan has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2].

Most importantly, the cap addresses the wrong constraint. In the NGO survey of 440 households, 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan does not name rent when asked what would help most; he names the waiting list [D3p3]. The segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2]. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire, partition and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

A hard cap also carries legal risk: framed as deprivation of rental income, it would attract compensation claims and judicial review, with litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. The middle instrument—a published, indexed per-square-foot band—sits on firmer ground but requires survey infrastructure that does not exist [D9p2].

The proportionate answer is the one the NGO report and the consultation signals converge on: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents [D11p3][D12p1]. This protects incumbents like Mr Chan from two-week notice [D3p1] without triggering the contraction that a hard cap would. Tightening beyond that band would trade certain harm to outsiders for uncertain relief to insiders.
