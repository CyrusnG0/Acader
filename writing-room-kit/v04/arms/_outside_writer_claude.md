Subdivided Housing in Hong Kong: A Primer (Policy brief)
[D1p1] About 110,000 households in Hong Kong live in subdivided units (SDUs): flats carved into small cubicles, often with shared kitchens and bathrooms. Median SDU rent is roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats, but on a per-square-foot basis SDU tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent, because space is carved and priced at its most desperate margin.
[D1p2] SDU tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households and residents waiting for public housing, where the average waiting time has hovered around five and a half years. For these households the SDU is not a choice but a residual option while the queue moves.
[D1p3] Policy debate has long centred on whether to regulate rents in this segment. Hong Kong already operates one limited instrument: the Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped rent increases within an existing tenancy but did not cap the initial rent level, and excluded SDUs with co-living services from key provisions.
[D1p4] Any tightening therefore raises the classic question of rent economics: does capping the initial rent help tenants, or does it shrink the very stock they depend on? The answer depends on supply elasticity, enforcement capacity and what happens to households if units exit the market.

SDU Rent and Stock Index, 2019–2025 (excerpt) (Data excerpt)
[D2p1] Estimated SDU stock: 2019 — 100,800 units. 2021 — 107,300. 2023 — 109,800. 2025 — 111,200. Growth continued through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, suggesting that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit.
[D2p2] Median SDU rent per square foot rose 6.8 percent in 2019-2021, then 3.1 percent in 2021-2023 under the ordinance, against general private rent growth of 2.4 and 1.7 percent in the same windows. Real rent burden (rent to income) for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent.
[D2p3] The index notes a data caveat: post-2023 figures rely on survey extrapolation because reporting of new tenancy terms is voluntary below the ordinance threshold.

Tenant Interview: Mr Chan, Kwai Chung (Interview)
[D3p1] Mr Chan, 41, delivery driver, lives with his wife and two children in a 120 sq ft cubicle. He moved in after a factory injury. Rent is 5,800 dollars, about a third of household income. He says the worst part is not price but insecurity: the landlord ended his previous tenancy with two weeks notice when he asked to repair a leaking window.
[D3p2] On the proposed tightening he is split. A hard cap would free maybe 800 dollars a month, which is food for a month. But he fears landlords would rather convert cubicles back to storage rooms than deal with regulated tenants. He has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year.
[D3p3] Asked what would help most, he does not name rent. He names the waiting list. If the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave.

Tenant Interview: Mrs Wong, Sham Shui Po (Interview)
[D4p1] Mrs Wong, 67, retired, has lived in an SDU for nine years and pays 4,200 dollars. Her tenancy predates the 2021 ordinance. She has never received a written tenancy agreement, and says the ordinance changed nothing she can see: the landlord still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears.
[D4p2] She supports a hard cap on initial rents. Her building has four SDU landlords, she says, none of whom would leave the business: where else would the money come from? Units here are their pension.
[D4p3] Interviewers note a contradiction she does not see: she reports two flats in her stairwell converted to non-residential storage after the ordinance, and still believes landlords cannot exit.

Submission: Landlords Association of Hong Kong (Stakeholder submission)
[D5p1] The Association represents owners of roughly 18,000 SDU units. It argues a hard initial-rent cap is confiscatory: many owners hold thin margins after mortgage payments, rates and the 2021 compliance costs of windows, wiring and fire doors.
[D5p2] It commits that members will exit the segment if returns fall below mortgage cost: every unit converted back, locker rooms or simply emptied, removes housing that, however poor, houses someone tonight.
[D5p3] The Association concedes it has no data on members actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures cite a 2022 survey of 210 members with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported.

Working Paper: Rent Control and Supply Elasticity in Marginal Housing (Academic working paper)
[D6p1] Standard theory predicts supply contraction under binding rent ceilings, but the elasticity is an empirical question. In marginal stock — where units are already at the bottom of the market — the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48 in the reviewed literature: meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years.
[D6p2] Crucially, the paper distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies). First-generation controls show consistent contraction effects; second-generation show small or null effects on stock — the 2021 Hong Kong ordinance is second-generation, which explains the stable stock in the D2 index.
[D6p3] The welfare arithmetic is uncomfortable: contraction removes units at the bottom, and the households that lose units are the same population the cap intends to protect. Whether tenants gain depends on how many stay long enough in regulated tenancies to offset entrants who can no longer find units.
[D6p4] The paper models a tightened cap scenario for a Hong Kong-like market: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households.

Comparative Note: Berlin, Mietendeckel 2020–2021 (Comparative case)
[D7p1] Berlin capped rents on ~1.5 million flats at 2020. Reported initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply; classified-listing supply then contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments.
[D7p2] The constitutional court struck the law down in 2021 on federal-competence grounds, not economic ones. When it fell, a backlog of suppressed increases released into the market, and rents for new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend line.
[D7p3] Berlin lesson for the brief: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents; the tenants the cap protects are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside.

Comparative Note: New York City Rent Stabilisation (Comparative case)
[D8p1] New York has regulated rents since 1947, currently covering roughly 44 percent of rental stock, a second-generation scheme: increases are capped within tenancies, initial rents and vacancy are lightly regulated, and vacancy decontrol ended in 2019.
[D8p2] Studies find stabilised units show lower mobility: tenants stay longer, and measured rent gaps to unregulated units are wide. Stock contraction is modest because the scheme is light at first-rent — the exact point Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance also left untouched.
[D8p3] The cost surfaces elsewhere: maintenance incentives fall with regulated returns, and the 2019 tightening is associated with a measurable fall in available stabilised listings as owners shift units to other uses.

Legal Opinion: Compensation and Judicial Review Risk (Legal opinion)
[D9p1] A hard cap on initial SDU rents would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's protection of property rights if framed as a deprivation of rental income stream; a cap framed as contractual-term regulation, like the 2021 ordinance, sits on firmer ground but leaves the initial rent — the actual pain point — unregulated.
[D9p2] Counsel assesses a middle instrument: cap initial rent by reference to a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, which regulators can defend as rate-capping rather than confiscation. Bands would need a survey infrastructure that does not currently exist.
[D9p3] Without compensation, the government should expect judicial review from institutional owners; the Association's submission (D5) previews the argument. Litigation timeline alone would outlast a Council term.

Internal Minute: Housing Authority Enforcement Subcommittee (draft, not for circulation) (Internal minute)
[D10p1] Members were briefed that a tightened cap is only as real as its inspection regime. Current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year; a band-based initial-rent cap requires a certifiable tenancy register, which the Rating and Valuation Department estimates at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up.
[D10p2] The subcommittee's own modelling note records a risk the public consultation omits: if landlords reprice above-band initial rents, the first instrument tenants hold is complaint — but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement (see D4) or the newest arrivals, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail.
[D10p3] A member noted, and the chair agreed to strike from the public version, that the subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail — the population named in D1 — is protected first rather than last.

NGO Report: Rehousing Pathways First (NGO report)
[D11p1] The report surveys 440 SDU households and finds the binding constraint named by tenants is not rent level but queue length and tenancy insecurity: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for a three-year security of tenure, 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer.
[D11p2] It argues rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem: the segment is tight because the queue is long. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent on inspection of conditions (fire, partition, ventilation), where harm is documented and immediate.
[D11p3] The report supports a narrow tightening — tenure security plus a light band on initial rents — and opposes a hard cap, on the D6-type contraction arithmetic and on its own survey's revealed preference.
