Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but narrowly. The first step should be stronger security of tenure, with a light, data-based band on initial rents only later. A hard cap on initial rents would be a mistake.

Subdivided units (SDUs) are flats carved into small cubicles. About 110,000 households live in them, and on a per-square-foot basis they pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent over mainstream flats [D1p1]. These tenants are mostly new arrivals, single parents and people waiting for public housing, so the unit is a leftover option, not a choice [D1p2]. Doing nothing is not acceptable. Rent per square foot kept rising, and the share of income spent on rent went from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2].

Money is not the only problem. Mr Chan, a delivery driver, pays 5,800 dollars for a 120 square foot cubicle. He says the worst part is insecurity, because his old landlord ended his tenancy with two weeks notice after he asked for a leaking window to be fixed [D3p1]. A survey of 440 households points the same way: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of secure tenure [D11p1]. Mrs Wong has lived in her unit for nine years and never got a written agreement, and she says the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. So the biggest gap is that tenants lack written, secure tenancies.

The strongest objection is that landlords will leave if rules get tougher. The working paper I read supports this for hard caps. It estimates that a 10 percent cut in rent removes about 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. For a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut in initial rents gives current tenants about 900 dollars a month, but 3,000 to 4,000 households a year cannot get into the segment [D6p4]. That means the people the cap is meant to help can lose the most. The tenants' own stories show the risk. Mr Chan has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked [D3p2], and Mrs Wong saw two flats in her stairwell turned into storage, even though she thinks landlords cannot leave [D4p3]. A hard cap also faces legal trouble, since counsel expects compensation claims under the Basic Law and judicial review [D9p1, D9p3].

My reply is that the rules I propose are of the other kind. The same working paper separates hard caps on rent levels from caps on increases within a tenancy, and finds small or no effects on stock from the second type [D6p2]. The data fit this. Estimated SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, including after the 2021 ordinance [D2p1]. I should be careful here, because the index warns that figures after 2023 rely on survey extrapolation [D2p3]. So I trust the evidence up to 2023 more, and I only say that moderate rules have not caused a visible exit so far.

What would narrow tightening look like? First, a written tenancy for every SDU with three years of tenure security, plus protection against being removed for asking for repairs. This answers what Mr Chan and the survey say matters most [D3p1, D11p1]. Second, a rent band could come later. Counsel says a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, is easier to defend than a hard cap, but it needs survey infrastructure that does not exist yet [D9p2]. The government should build that data first.

Rent control also cannot fix the whole problem. Mr Chan says that if the queue for public housing fell from five and a half years to three, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p3]. The report I read says the segment is tight because the queue is long, and that enforcement effort is better spent on inspecting fire and ventilation risks [D11p2]. Tightening should therefore go together with faster public housing.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten, but in the second-generation way: security of tenure now, a light rent band when the data exists, and no hard cap on initial rents. This protects tenants without shrinking the stock they depend on.
