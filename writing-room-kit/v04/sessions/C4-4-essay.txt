Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in a limited way: it should cap rent increases inside a tenancy and protect tenants from sudden eviction, and it should not impose a hard cap on starting rents. This keeps tenants safer without pushing landlords to take units off the market.

The case for acting starts with how tenants are doing. The archive's rent index says real rent burden for subdivided-unit households rose from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Mr Chan, a delivery driver, pays 5,800 dollars for a 120 square foot cubicle, about a third of his household income [D3p1]. His worst problem is insecurity, since his last landlord ended his tenancy with two weeks notice after he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. Rent control that limits increases and gives tenants more time would answer exactly this problem.

Second, the supply evidence cuts against a binding cap. The stable SDU stock through the 2021 ordinance, 100,800 units in 2019 rising to 111,200 by 2025 [D2p1], is real, but the working paper explains why it is not evidence that a harder instrument is safe. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation: it caps increases within tenancies but leaves initial rents unregulated [D1p3][D6p2]. Second-generation controls show small or null stock effects; first-generation controls on levels show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The reviewed elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The paper's Hong Kong-like model is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while pricing 3,000 to 4,000 households per year out of the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin confirms the mechanism, since caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

I also trust the landlord evidence less than the other sources. The Association admits it has no data on its members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a 2022 survey of 210 members with a 34 percent response rate, all self-reported [D5p3]. That does not make them wrong, but it means the warning of mass exit is a claim and not a measured result. There is a limit on my own evidence too: the index says its post-2023 figures rely on survey extrapolation because reporting is voluntary [D2p3]. So I rely on the 2019 to 2023 part of the series, which comes before that problem.

Third, a hard cap cannot be enforced where it is needed most. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. More damning, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU with no tenancy document [D4p1], and the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2], and the subcommittee could not identify any design under which that tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. Mrs Wong's confidence that landlords cannot exit is contradicted by her own report of two flats converted to storage [D4p3], and the Association's exit threat, however thin its evidence [D5p2][D5p3], is credible at the margin where returns approach mortgage cost.

The strongest argument against a hard cap is that it targets price while the real constraint is quantity. The segment is tight because the public-housing queue is long, averaging around five and a half years, and the SDU is a residual option while the queue moves [D1p2]. Mr Chan makes this explicit: asked what would help most, he does not name rent but the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households corroborates this at scale: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. A policy that suppresses price while leaving the queue and tenure insecurity untouched treats the symptom, not the disease.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units by extending the kind of control already used in 2021: limits on increases inside a tenancy plus more notice. The evidence says this kind of control does not shrink supply, while tenants still carry a heavy and growing rent burden. A hard cap on starting rents is the version that risks the exits landlords and Mr Chan fear, so Hong Kong should avoid it.
