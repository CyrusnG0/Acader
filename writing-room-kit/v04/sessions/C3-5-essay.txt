# Tighten, but Not Where It Hurts: A Case for Tenure Security and a Light Band

Hong Kong should tighten regulation of subdivided units (SDUs)—but not with the hard cap on initial rents that dominates public debate. The archive points toward a narrower package: enforceable tenure security plus an indexed per-square-foot rent band. A hard cap would likely protect incumbents while shrinking entry for the very households the policy intends to help.

The case for doing something is strong. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats, and the segment houses roughly 110,000 households who are disproportionately new arrivals, single parents, and public-housing waiters facing a queue of about five and a half years [D1p1][D1p2]. Real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU with no written agreement, reports that the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. Mr Chan's worst problem is not price but two weeks' notice after requesting a window repair [D3p1]. Insecurity, not rent level, is the documented harm.

Yet the instrument matters. The academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies): the former show consistent supply contraction, the latter small or null effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which plausibly explains the stable SDU stock—100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But that stability is also evidence the instrument is non-binding at the point of pain: it left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs [D1p3]. The stable stock is not proof that a binding cap is safe; it is proof the current cap does not bind [D12p2].

The contraction arithmetic is the decisive objection to a hard cap. The estimated long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply is 0.31–0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents yields about HK$900 per month to incumbents but leaves 3,000–4,000 households per year unable to enter—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: caps bind on entrants, not incumbents; when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p2][D7p3]. New York's second-generation scheme shows modest contraction precisely because it is light at first rent [D8p2]—the point Hong Kong's ordinance also leaves open.

Mrs Wong's intuition that landlords cannot exit is contradicted by her own observation: two flats in her stairwell converted to storage after the ordinance [D4p3]. The Landlords Association threatens exit if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]—though its evidence is thin, a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate, and it concedes it has no margin data [D5p3]. The threat is not proven, but the elasticity literature suggests it is not empty either.

The enforcement problem compounds the case against a hard cap. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually; a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: tenants without written agreements or the newest arrivals are least able to complain [D10p2]. The subcommittee could identify no design under which the newest-arrival population is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A cap that cannot reach its intended beneficiaries, while deterring supply, is the worst of both worlds.

What should be done? First, tenure security: written agreements, minimum notice periods, and a statutory presumption of tenancy. Mr Chan would trade rent relief for security [D3p1]; 61 percent of surveyed tenants would trade a nominal increase for three-year security, and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]. Second, a light, indexed per-square-foot band on initial rents, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation and therefore less exposed to judicial review [D9p2]. Third, redirect enforcement capacity toward conditions—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is immediate and documented [D11p2]. The NGO report's framing is correct: the segment is tight because the queue is long; caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2]. The deepest fix remains shortening the public-housing queue, which Mr Chan names as what would help most [D3p3].

Tightening is justified—but the binding constraint is insecurity, not price. A hard cap would hand the bill to those still outside, as Berlin and the elasticity estimates warn. A tenure-secure, banded, condition-focused package protects the tenants the policy is meant to serve without shrinking the stock they depend on.
