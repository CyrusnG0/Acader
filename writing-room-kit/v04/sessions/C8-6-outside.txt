# Tighten, but Not With a Hard Cap

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) — but through tenure security and an indexed initial-rent band, not a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points consistently to one conclusion: the binding constraint on SDU households is not the price of a cubicle but the length of the public-housing queue and the insecurity of tenancy, and a hard cap attacks the symptom while shrinking the stock the queue depends on.

Start with the stock arithmetic. Standard theory predicts contraction under binding ceilings, but the magnitude is an empirical question, and the reviewed literature puts the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Crucially, the paper distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies), which show small or null effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance is second-generation — it capped increases within a tenancy but left the initial rent untouched [D1p3] — and the stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 straight through it [D2p1]. That stability is real, but it is also evidence that the instrument was never binding at the point that matters.

That point is the initial rent. On a per-square-foot basis SDU tenants already pay a 15 to 25 percent premium over mainstream flats [D1p1], and real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the ordinance [D2p2]. Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU with no written agreement, reports the ordinance changed nothing she can see: the landlord still charges whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears [D4p1]. A hard cap would address exactly this pain — Mr Chan estimates it would free about 800 dollars a month, a month of food [D3p2].

But the bill for that transfer lands on households not yet inside. The modelled tightened-cap scenario yields a net transfer of roughly 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin is the cautionary case: capped initial-offer rents fell sharply, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law was struck down the suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. New York's stabilisation scheme, by contrast, shows modest contraction precisely because it is light at first-rent [D8p2] — the same point Hong Kong's ordinance left untouched.

The enforcement evidence makes a hard cap worse still. Current inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. More damning, the subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail — the population the policy names — is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. The tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited.

What, then, should tightening look like? The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, does not name rent; he names the waiting list [D3p3]. Counsel advises that an indexed, reviewable per-square-foot band — rate-capping rather than confiscation — sits on firmer legal ground than a hard cap, which would attract compensation claims and judicial review [D9p1][D9p2]. The Chief Executive's expected "floor-standard + rent band" package points the same way [D12p1].

So: tighten, but tighten where the harm is documented. Enforce fire, partition and ventilation standards; mandate written tenancy agreements and minimum notice periods; publish an initial-rent band that binds without confiscating. A hard cap would help incumbents like Mr Chan's 800 dollars while handing the bill to the next family still outside the door — and in a city where the queue runs five and a half years [D1p2], that is not a trade worth making.
