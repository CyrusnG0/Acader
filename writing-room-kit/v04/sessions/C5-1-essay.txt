Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on starting rents in subdivided units. It should tighten regulation narrowly, with security of tenure and a light, indexed band on starting rents, because a hard cap could hurt the households it is meant to help.

The case for a hard cap rests on real hardship. Tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium per square foot [D1p1], and real rent burden has gone from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Mr Chan pays about a third of his income for 120 sq ft [D3p1]. Mrs Wong has no written agreement and says the ordinance changed nothing [D4p1]. These are real harms.

But the decisive question is whether capping initial rents shrinks the stock tenants depend on. The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity in marginal housing at 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The working paper models a 15 percent cut in initial rents as a net transfer of about HK$900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000–4,000 households per year—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households—unable to enter the segment [D6p4]. That arithmetic is uncomfortable precisely because the losers are the population the cap intends to protect [D6p3].

The 2021 ordinance is sometimes cited as proof that regulation does not reduce supply: stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this is second-generation control—caps on increases within tenancies, not on initial levels [D6p2]—and the paper finds such controls show small or null stock effects, unlike first-generation hard caps, which show consistent contraction [D6p2]. Berlin illustrates the difference: capped initial offers fell, listing supply contracted within a year, and when the law was struck down, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The cap protected those already inside; the bill went to those still outside [D7p3].

New York reinforces the point. Its second-generation scheme leaves initial rents and vacancy lightly regulated, and stock contraction is modest for exactly that reason [D8p2]. Where it tightened in 2019, available stabilised listings fell as owners shifted units to other uses [D8p3]. This is an association, not proof, but the lesson points the same way.

Enforcement makes a hard cap worse still. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. If landlords reprice above-band, the first instrument tenants hold is complaint—but the least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], and the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. A hard cap would also invite compensation claims and judicial review, with litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

The landlords say owners will exit if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]. That is a real risk for a hard cap, though Mrs Wong thinks her landlords would stay because the units are their pension [D4p2].

What do tenants themselves say? Mr Chan does not name rent; he names the waiting list—if the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p3]. The NGO survey finds 61 percent would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2].

So the defensible path is tenure security plus a published per-square-foot band that regulators can defend as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2].
