# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

**Position: Hong Kong should tighten rent control on SDUs, but only through a tenure-security-plus-rent-band package—not a hard cap on initial rents.**

The case for doing nothing is weak. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium precisely because they are desperate, and the queue they are waiting in runs five and a half years [D1p1][D1p2]. Real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU with no written tenancy, reports that the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. The status quo is not neutral; it transfers income from the poorest renters to landlords who face no effective constraint on initial pricing.

But the instrument matters enormously. The evidence against a hard initial-rent cap is strong and comes from three independent directions. First, the academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (caps on levels), which show "consistent contraction effects," from second-generation controls (caps on increases), which show "small or null effects" [D6p2]. Hong Kong's stable SDU stock—growing from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]—is evidence that *moderate* regulation did not trigger exit, not that a binding cap would be equally harmless. The think-tank debate in the news digest captures exactly this ambiguity: one side reads stable stock as proof that "capacity exists for more control," the other as an artifact of a non-binding instrument [D12p2]. The second reading is more consistent with D6's elasticity estimate of 0.31–0.48, which implies a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Modelled for Hong Kong, a 15 percent initial-rent cut delivers about $900/month to incumbents while pricing out 3,000–4,000 households per year—"mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]. That is the same population the cap is meant to protect.

Second, the comparative cases reinforce the point. Berlin's Mietendeckel produced a measurable supply contraction within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats [D7p1], and when the law fell, suppressed increases released into the market and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is explicit: "the tenants the cap protects are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3]. New York's second-generation scheme, by contrast, shows only modest contraction because it is "light at first-rent"—the exact point Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance also left untouched [D8p2].

Third, enforcement reality undermines the hard-cap case even on its own terms. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. More damning: the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals—are precisely those a complaint-driven enforcement regime would miss [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which "the newest-arrival tail… is protected first rather than last" [D10p3].

So what should be done? The NGO report's survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, "does not name rent. He names the waiting list" [D3p3]. This is not sentimentality; it is revealed preference from the people the policy targets. A narrow tightening—tenure security plus a light indexed band on initial rents, as counsel suggests, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]—addresses the actual pain point without triggering the contraction arithmetic of D6. It also avoids the judicial-review risk that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream would almost certainly attract [D9p1].

The landlords' claim that they would exit en masse is not credible as stated: the Association has no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation that units are their pension [D4p2] cuts both ways—it suggests exit is costly for owners, but also that they will fight any instrument that bites.

The honest answer is that rent control treats a price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2]. The queue is the disease. But waiting for the queue to shorten while tenants pay 34 percent of income for 120 square feet is not a policy; it is an abdication. Tighten—but tighten smartly, with tenure security and a reviewable band, not a hard cap that would shrink the very stock the poorest tenants depend on.
