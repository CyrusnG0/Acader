Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in a narrow way: stronger limits on increases inside tenancies, tenure security, and better enforcement, not a hard cap on initial rents.

Tenants in subdivided units are badly served. They pay a 15 to 25 percent premium per square foot compared with ordinary flats [D1p1], and the rent burden for these households rose from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Many of them are new arrivals or families waiting for public housing, so the unit is not a real choice [D1p2].

The strongest argument against a hard initial-rent cap comes from the academic literature on supply elasticity. The working paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of SDU-type stock over five years [D6p1]. In a market of about 111,200 units [D2p1], this means thousands of units lost. The paper's model of a 15 percent initial-rent cut predicts a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, at the cost of 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals [D6p4]. This is a transfer from those outside to those already inside.

The Berlin case reinforces this concern. When Berlin capped rents on about 1.5 million flats in 2020, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats [D7p1]. When the law was struck down in 2021, rents for new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2]. Caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Despite these risks, the status quo is not good enough. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents unregulated [D1p3]. Mrs Wong, who has lived in her unit for nine years without a written agreement, says the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. A better approach combines tenure security and a light rent band. The NGO report finds that 61 percent of tenants would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security [D11p1], and Mr Chan was given two weeks notice after asking for a repair [D3p1]. A legal opinion says a band set by a published, indexed per-square-foot rate could be defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. New York's light scheme has shown modest stock contraction [D8p2].

Enforcement is the weak point. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock a year, and a band needs a tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. So tenure security should come first and the band only after enforcement capacity is built.

In conclusion, I would tighten rent control in a narrow way. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap and thinks landlords would not leave [D4p2], but the evidence on lost units and blocked newcomers [D6p4] makes that risky for the same people she speaks for. The landlords' exit threat is weak because they admit having no margin data [D5p3].
