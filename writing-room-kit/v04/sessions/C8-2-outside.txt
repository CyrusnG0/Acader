# Tighten, but Not on the Initial Rent: A Case for Tenure Security and Rate-Banding over a Hard Cap

The question of whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) is usually framed as a binary: cap rents hard, or leave the market alone. The archive suggests a third position. Hong Kong should tighten regulation, but the tightening should target tenure security and the enforcement architecture first, with only a light, band-based constraint on initial rents. A hard cap on initial rents is the one design most likely to harm the households it intends to protect.

**The case for tightening at all is strong.** The status quo is not neutral. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income between 2019 and 2025, even as the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance was in force [D2p2]. That ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left the initial rent unregulated and excluded units with co-living services [D1p3]. Mrs Wong's experience illustrates the gap precisely: her tenancy predates the ordinance, she has never had a written agreement, and the landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. Mr Chan's worst problem is not price but insecurity—a landlord ended his tenancy with two weeks' notice after a repair request [D3p1]. The binding constraint tenants name is not the rent level; it is queue length and insecurity [D11p1]. Sixty-one percent of surveyed households would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Any tightening that ignores this is treating a symptom while the disease—a five-and-a-half-year queue [D1p2]—continues untreated.

**But a hard cap on initial rents is the wrong instrument.** The academic evidence is unusually clear on this point. First-generation controls—hard caps on levels—show consistent supply contraction, while second-generation controls—caps on increases within tenancies—show small or null effects [D6p2]. The stable SDU stock under the 2021 ordinance (100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025) [D2p1] is not proof that binding controls are safe; it is evidence that a non-binding instrument does not bind [D12p2]. The modelled tightening scenario is sobering: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin's Mietendeckel shows the same asymmetry in practice—the cap protected those already inside, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2][D7p3].

**The enforcement problem is decisive.** A hard cap is only as real as its inspection regime, and current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year [D10p1]. A band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register, estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], and the newest arrivals—are exactly the ones enforcement would miss. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would therefore bind hardest on the most vulnerable, who cannot navigate the complaint system, while landlords reprice above-band and the visible market clears at the regulated rate.

**The middle path is defensible.** The legal opinion suggests a cap framed as rate-capping—a published, indexed per-square-foot band—rather than confiscation, which sits on firmer ground and avoids compensation claims under the Basic Law [D9p1][D9p2]. The NGO report supports exactly this: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, opposing a hard cap on both the contraction arithmetic and tenants' own revealed preferences [D11p3]. The consultation document expected in 2026 appears to be moving this way, floating a "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap [D12p1].

**The objection from landlords is weaker than it appears.** The Landlords Association claims thin margins and threatens exit [D5p1][D5p2], but concedes it has no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation cuts the other way: her building has four SDU landlords, none of whom would leave, because "where else would the money come from?" [D4p2]. The 2021 ordinance did not trigger visible stock exit [D2p1], and the elasticity estimate of 0.31 to 0.48 applies to binding caps, not to the light band proposed here [D6p1].

**Conclusion.** Hong Kong should tighten SDU regulation—but tighten the right thing. Tenure security, a certifiable tenancy register, and a light, indexed band on initial rents would address the insecurity tenants actually name [D11p1] while avoiding the contraction arithmetic that a hard cap guarantees [D6p4]. A hard cap would protect incumbents like Mrs Wong and hand the bill to the households still outside—the new arrivals and waiting-list families the policy is meant to serve [D1p2][D7p3]. The queue, not the rent, is the disease; regulation should not make the medicine worse than the symptoms.
