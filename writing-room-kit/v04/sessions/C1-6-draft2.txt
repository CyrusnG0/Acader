Subdivided units (SDUs) in Hong Kong house about 110,000 households, and they pay 15 to 25 percent more per square foot than people in ordinary flats [D1p1]. Most tenants are new arrivals, single parents and people waiting for public housing [D1p2]. For them the SDU is a residual option while the queue moves.

My claim is that Hong Kong should not set a hard cap on initial SDU rents, but should tighten narrowly with tenure security and at most a loose band on initial rents.

The evidence is that hard caps shrink supply. A working paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Caps on levels show consistent contraction, while caps on increases inside a tenancy show small effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is the second kind, and stock kept growing from 107,300 to 111,200 afterwards [D2p1]. Berlin shows the first kind: listings fell and rents jumped after the law was struck down [D7p1] [D7p2].

The objection from landlords is that they will leave [D5p2]. I do not accept this fully, since they admit they have no data on their margins [D5p3]. But the tenant Mr Chan fears the same thing [D3p2].

My reply is that tenants care most about security and the queue. In a survey of 440 households, 61 percent would trade a rent increase for tenure security [D11p1].
