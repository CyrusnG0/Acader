Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but it should do so by strengthening protection inside tenancies rather than by capping initial rents. This position protects tenants without the supply loss that harder caps seem to cause.

Subdivided units are a last resort for many people. About 110,000 households live in them, and tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium per square foot compared with mainstream flats [D1p1]. They are mostly new arrivals, single parents and families waiting for public housing, which has averaged about five and a half years [D1p2]. For these households the unit is not a choice. The burden is also getting worse: rent to income rose from 31 to 34 percent for SDU households [D2p2]. That is why doing nothing is hard to defend.

The current law is limited. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped rent increases within an existing tenancy but did not cap the starting rent, and it excluded co-living units from key provisions [D1p3]. It did slow things down. SDU rent per square foot grew 6.8 percent in 2019 to 2021 but only 3.1 percent in 2021 to 2023, although private rents overall grew 1.7 percent in that later window [D2p2]. So the ordinance helped, but not enough, because the starting rent was left free.

The main danger of tightening is that owners leave. The Landlords Association argues that a hard cap on initial rents is confiscatory and says members will convert units back or empty them if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1, D5p2]. I take this seriously, but the evidence behind it is thin. The Association admits it has no data on its members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. A threat is not data.

Better evidence comes from research and from Berlin. The working paper separates first-generation controls, which are hard caps on levels, from second-generation controls, which cap increases inside tenancies. Hard caps show consistent contraction, while second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. Hong Kong's stock kept growing from 107,300 in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025 after the ordinance [D2p1], which fits that pattern. Berlin shows what the other path looks like. After its 2020 cap, listing supply fell within a year as owners withheld flats, and when the law was struck down rents for new contracts jumped above the earlier trend [D7p1, D7p2]. The lesson is that caps protect those already inside and hand the bill to those outside [D7p3].

This is why I do not support a hard cap. The same paper warns that the households who lose units are the same people the cap is meant to protect [D6p3]. If a cap removes 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years for every 10 percent rent cut, as the estimates suggest [D6p1], then new arrivals and families in the queue would pay for it.

My proposal is therefore to tighten the second-generation tools. Extend in-tenancy protection to the co-living units that were excluded [D1p3], and give tenants more security so they can stay in regulated tenancies longer. The working paper says tenant gains depend on how many stay long enough to offset those who cannot find units [D6p3]. This addresses the rising burden without copying Berlin.

There are limits to my argument. The post-2023 rent figures rely on survey extrapolation because reporting is voluntary [D2p3], so the recent trend is uncertain. I also cannot prove that a stronger in-tenancy rule would reduce the burden, since the current one did not stop it rising [D2p2]. Still, the evidence supports moving carefully, and it does not support a hard cap.
