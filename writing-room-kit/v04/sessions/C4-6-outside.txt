# The Case Against a Hard Cap on SDU Rents

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units through a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead pursue a narrow package of tenure security and a light, indexed rent band, paired with a genuine attack on the public-housing queue. The evidence in this archive points consistently in one direction: a hard cap would transfer income to incumbent tenants while shrinking the stock of units available to the very households the policy claims to protect.

The strongest argument for tightening is the lived reality of tenants. Mr Chan pays HK$5,800 for 120 square feet, roughly a third of household income, and describes insecurity as worse than price: his landlord ended a tenancy with two weeks' notice when he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. Mrs Wong has lived in an SDU for nine years with no written agreement, and reports that the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent even after the ordinance [D2p2]. These are serious harms, and the status quo is not defensible on its own terms.

But the remedy matters. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance was a second-generation instrument: it capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent unregulated [D1p3]. The academic literature distinguishes precisely these two designs. First-generation controls on rent levels show consistent supply contraction; second-generation controls on increases show small or null effects [D6p2]. The stable SDU stock — from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 — is therefore evidence that *moderate* regulation did not trigger exit, not evidence that a binding cap would be safe [D2p1]. As one think-tank piece notes, the visible stability may be an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and a binding one behaves differently [D12p2].

How differently? The reviewed elasticity estimates for marginal SDU-type stock run 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about HK$900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism in practice: capped initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law was struck down a backlog of suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is explicit — the cap protects those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

The landlord side is not credible on its own. The Association claims thin margins and threatens exit, but concedes it has no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation that units are landlords' pension and that they will not leave is at least as plausible [D4p2]. Yet the Association's threat does not need to be universally true to matter: even a partial exit at the bottom removes housing that houses someone tonight [D5p2].

Two further problems make a hard cap administratively self-defeating. First, enforcement capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Second, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement — Mrs Wong's situation [D4p1] — or the newest arrivals, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A legal opinion adds that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income would invite compensation claims and judicial review, with a litigation timeline outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

The better path is the one the NGO report and the consultation signals converge on: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, and enforcement resources redirected to fire, partition and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2][D11p3][D12p1]. Mr Chan himself does not name rent as the priority — he names the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. That is the real constraint. A hard cap treats the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2], and in doing so would shrink the quantity further. Hong Kong should regulate tenure and conditions tightly, cap initial rents lightly and reviewably, and treat the public-housing queue as the policy that actually helps the households named in [D1p2].
