# Tighten the Tenancy, Not the Rent: A Case Against a Hard Cap on Hong Kong's Subdivided Units

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) with a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead pursue a targeted package of tenancy security and a light, indexed rent band. The evidence in this archive points consistently to one conclusion: a binding cap on initial rents would shrink the very stock the poorest households depend on, while leaving the most exploited tenants—new arrivals and those without written agreements—systematically unprotected.

The case for a hard cap rests on genuine hardship. Mr Chan pays HK$5,800 for 120 sq ft, about a third of household income, and Mrs Wong has lived nine years in an SDU with no written tenancy, watching rent rise "by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. The pain is real, and the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance did not address it: it capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated [D1p3][D9p1].

But the decisive question is not whether tenants hurt, but whether a cap helps them. The academic evidence says no, or at least not net. The long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rents is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents would transfer about HK$900 per month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. That is the cruelest arithmetic in the archive: the households priced out are the same population the cap intends to protect [D6p3].

The 2021 ordinance is often cited as proof that regulation is safe, since SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument. The working paper distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies), which show small or null effects [D6p2]. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation [D6p2], and its stability therefore tells us nothing about what a binding initial-rent cap would do. The think-tank debate in the news digest captures exactly this disagreement [D12p2].

Landlord exit threats should be read with care, but not dismissed. The Association's submission is self-interested and evidentially weak—no data on actual margins, and compliance costs drawn from a 210-member survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Yet Mr Chan has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2], and Mrs Wong reports flats in her stairwell converted to storage even as she insists landlords cannot exit [D4p3]. Berlin offers the cautionary tale: after the Mietendeckel, supply contracted measurably as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The tenants a cap protects are those already inside; the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

A hard cap also fails on enforcement. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, and the newest arrivals—are precisely those enforcement would miss, so the regime would systematically fail the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would also invite compensation claims and judicial review, with litigation timelines outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

What should be done instead? The NGO survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan himself does not name rent—he names the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The binding constraint is quantity, not price [D11p2]. The appropriate response is a narrow tightening: tenure security plus a light, indexed band on initial rents, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2][D11p3]. That would address Mrs Wong's insecurity and Mr Chan's fear of two weeks' notice without triggering the contraction that a hard cap would.

A hard cap is emotionally satisfying and economically self-defeating. Hong Kong should regulate the tenancy, not the rent.
