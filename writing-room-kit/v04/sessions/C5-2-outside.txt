# Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units: A Case for Targeted Reform, Not a Hard Cap

The question of whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) is not a binary choice between laissez-faire and confiscation. The evidence in the course archive points toward a nuanced answer: a hard cap on initial rents would likely harm the very households it intends to protect, while a carefully designed package of tenure security and light-touch rent bands could improve tenant welfare without triggering significant stock exit. I argue that Hong Kong should tighten regulation, but only through second-generation controls and accompanying enforcement infrastructure—not through a first-generation hard cap.

**The Case Against a Hard Cap**

The strongest argument against a hard initial-rent cap comes from the academic literature on supply elasticity. The working paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of SDU-type stock over five years [D6p1]. In a market of approximately 111,200 units [D2p1], this translates to 3,300 to 5,500 units lost—housing that currently shelters the most vulnerable. The paper's modelling of a 15 percent initial-rent cut predicts a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, but at the cost of 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. This is a regressive transfer: protecting those already inside at the expense of those still outside.

The Berlin case reinforces this concern. When Berlin capped rents on approximately 1.5 million flats in 2020, classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. When the law was struck down in 2021, suppressed increases released into the market and rents for new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is stark: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

The Landlords Association's threat of exit [D5p2] is self-interested and empirically thin—it concedes no data on actual margins and relies on a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Yet the academic evidence suggests that even without coordinated exit, marginal units will leave the market if returns fall below operating costs. Mr Chan's observation that two units on his floor have been emptied and locked for a year [D3p2] is anecdotal but consistent with the elasticity estimates.

**The Case for Targeted Tightening**

Despite these risks, the status quo is indefensible. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents unregulated [D1p3]. The result is that Mrs Wong, who has lived in her SDU for nine years, reports the ordinance changed nothing she can see: her landlord still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears [D4p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent between 2019 and 2023 [D2p2]. The instrument is non-binding on the actual pain point.

A better approach would combine two elements. First, tenure security: the NGO report finds that 61 percent of tenants would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan's experience—evicted with two weeks' notice after requesting a repair [D3p1]—illustrates the harm that insecurity inflicts. Second, a light band on initial rents: the legal opinion suggests that a cap framed as rate-capping by reference to a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, could survive judicial review where a hard cap would not [D9p2]. This is consistent with the New York model, where second-generation controls have produced only modest stock contraction because the scheme is light at first-rent [D8p2].

**The Enforcement Gap**

Any tightening is only as real as its enforcement. Current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to establish [D10p1]. More troubling, the internal minute records that enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. This is a serious objection, but it argues for building enforcement capacity, not for abandoning regulation altogether.

**Conclusion**

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on SDUs, but not through a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence from supply elasticity theory [D6p1], the Berlin case [D7p1–D7p3], and the contraction arithmetic [D6p4] all point to the same conclusion: first-generation controls would shrink the stock and harm new entrants. Instead, the government should adopt a second-generation package: mandatory written tenancies with three-year security of tenure, a published and indexed rent band for initial rents, and a substantial investment in inspection and enforcement infrastructure. This approach would address the actual insecurities tenants face—eviction and unregulated increases—while avoiding the stock exit that a hard cap would trigger. The queue for public housing remains the binding constraint [D11p1], but until it shortens, the SDU segment needs regulation that protects tenants without destroying the housing they depend on.
