Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? My answer is yes, but not in the way I first thought. My first instinct was a hard cap on starting rents. After reading the course archive I now think the better tightening is three-year tenure security plus a light band on initial rents.

The need is clear. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they pay 15 to 25 percent more per square foot than people in mainstream flats [D1p1]. Many are new arrivals, single parents, or people waiting for public housing, which has averaged around five and a half years [D1p2]. For them the unit is a residual option, not a choice. That is why landlords hold so much power: a tenant cannot easily walk away.

The current rule is too weak. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped rent increases inside an existing tenancy but not the starting rent [D1p3]. Under it, rent growth per square foot slowed, yet the rent to income burden of SDU households still rose from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. So the present control has not fixed affordability. This is the first part of my claim. The second part is that rent rules alone cannot solve the problem. An NGO survey of 440 households found the main constraint tenants name is queue length and insecure tenure, not rent level, and 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of security [D11p1].

The strongest objection is about supply, and it changed my mind about a hard cap. A working paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of marginal stock over five years [D6p1]. In its model of a 15 percent cut in initial rents, incumbents gain about 900 dollars a month, while 3,000 to 4,000 households a year cannot enter the segment [D6p4]. Berlin points the same way. After its 2020 cap, listed supply contracted within a year, and the report concludes the tenants who benefit are those already inside while the bill goes to those still outside [D7p1][D7p3]. Since the people locked out are mostly newcomers, a hard cap would hurt the same group it is meant to protect.

I do not accept the landlords' claim in full, though. The Landlords Association says members will leave the segment if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]. But it also admits it has no data on members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. The 3 to 5 percent figure is stock contraction, which is related to owners leaving but is not a direct count of it, so I will not claim to know how many would go. What I can say is that the threat is real but partial and not as total as the Association suggests.

My reply is that the tightening should be of the kind that did not shrink stock. SDU stock grew from 100,800 in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, including through the 2021 ordinance [D2p1]. I should be careful here: the working paper says this stability exists because the ordinance is a second-generation control that caps increases within tenancies, not hard levels [D6p2]. So I am not saying landlords simply stayed put. I am saying that softer rules have a better record on supply, and the NGO report reaches the same view, supporting tenure security and a light band on initial rents while opposing a hard cap [D11p3].

There is one limit on my claim. The NGO report also argues that enforcement effort spent policing rents is not spent on inspecting fire, partition and ventilation, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. I take this seriously. Rent rules are second best to shortening the public housing queue and enforcing safety. My own reasoning, not something the passages say, is that tenure security should be less costly to enforce than checking every rent level, so it fits this limit better than a hard cap would.

In the end, Hong Kong should tighten, but carefully. Extend the 2021 approach with longer tenure and a light band on starting rents, and do not use a hard initial cap, because its costs fall on the newcomers who most need a place.
