Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? I think it should tighten protection for tenants, but a hard rent cap is the wrong tool. The evidence I looked at in the course archive points to tenure security as the bigger problem, and to real risks if the rules are too blunt.

Start with the tenant's side. Mr Chan is a delivery driver who lives with his wife and two children in a 120 square foot cubicle. He pays 5,800 dollars in rent, about a third of the household income [D3p1]. That is a heavy burden and it shows why people want a cap. But he says the worst part is not the price. It is insecurity. His last landlord ended the tenancy with two weeks notice after he asked for a leaking window to be repaired [D3p1]. So a tenant who complains can lose his home, and a rent cap alone does nothing about that.

Mr Chan is also split on the cap itself. He says a hard cap could free up around 800 dollars a month, which is about a month of food [D3p2]. Yet he worries that landlords would rather turn cubicles back into storage rooms than deal with regulated tenants, and he has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]. This is only one interview, but I take it seriously.

The Berlin case supports his worry. Berlin capped rents on about 1.5 million flats in 2020. Asking rents for new listings fell sharply, which looks like a success at first. But the supply of listed flats shrank within a year because owners held flats back or moved them into segments that were not controlled [D7p1]. For a city like Hong Kong where subdivided units are already scarce, losing supply would hurt the very people the policy is meant to help.

The Berlin story also has a second lesson. The court struck the law down in 2021 for legal reasons about federal powers, not because of the economics [D7p2]. When the cap fell, suppressed increases came back and rents for new contracts jumped above the earlier trend [D7p2]. So a cap that is hard and temporary can leave tenants worse off than before. I should be careful here, because the court case says little about whether the cap worked. But the rebound shows that a freeze can store up pressure rather than remove it.

Putting this together, my position is that Hong Kong should tighten rules for subdivided units, but it should focus on security of tenure rather than a hard cap. That means longer notice periods and protection for tenants who ask for repairs, which is exactly what Mr Chan said went wrong for him [D3p1]. A softer limit on rent increases could sit alongside this, so that landlords are not pushed to lock units away [D3p2].

There are limits to my argument. I only read two sources, one interview and one comparison with a very different city. I did not look at the data or the economic papers, so I cannot say how big the supply effect would be in Hong Kong. But on the evidence I have, tightening protection without a hard cap seems the safer path.
