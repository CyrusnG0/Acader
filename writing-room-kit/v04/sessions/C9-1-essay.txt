**Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units?**

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) — but not with a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points toward a narrower, better-targeted package: a light initial-rent band combined with meaningful tenure security and faster rehousing. A hard cap is the intuitive response to a genuine injustice, but the archive shows it would likely harm the very households it is meant to protect.

The case for doing nothing is weak. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs [D1p3]. Mrs Wong's experience — nine years in an SDU, no written agreement, rent raised "by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1] — shows the current instrument misses the actual pain point. The status quo is not neutral; it is a slow transfer from the poorest renters to small landlords.

Yet the archive is equally clear that a hard cap on initial rents would backfire. The academic literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock at 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is stark: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about HK$900 per month to incumbents while pricing out 3,000–4,000 households per year, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those excluded are precisely the population named in [D1p2] — new arrivals, single-parent households, public-housing waiters. Berlin confirms the mechanism: after the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The bill for protecting insiders is handed to outsiders.

Crucially, the stable SDU stock under the 2021 ordinance [D2p1] is not evidence that a binding cap is safe. The working paper distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases), which show small or null effects [D6p2]. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation and non-binding at the first-rent margin — which is exactly why stock held steady, and why the think-tank argument that "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2] misreads the data. A binding first-generation cap would behave like Berlin, not like 2021 Hong Kong.

Enforcement makes a hard cap worse still. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. The subcommittee's own modelling concedes it could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. Mrs Wong's landlord never gave her a written agreement [D4p1]; tenants least able to complain are exactly those a cap is meant to help. A hard cap would thus be strongest on paper and weakest where exploitation is deepest.

What, then, should be done? The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, did not name rent — he named the waiting list [D3p3]. The legal opinion offers a defensible middle instrument: cap initial rent by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band, framed as rate-capping rather than confiscation, which avoids the compensation and judicial-review risk of a hard cap [D9p2]. This is also the direction the government appears to be moving, with a "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap [D12p1].

The honest trade-off is this: a band plus tenure security will not deliver the full HK$800 monthly relief Mr Chan imagines [D3p2], and it will not satisfy those who see landlord margins as the problem. But it avoids the contraction arithmetic of [D6p4], it targets the insecurity tenants actually name [D11p1], and it preserves the stock that houses someone tonight. Tightening is justified; the question is which instrument. The archive's answer is a light band on first rents, real security of tenure, and — above all — a shorter queue. Rent control treats the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2]. Hong Kong should tighten, but it should not mistake a cap for a solution.
