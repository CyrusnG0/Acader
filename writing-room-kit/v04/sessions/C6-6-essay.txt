**Tighten Tenure, Not Rents: A Qualified Case Against a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on initial rents in subdivided units (SDUs). It should instead tighten regulation along the dimension tenants themselves identify as binding—security of tenure—paired with a light, indexed band on initial rents and a credible enforcement register. The evidence in this archive points consistently to one conclusion: a hard cap on initial rent levels would transfer income to today's incumbents while shrinking the residual stock that tomorrow's most vulnerable households depend on, and it would do so through an enforcement apparatus that cannot reach the tenants it claims to protect.

Start with the central trade-off. Standard theory predicts contraction under binding ceilings, and the reviewed literature puts the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48—a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Modelled for a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields about HK$900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. That is not a hypothetical harm; it is the same population the cap is meant to help [D1p2].

The strongest counter-argument is that Hong Kong already ran the experiment. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but not initial rents, and the stock grew from 107,300 units in 2021 to 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this stability is precisely what the academic literature predicts for second-generation controls—caps on increases show small or null stock effects, while first-generation controls on levels show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The think-tank reading that "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2] misreads a non-binding instrument as proof that a binding one would behave the same way. The 2021 ordinance left the actual pain point—initial rent—untouched [D9p1].

Tenants themselves confirm where the pain lies. Mr Chan, facing a possible HK$800 monthly saving, fears landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants, and has watched two units on his floor sit locked for a year [D3p2]. Asked what would help most, he does not name rent; he names the waiting list—he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. A hard cap addresses a symptom while the quantity constraint—the queue—remains untouched [D11p2].

Enforcement makes the case worse. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. The tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement—Mrs Wong has lived in her SDU for nine years without one, and reports the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]—or the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail, and the subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival population is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

The comparative record reinforces caution. Berlin's Mietendeckel suppressed listed rents, then supply contracted as owners withheld or rerouted flats; when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. New York's second-generation scheme shows modest contraction precisely because it is light at first-rent [D8p2], while its 2019 tightening is associated with a fall in available stabilised listings [D8p3]. Legal risk compounds the problem: a hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream invites compensation claims and judicial review whose timeline alone would outlast a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

What should Hong Kong do instead? Tighten tenure security—written agreements, notice periods, protection against retaliatory eviction—because that is what tenants reveal they value [D3p3][D11p1]. Pair it with the middle instrument counsel identifies: an indexed, reviewable per-square-foot band on initial rents, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. Fund the register and inspection capacity first, so enforcement reaches the tail rather than the documented. And treat the queue as the real policy lever: every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing prices is a unit not spent on fire, partition and ventilation harms that are documented and immediate [D11p2].

A hard cap is the intuitive answer, and it is the wrong one. It would pay incumbents, shrink the bottom of the market, and fail the newest arrivals first. Hong Kong should tighten tenure, not rents.
