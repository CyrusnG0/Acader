# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

**Position: No—not with a hard cap. Hong Kong should tighten tenure security and adopt a light, indexed rent band, but reject a hard cap on initial rents.**

The case for tightening rent control rests on real hardship. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium precisely because they are desperate [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. Mrs Wong's account—nine years, no written agreement, rents set by "whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]—shows the 2021 instrument left the actual pain point untouched, since it capped increases within tenancies but not initial rent levels [D1p3]. Something should change. But the evidence suggests a hard cap is the wrong instrument.

First, the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance is not evidence that a binding cap is safe. The academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent supply contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases), which show small or null effects [D6p2]. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which explains the stock growth from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1][D6p2]. The think-tank argument that this stability proves "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2] misreads the mechanism: the visible stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and the estimated elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels is 0.31–0.48—a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1][D6p2].

Second, the welfare arithmetic is regressive in a way that matters. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about HK$900 per month to incumbents but leaves 3,000–4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin confirms the pattern: caps protect those already inside, and the bill goes to those still outside—when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2][D7p3]. Mr Chan, whose family would gain roughly HK$800 a month from a hard cap, already fears landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants, and he has seen two units on his floor locked for a year [D3p2]. His own ranking is telling: asked what would help most, he names the waiting list, not rent [D3p3].
