**Tighten Tenure, Not the First Rent**

The question of whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) is usually framed as a binary: cap rents or leave the market alone. The archive suggests a third position. Hong Kong should tighten regulation of the SDU segment, but the instrument that tightening should target is tenancy security and habitability, not a hard cap on initial rents. A hard first-rent cap would transfer income to incumbents while shrinking entry at the bottom, exactly where the harm is concentrated [D6p3, D6p4]. A tenure-and-standards package, by contrast, addresses the constraint tenants themselves name, and it avoids the legal and enforcement traps that a binding cap would set.

Start with what tenants actually say. Mr Chan, a delivery driver in Kwai Chung, is offered a hard cap that would free about 800 dollars a month, but he names the waiting list, not rent, as what would help most: "If the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave" [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households confirms this is not one man's preference: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mrs Wong, who supports a hard cap, has lived in her unit nine years with no written agreement, and the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. Her testimony is not evidence that caps work; it is evidence that a cap without enforcement infrastructure is decorative.

The supply evidence points the same way. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but not initial rents, and the estimated SDU stock grew from 107,300 in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. That stability is real, but the academic literature warns it is an artifact of a non-binding instrument: second-generation controls show small or null stock effects, while first-generation caps on levels show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The elasticity estimate for marginal SDU-type stock is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The paper's Hong Kong-like model is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]. Those are precisely the households the cap claims to protect [D1p2].

Berlin shows the mechanism in the wild. The Mietendeckel capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats; initial-offer rents fell, but classified-listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted units, and when the court struck the law down, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p1, D7p2]. New York's second-generation scheme, light at first rent, shows modest stock contraction but falling maintenance incentives and a measurable fall in available stabilised listings after the 2019 tightening [D8p2, D8p3]. The lesson is consistent: the tenants a hard cap protects are those already inside; the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

There are also legal and enforcement reasons to prefer a different instrument. A hard cap framed as deprivation of rental income invites compensation claims under the Basic Law; a cap framed as contractual-term regulation sits on firmer ground but leaves the actual pain point—the initial rent—unregulated [D9p1]. A band-based middle instrument is defensible, but it requires survey infrastructure that does not exist, and the Rating and Valuation Department estimates 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up a certifiable tenancy register [D9p2, D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals least able to complain [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

The alternative is not deregulation. It is to spend the same political capital on tenure security, a written-agreement mandate, and condition inspection—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. The NGO report supports exactly this: a narrow tightening of tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, and opposition to a hard cap [D11p3]. Mr Chan's fear that landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants [D3p2] is a supply warning, but it is also a reason to regulate the tenancy relationship rather than the price. If the queue is the binding constraint [D11p1], then the highest-return intervention is to shorten it and to make the waiting period survivable—not to cap a price and hand the adjustment to the households still outside the door.
