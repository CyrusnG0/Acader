**Tighten Carefully, but Don't Cap the First Rent**

The question of whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) is really two questions: should the government regulate rents more aggressively, and if so, which instrument? The archive points toward a defensible position: yes, tighten—but through tenure security and a band-based cap on initial rents, not a hard cap on rent levels. A hard cap would likely help today's tenants while shrinking the stock that tomorrow's tenants depend on, and it would systematically fail the most vulnerable households it claims to protect.

Start with the strongest argument for tightening: the status quo is not working. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs from key provisions [D1p3]. Mrs Wong's experience illustrates the gap: nine years in an SDU, no written tenancy agreement, and a landlord who "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent even under the ordinance [D2p2]. The instrument that exists regulates the wrong margin.

But the case for a hard cap collapses under the supply evidence. The academic literature estimates long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48—a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars per month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]. Those excluded are precisely the population named in D1: new arrivals, single-parent households, public-housing waiters [D1p2]. The cap protects insiders and bills outsiders.

The comparative cases reinforce this. Berlin's Mietendeckel produced measurable supply contraction within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. New York's second-generation scheme shows modest contraction precisely because it is "light at first-rent" [D8p2]. The lesson is consistent: first-generation controls on rent levels contract stock; second-generation controls on increases do not [D6p2].

The 2021 ordinance's stable stock [D2p1] is sometimes cited as evidence that more control is safe. But D6p2 explains this as an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and D12p2 records the same debate between think tanks. Stability under a light regime says little about behavior under a binding one.

Enforcement is the second fatal problem for a hard cap. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based register would require 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain—those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], and the newest arrivals—are exactly those a complaint-driven enforcement regime would miss [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would thus be announced as protection and delivered as theater for the most exploited.

What about the landlords' exit threat? The Association claims members will exit if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2], but it concedes it has no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation cuts the other way: units are landlords' pensions, and "where else would the money come from?" [D4p2]. Yet she also reports two flats converted to storage after the ordinance [D4p3]—a contradiction, but also evidence that some exit does occur. The honest reading is that exit is real but modest under light regulation, and larger under binding caps, as D6 predicts.

So what should be done? The NGO report's survey is instructive: 61 percent of tenants would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, "does not name rent. He names the waiting list" [D3p3]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not price alone [D11p1]. The legal opinion offers a workable middle path: cap initial rent by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. This addresses the pain point Mrs Wong identifies while avoiding the contraction arithmetic of a hard cap.

The strongest counterargument is that a band without enforcement infrastructure is symbolic. That is a reason to build the register and inspection capacity first—not a reason to pass a hard cap that cannot be enforced and would shrink supply. Tighten tenure security immediately; build the band infrastructure; expand the public-housing queue. Do not cap the first rent. The evidence says the tenants a hard cap helps are the ones already inside, and the ones it hurts are still outside the door.
