Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in a narrow way. It should limit rent increases inside tenancies and give tenants longer security of tenure. It should not bring in a hard cap on rent levels. This position fits the evidence in the course archive better than either doing nothing or going for a strict cap.

The strongest worry about tighter control is that landlords will pull units off the market. The working paper on supply elasticity says that in marginal housing, a 10 percent rent cut caused by a cap removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The landlords association says the same thing in its own words. It argues a hard initial-rent cap is confiscatory and promises that members will leave the segment if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1][D5p2]. If that happened, housing would disappear that, however poor, still houses someone tonight.

However, the landlord claim is weaker than it first looks. The association admits it has no data on its members' real margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with only a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. So its threat to exit is a claim, not a measured fact. The academic paper is more careful. It separates first-generation controls, which are hard caps on levels, from second-generation controls, which cap increases within tenancies. Hard caps show consistent contraction, while second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock [D6p2].

Hong Kong's own history supports this split. The 2021 ordinance is a second-generation control. After it, the estimated stock of subdivided units kept growing, from 100,800 in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021 and 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. That suggests moderate regulation did not cause visible exit. So the fear of supply loss does not seem to apply to the kind of control I am recommending.

There is also a good reason to want something tighter than the current rules. The rent data show that median rent per square foot in subdivided units still rose 3.1 percent in 2021 to 2023, faster than the 1.7 percent for general private rent. Over the same period the rent burden for these households worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Tenants are still under pressure even with the ordinance, so leaving things as they are does not look fair.

Still, tenants may not mainly want lower rent. The NGO survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would trade a rent increase for three years of security of tenure, and 74 percent would trade it for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The report says the real problem is a long queue, and that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem. It also says enforcement effort is better spent on inspecting fire and ventilation conditions than on policing rents [D11p2]. This is why I would tighten tenure protection first, and not simply lower rents.

One limit of my argument is that the NGO has its own view, so its survey should be read with care. The report itself supports a narrow tightening, with tenure security and a light band on initial rents, and opposes a hard cap [D11p3]. That is close to my position, though my package leaves out even the light initial-rent band to stay safely away from the contraction risk in D6p1. A light band might be acceptable, but the evidence I read does not say how light is safe.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units by capping increases and extending tenure security, and it should reject a hard rent cap. The contraction evidence applies to hard caps [D6p1][D6p2], the Hong Kong stock data show no exit under the milder rules [D2p1], and tenants say they value security over a lower price [D11p1]. This keeps people housed tonight while still easing the pressure on their rents.
