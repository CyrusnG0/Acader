Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? My answer is yes, but not in the way I first thought. I began by expecting that a firm cap on rents would be fair, because tenants have little power. After reading the course archive I now think the city should tighten limits on rent increases, give tenants three years of security of tenure, and move enforcement effort towards the condition of the flats. It should not cap starting rents.

The case for doing something is strong. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and per square foot they pay 15 to 25 percent more than tenants in ordinary flats [D1p1]. These tenants are often new arrivals, single parents, or people waiting for public housing, so the flat is a leftover option and not a free choice [D1p2]. When people cannot walk away, a market does not protect them well. That is why I still support tightening.

What the city has done so far is limited. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped rent increases inside a tenancy but did not cap the starting rent [D1p3]. The data suggest it did no harm to supply: the number of units grew from 107,300 in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. Rents per square foot rose more slowly under the law, 3.1 percent against 6.8 percent before it [D2p2]. However, rent burden still went from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2], so there is room to tighten. One warning is that the data after 2023 rely on extrapolation [D2p3].

The obvious next step is a hard cap on starting rents. I rejected it because of the evidence. A working paper separates hard caps on levels from caps on increases. Hard caps show steady shrinking of supply, while caps on increases show small or no effects [D6p2]. Its model of a 15 percent cut in starting rents finds that people already inside gain about 900 dollars a month, but 3,000 to 4,000 households a year cannot get in, mostly new arrivals [D6p4]. Berlin shows the same pattern. Its cap helped those already inside and handed the bill to those still outside, and when a court struck it down, rents for new contracts jumped above the old trend [D7p2][D7p3]. A rule meant to protect the poorest would hurt the poorest newcomers.

There are two objections to my position. The first comes from landlords, who say members will leave the market if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]. I do not find this convincing. The association admits it has no data on its members' real margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Also, stock kept growing after 2021 [D2p1]. I take the threat seriously only for a hard cap, which is one more reason to avoid it.

The second objection is stronger, and it comes from the government's own minute. Inspection covers under 6 percent of stock each year [D10p1]. Tenants who have no written agreement or who are new are the least able to complain, so enforcement based on complaints would miss the most exploited [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not find a design that protects the newest arrivals first [D10p3]. I accept this. It means rent rules alone will not be enough, and my claim has to be narrower than I first wanted.

This is why I add tenure security. A survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would accept a nominal rent rise for three years of security, and 74 percent would trade it for a faster public housing offer [D11p1]. The report also argues that caps treat the price symptom of a long queue, and that inspection effort is better spent on fire, partition and ventilation, where harm is documented [D11p2]. I agree with most of this. I still keep a limit on increases, because without one a landlord could end a secure tenancy by pricing the tenant out at renewal.

So my position is a narrower tightening. Limit increases more tightly, give three years of tenure, and put enforcement into conditions. Do not cap starting rents, and do not pretend rent rules alone will reach the people in the weakest position. The evidence changed my mind about the tool, but not about the problem: these tenants are paying a premium for very little power.
