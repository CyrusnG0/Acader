Hong Kong should tighten its rules on subdivided units, but only narrowly. The new rules should give tenants more security of tenure and add a light limit on initial rents. They should not be a hard cap on rent levels. In this essay I argue that a hard cap would hurt the people it is meant to help, while doing nothing would also leave tenants worse off.

First, the case for tightening at all. The 2021 ordinance limits rent increases inside a tenancy, and it has not fully protected tenants. Median rent per square foot rose 3.1 percent in 2021 to 2023, against 1.7 percent for general private rents, and the share of income that SDU households pay in rent went from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. So leaving the current rule alone is not enough.

Second, why not a hard cap. A working paper reviewing the literature estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The same paper makes the point that bothers me most: the households who lose units are the same people the cap is supposed to protect [D6p3]. Berlin shows how this can look in practice. After its 2020 cap, the supply of listed flats shrank within a year as owners withheld or moved flats [D7p1]. The note concludes that the tenants who gain are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3]. Hong Kong has a long queue for public housing, so the people outside are exactly the ones who would be hurt.

Third, the evidence that moderate rules are safer. Hong Kong's stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 109,800 in 2023, including the period after the 2021 ordinance [D2p1]. The working paper explains this by saying the ordinance is a second-generation control, which caps increases within tenancies, and these show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. New York is a similar example. Its scheme caps increases and regulates initial rents only lightly, and stock contraction there is modest while tenants stay longer [D8p2]. Longer stays are a kind of tenure security.

What do tenants themselves want? In a survey of 440 SDU households, 61 percent said they would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of security of tenure, and 74 percent would trade for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The NGO behind the survey supports a narrow tightening of tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, and opposes a hard cap [D11p3]. The sample is only 440 households and the NGO has its own view, so I treat this as supporting evidence, not proof. Still, it fits the other sources.

There are objections. The landlords' association says a hard initial-rent cap is confiscatory and that members will exit if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]. I take this seriously for a hard cap, which is why I reject one. But the association admits it has no data on its members' margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey of 210 members with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. That is a weak basis for saying that even a light band would remove units. I also accept that the stock figures after 2023 rely on survey extrapolation because reporting is voluntary [D2p3], so my claim that stock stayed stable is solid only up to 2023.

A second objection is that even light tightening has costs. New York's 2019 tightening is associated with fewer stabilised listings, and regulated returns weaken the incentive to maintain units [D8p3]. The NGO also warns that enforcement capacity spent on policing rents is not spent on inspecting fire and ventilation, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. These points are why I say the band must be light and simple. It should rely on written tenancy agreements so that it is easy to check, and the government should keep inspecting conditions.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten, but by adding tenure security and a light band on initial rents, not by capping rent levels. The evidence shows the current rule leaves rent burdens rising, hard caps shrink supply at the bottom, and tenants say security matters more to them than the rent number.
