# Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units: A Case for Narrow, Tenure-Focused Reform

Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on initial rents in subdivided units (SDUs). It should instead tighten regulation narrowly—combining security of tenure with a light, indexed band on initial rents—because the evidence in this archive shows that a hard cap would protect incumbents at the expense of the very households the policy aims to help, while the binding constraint tenants themselves name is not price but queue length and insecurity.

The case for a hard cap rests on real hardship. Median SDU rent is only about 30 percent below mainstream flats, yet tenants pay a 15–25 percent premium per square foot [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Mr Chan pays roughly a third of household income for 120 sq ft [D3p1]. Mrs Wong, without any written agreement, sees the ordinance change nothing [D4p1]. These are genuine harms, and something should be done.

But the decisive question is whether capping initial rents shrinks the stock tenants depend on. The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity in marginal housing at 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The working paper models a 15 percent cut in initial rents as a net transfer of about HK$900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000–4,000 households per year—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households—unable to enter the segment [D6p4]. That arithmetic is uncomfortable precisely because the losers are the population the cap intends to protect [D6p3].

The 2021 ordinance is sometimes cited as proof that regulation does not reduce supply: stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this is second-generation control—caps on increases within tenancies, not on initial levels [D6p2]—and the paper finds such controls show small or null stock effects, unlike first-generation hard caps, which show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The think-tank argument that stable stock proves capacity for more control confuses a non-binding instrument with a binding one [D12p2]. Berlin illustrates the difference: capped initial offers fell, listing supply contracted within a year, and when the law was struck down, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The cap protected those already inside; the bill went to those still outside [D7p3].

New York reinforces the point. Its second-generation scheme leaves initial rents and vacancy lightly regulated, and stock contraction is modest for exactly that reason [D8p2]. Where it tightened in 2019, available stabilised listings fell as owners shifted units to other uses [D8p3]. The lesson is consistent: light-at-first-rent schemes preserve stock; hard caps on entry prices do not.

Enforcement makes a hard cap worse still. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. If landlords reprice above-band, the first instrument tenants hold is complaint—but the least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], and the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would also invite compensation claims and judicial review, with litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

What do tenants themselves say? Mr Chan does not name rent; he names the waiting list—if the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p3]. The NGO survey finds 61 percent would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem: the segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2].

The narrow path is therefore the defensible one: tenure security plus a light, indexed band on initial rents, which regulators can defend as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], supported by the enforcement infrastructure the government has not yet built [D10p1]. This addresses Mrs Wong's core grievance—arbitrary increases and no written agreement—without triggering the contraction that would fall hardest on those still outside. A hard cap would feel like protection tonight and remove the housing someone needs tomorrow.
