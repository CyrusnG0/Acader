**Tighten the Tenancy, Not the Rent: A Case Against a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

The question of whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) is usually framed as a binary: protect tenants from exploitative rents, or preserve the stock of housing they depend on. The course archive suggests a third position. Hong Kong should tighten regulation of SDUs, but the instrument that should be tightened is tenancy security and habitability, not the initial rent level. A hard cap on initial rents is the one intervention that the evidence most clearly warns against, because it targets the symptom (price) while the binding constraint named by tenants themselves is something else entirely: queue length and insecurity.

The case for a hard cap rests on real hardship. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats [D1p1], and the median tenant is a new arrival, single parent, or public-housing waiter facing a five-and-a-half-year queue [D1p2]. Real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Mrs Wong's account of a landlord who "raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" is damning [D4p1]. But the archive's strongest evidence points away from price controls as the remedy.

First, the supply arithmetic is unfavourable. The reviewed literature estimates long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about HK$900 per month to incumbents while leaving 3,000–4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]. These are precisely the households the cap intends to protect [D1p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell, but listing supply contracted as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is explicit: "the tenants the cap protects are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3].

Second, the 2021 ordinance's stable stock is not evidence that a binding cap is safe. The Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but not initial rents, and excluded co-living SDUs [D1p3]. The stock grew from 100,800 to 111,200 units across 2019–2025 [D2p1], but the working paper is clear that this stability reflects a second-generation, non-binding instrument; first-generation controls on levels show "consistent contraction effects" [D6p2]. The think-tank argument that stable stock proves capacity for more control [D12p2] therefore misreads the evidence: it treats a non-binding instrument's null result as a licence for a binding one.

Third, a hard cap would be difficult to enforce where it matters most. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. More damningly, the subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]; enforcement would "systematically miss the most exploited tail" [D10p2]. Mrs Wong's nine years without a written agreement [D4p1] is not an anomaly but the enforcement gap in human form. A hard cap also invites judicial review and compensation claims if framed as deprivation of income [D9p1], and the litigation timeline alone would outlast a Council term [D9p3].

What, then, should be tightened? The archive points to tenure security and conditions. Mr Chan says the worst part is not price but insecurity—two weeks' notice after asking for a window repair [D3p1]—and he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years [D3p3]. The NGO survey finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. It supports tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, opposing a hard cap on the contraction arithmetic and tenants' own revealed preference [D11p3]. The legal opinion's middle instrument—a published, indexed per-square-foot band defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]—is compatible with this approach, provided it is paired with the register and inspection capacity the minute says is missing [D10p1].

The strongest objection is that a light band leaves the initial rent—"the actual pain point"—unregulated [D9p1]. That is true, and it is a real cost. But the archive's welfare arithmetic is uncomfortable precisely because the alternative imposes its costs on people who never appear in the consultation: the 3,000–4,000 households per year priced out of the segment [D6p4]. Tightening rent control on SDUs, in the form of a hard initial-rent cap, would help some incumbents and hurt more outsiders. Tightening tenancy security and habitability enforcement would help the population the policy actually names. Hong Kong should do the latter.
