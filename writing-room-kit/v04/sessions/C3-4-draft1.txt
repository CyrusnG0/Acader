# Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units: A Case for Targeted Tenure Security Over Hard Caps

Hong Kong's roughly 110,000 subdivided unit (SDU) households face a housing market that is cruel precisely because it is a residual one: tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent families, and public-housing waiters stranded in a queue averaging five and a half years [D1p2]. Any proposal to tighten rent control must therefore answer a hard question: does capping the initial rent protect these households, or does it shrink the stock they depend on? I argue that Hong Kong should **not** impose a hard cap on initial SDU rents, but should instead pursue a narrow tightening combining tenure security with a light, indexed rent band. The evidence points consistently to this conclusion.

First, the strongest argument against a hard cap is contraction. Standard theory predicts supply loss under binding ceilings, and the empirical literature on marginal housing estimates long-run supply elasticity of 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Modelled for a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while leaving 3,000–4,000 households per year — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households — unable to enter the segment at all [D6p4]. This is not a hypothetical. Berlin's Mietendeckel saw listing supply contract measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The tenants a hard cap protects are those already inside; the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Second, defenders of a hard cap point to the stable SDU stock under the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance — 100,800 units in 2019 rising to 111,200 by 2025 [D2p1]. But this stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies while leaving initial rents untouched and excluding co-living SDUs [D1p3]. The academic literature distinguishes exactly this: first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) show consistent contraction; second-generation controls (caps on increases) show small or null stock effects [D6p2]. New York's rent stabilisation, a second-generation scheme light at first-rent, shows only modest contraction [D8p1][D8p2]. The 2021 ordinance tells us little about what a binding cap would do.

Third, the enforcement problem is fatal to a hard cap in practice. Current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, whose landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1], and the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap that cannot be enforced against the worst landlords is a paper victory.

Fourth, tenants themselves do not prioritise rent levels. Mr Chan, facing a 5,800-dollar rent, says the worst part is insecurity — two weeks' notice after requesting a window repair — and names the waiting list, not rent, as what would help most [D3p1][D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The binding constraint is queue length, not price.

The counter-argument deserves weight: Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU, supports a hard cap and notes her stairwell landlords treat units as pensions and would not exit [D4p2]. The Landlords Association, representing 18,000 units, threatens exit if returns fall below mortgage cost — but concedes it has no data on actual margins, citing a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p1][D5p2][D5p3]. Its threat is credible in aggregate but unquantified. And a hard cap framed as deprivation of rental income would likely attract compensation claims and judicial review outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

The balanced path is the one the consultation appears to be floating: a floor-standard plus rent band package [D12p1], with initial rents capped by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. This addresses the actual pain point — initial rents — while avoiding the first-generation contraction trap. It must be paired with tenure security, since that is what tenants name, and with enforcement capacity redirected toward conditions where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2][D11p3].

Tightening is justified, but not as a hard cap. The evidence from elasticity modelling, Berlin, New York, and Hong Kong's own enforcement gap converges: protect incumbents through tenure security and a light band, and do not hand the bill to those still outside the door.
