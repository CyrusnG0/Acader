**Tighten Tenure, Not Rents: A Case Against a Hard Cap on SDU Rents**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) by imposing a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead pursue a narrow package of tenure security and a light, indexed rent band. The archive's evidence converges on a single conclusion: a hard cap would transfer modest gains to incumbent tenants while shrinking the stock of units available to the very households the policy is meant to protect — new arrivals, single-parent families, and those waiting for public housing [D1p2].

Start with the empirical record. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance was a second-generation instrument: it capped increases within an existing tenancy but left initial rents untouched [D1p3]. SDU stock did not contract; it grew from roughly 107,300 units in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this stability is not evidence that a binding cap would be equally benign. The academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases), which show small or null effects [D6p2]. The stable stock under the ordinance is precisely what one would expect from a non-binding instrument — not proof that capacity exists for more control.

The contraction arithmetic is uncomfortable and specific. The estimated long-run supply elasticity for SDU-type stock is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. In a modelled Hong Kong-like scenario, a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about HK$900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. The cap would protect those already inside and hand the bill to those still outside.

The comparative cases reinforce this. Berlin's Mietendeckel suppressed initial-offer rents but triggered measurable supply contraction as owners withheld or rerouted flats; when the law was struck down, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. New York's rent stabilisation, a second-generation scheme light at first-rent, has seen only modest stock contraction — but its 2019 tightening is associated with a fall in available stabilised listings as owners shifted units to other uses [D8p1][D8p3]. The lesson is consistent: caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents, and the cost surfaces elsewhere.

The enforcement reality makes a hard cap even less defensible. Current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. More damning, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals — so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

What do tenants themselves say? Mr Chan is split: a hard cap would free maybe HK$800 a month, but he fears landlords would rather convert cubicles back to storage than deal with regulated tenants — and he has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]. Asked what would help most, he does not name rent; he names the waiting list [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not price.

The landlords' threat of exit should be treated with caution — the Association concedes it has no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation that units are landlords' pension and they would not leave is plausible [D4p2]. But even a modest, credible exit risk is enough to make a hard cap the wrong instrument when the elasticity literature points to contraction [D6p1].

The alternative is already on the table: cap initial rent by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. Pair this with tenure security — written agreements, notice periods, and enforcement focused on conditions rather than price [D11p2]. The consultation document expected in 2026 appears to float exactly this "floor-standard + rent band" package [D12p1]. That is the right direction. A hard cap would help some incumbents and hurt the next cohort of the desperate. Hong Kong should tighten tenure, not rents.
