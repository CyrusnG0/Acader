Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only moderately. The best policy is to strengthen tenure security and add a light band on initial rents, and not to impose a hard cap on rent levels. This essay explains why tenants need more protection and why a hard cap would backfire.

Tenants in subdivided units are in a weak position. About 110,000 households live in these units, and although the median rent is about 30 percent lower than normal flats, tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium per square foot [D1p1]. Many of them are new arrivals, single parents or people waiting for public housing, and the wait is around five and a half years [D1p2]. For them the unit is not a real choice but the only option while they wait. The data also shows pressure. Rent per square foot rose 3.1 percent in 2021-2023 while general private rents rose only 1.7 percent, and the rent burden went from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. So tenants are being squeezed, which is a reason to act.

The main argument against tightening is that landlords will leave. The landlords association says a hard cap is confiscatory and that members will convert or empty units if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1] [D5p2]. This is a serious warning because every lost unit means someone has no home tonight. However, the evidence behind it is weak. The association admits it has no data on members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. So the threat is a claim from a group with a stake, and it is not proven.

The academic evidence is more useful. A working paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. It also separates hard caps on levels, which cause contraction, from caps on increases within tenancies, which have small or null effects [D6p2]. The 2021 ordinance is the second type, and stock kept growing from 107,300 to 111,200 units afterwards [D2p1]. I should be careful here, because the post-2023 figures are extrapolated from voluntary reporting [D2p3], so this proof is not perfect. Still, it suggests that moderate rules do not push landlords out, while a hard cap probably would. The paper also warns that the people who lose units are the very tenants the cap is meant to protect [D6p3].

Tenants themselves want something different from a cap. A survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would accept a rent increase in return for three years of secure tenure, and 74 percent would trade it for a faster public housing offer [D11p1]. The NGO report argues that caps treat the price symptom of a long queue, and that enforcement effort is better spent on fire and ventilation inspections [D11p2]. It supports a narrow tightening of tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, and it opposes a hard cap [D11p3]. This fits my view because it uses tenants' own preferences.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten, but in a careful way. Tenants pay a premium and have few options [D1p1] [D1p2], yet hard caps risk shrinking the stock of housing for the same people [D6p1] [D6p3]. A light band on initial rents with three-year tenure security protects tenants while keeping landlords in the market [D11p3]. The landlords' exit threat should be taken seriously but not accepted at face value given its weak data [D5p3]. The policy should also be reviewed with better data, because current figures are partly extrapolated [D2p3].
