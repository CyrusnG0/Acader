Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in a limited way. The government should keep limiting how much rent can rise inside a tenancy, and it should avoid a hard cap on the rent level. I use the data and the working paper I found in the course archive.

First, the current rules have not made landlords leave the market. The estimated number of subdivided units was about 100,800 in 2019, then 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. So the number kept growing after the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, which suggests that moderate regulation did not cause a visible exit of stock [D2p1]. This matters because a common worry is that any control will make owners stop renting these flats.

Second, tenants are still under pressure, so there is a reason to do more. Median rent per square foot rose 6.8 percent from 2019 to 2021, then 3.1 percent from 2021 to 2023 under the ordinance, while general private rent rose 2.4 and 1.7 percent [D2p2]. So the ordinance seems to have slowed rent growth, but subdivided units still rose faster than the rest of the market. The rent burden for these households also got worse, from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. This is my main reason for saying some tightening is justified.

Third, the type of control matters. The working paper says standard theory predicts less supply under a binding rent ceiling, but the size of the effect is an empirical question [D6p1]. For housing at the bottom of the market it estimates an elasticity of 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent rent cut from a cap removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of the stock over five years [D6p1]. That is a real cost, because people in these units have few other options.

The paper also separates two kinds of control. First-generation controls are hard caps on levels and show consistent shrinking of supply. Second-generation controls only cap increases within a tenancy and show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The paper says the 2021 Hong Kong ordinance is the second kind, which explains the stable stock in the data [D6p2]. I am not sure whether the stability comes from the type of control or just because the rules are mild. The two sources fit together but do not fully separate these explanations, and that is a limit of my evidence.

So my recommendation is careful. Hong Kong could tighten the second kind of control, for example by lowering the limit on increases, since this kind of rule seems to have small effects on supply [D6p2]. I would not move to a hard cap, because the paper links that to losing units [D6p1]. If the government tried something stronger, it should watch the stock numbers every year [D2p1].

I did not read the landlord association submission or the NGO report, so I do not know what landlords or tenants say about how the ordinance works in practice. I also only have an excerpt of the data. Still, the evidence supports a modest tightening of the tenancy-based approach, not a hard cap.
