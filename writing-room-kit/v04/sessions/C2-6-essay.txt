Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in a moderate way. A very hard cap could backfire, while doing nothing leaves tenants with little protection. In this essay I argue for a middle path, using the two sources I read from the course archive.

The first reason is that tenants have a lot to gain. Mr Chan, a tenant in Kwai Chung, says a hard cap would free up about 800 dollars a month, which he describes as food for a month [D3p2]. For a family living in a tiny subdivided unit, that is a big amount. It shows that rent is not just a number on paper. It decides whether people can eat properly. If the government does nothing, tenants like him keep paying high rents with no real protection, so tightening the rules has a clear human benefit.

The second point is the risk. Mr Chan is actually split on the idea. He worries that landlords would rather turn cubicles back into storage rooms than deal with regulated tenants, and he has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]. This is only one person's story, so I cannot treat it as proof of what happens across the city. But it is a fair warning that rules can change how landlords behave, and a policy that makes units disappear would hurt the very people it wants to help. I think this is the strongest argument against a hard cap.

That is why the data matters. The stock index shows that the number of subdivided units went from about 100,800 in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, then 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. The source says growth continued through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, which suggests that moderate regulation did not cause a visible exit of units [D2p1]. This is useful because it speaks directly to the fear that Mr Chan has. If landlords were going to pull out of the market because of regulation, we would expect the stock to fall after 2021, and it did not.

Putting these together, my position is that moderate tightening is a reasonable choice. The tenant interview shows the benefit of lower rent and also the fear of lost units [D3p2]. The stock data shows that the earlier, moderate rules did not lead to a drop in units [D2p1]. So a step that is similar in size to the 2021 rules seems safe enough to try, and it could bring real relief to people who are struggling.

I should be honest about the limits of what I found. I only read two short passages, and the data excerpt only covers up to 2025. It also cannot tell me whether a stricter rule than 2021 would cause a different result. Because of that, I would not support a very hard cap right now. I would support a gradual change, with the government watching the stock numbers each year and stopping if units start to fall. That way tenants get more protection, and the risk that Mr Chan described can be checked with real numbers instead of guesses.
