**Tighten Tenure, Not Just Rent: A Qualified Yes on SDU Regulation**

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units — but not with the hard initial-rent cap that dominates public debate. The evidence in this archive points to a narrower, better-targeted reform: a light, band-based cap on initial rents paired with enforceable security of tenure. A hard cap would help some incumbents while shrinking entry for the very households the policy names as its beneficiaries.

The case for tightening at all rests on a real and worsening harm. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats precisely because they are desperate [D1p1], and real rent burden rose from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. Mrs Wong's account — nine years, no written tenancy, rent raised "by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1] — shows the existing second-generation instrument leaving the actual pain point untouched. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within a tenancy but not the initial rent [D1p3], and the legal opinion confirms this design was chosen partly for judicial safety: a cap framed as contractual-term regulation "sits on firmer ground but leaves the initial rent — the actual pain point — unregulated" [D9p1]. Doing nothing leaves that gap intact.

But the mechanism matters more than the direction. The academic literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock at 0.31–0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is stark — a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while 3,000–4,000 households per year, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households," cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. That is the same population D1 identifies as the segment's core tenants [D1p2]. Berlin reinforces the point: caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents, and "the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3].

The stability of SDU stock under the 2021 ordinance — 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1] — is not evidence that a binding cap is safe. It is evidence that a non-binding instrument does not bind. The working paper is explicit: first-generation hard caps show consistent contraction; second-generation increase caps show small or null effects, "which explains the stable stock in the D2 index" [D6p2]. One think-tank piece cited in the news digest makes exactly the wrong inference from this data [D12p2].

Tenure security, by contrast, addresses the harm tenants actually name. Mr Chan says the worst part is not price but insecurity — a landlord ending his tenancy with two weeks' notice [D3p1] — and that he would "accept any rent to leave" if the public-housing queue fell from five and a half years to three [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]. Mrs Wong's own building shows landlords do sometimes exit — two flats converted to storage [D4p3] — but the Landlords Association's threat of mass exit is weakly evidenced: it concedes no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3].

Enforcement is the decisive practical constraint. Current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a certifiable tenancy register would take an estimated 40–60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. Worse, the subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design that protects the newest-arrival tail first rather than last [D10p3], because the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement [D10p2]. A hard cap that is unenforceable against the most exploited tail, while deterring new supply at the margin, fails on its own terms.

The defensible package is therefore the one the consultation is reportedly floating: a floor-standard plus indexed rent band [D12p1], defended legally as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], paired with mandatory written tenancies and security of tenure. This captures the incumbent protection that New York's second-generation model delivers with only modest stock contraction [D8p2], while avoiding the entry losses that Berlin and the elasticity literature warn about [D6p4][D7p3]. It also redirects scarce enforcement capacity toward conditions — fire, partition, ventilation — where the NGO report notes harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

The honest limitation is that none of this fixes the underlying quantity problem. The segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2], and no rent instrument shortens a five-and-a-half-year wait. Tightening rent control is worth doing — but as tenure reform with a light price band, not as the hard cap that would protect today's tenants by closing the door on tomorrow's.
