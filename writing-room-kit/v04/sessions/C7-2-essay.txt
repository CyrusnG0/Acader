**Tighten the Tenancy, Not the Rent: A Case Against a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on initial rents in subdivided units (SDUs). The better path is a narrow tightening—stronger tenure security plus a light, indexed rent band—because the evidence in this archive shows that a binding first-generation cap would shrink the very stock the poorest tenants depend on, while doing little for the newest arrivals it claims to protect.

The strongest argument for a hard cap is distributive: SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. A cap would transfer real money to incumbents—roughly HK$900 per month under the modelled 15 percent cut [D6p4]. That is not trivial; for Mr Chan it is "food for a month" [D3p2].

But the same model prices the cost: 3,000–4,000 households per year would be unable to enter the segment, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]—precisely the population the policy intends to help [D1p2]. The reviewed literature puts long-run supply elasticity for marginal stock at 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The welfare arithmetic is therefore regressive at the entry margin: incumbents gain, outsiders lose [D6p3].

The counterargument—that the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance proves regulation is safe [D2p1]—rests on a category error. The 2021 instrument was second-generation: it capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents untouched [D1p3], and the academic literature finds second-generation controls show "small or null effects on stock," while first-generation hard caps show "consistent contraction effects" [D6p2]. The visible stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument, exactly as one think-tank piece warns [D12p2]. A binding cap would behave differently.

Berlin is the cautionary case. Its 2020 Mietendeckel capped initial offers, listings contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the court struck the law down, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The lesson is structural, not legal: "the tenants the cap protects are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3].

Enforcement compounds the problem. Current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. More damning, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement—like Mrs Wong, who has never had one and says the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]—or the newest arrivals. Enforcement would "systematically miss the most exploited tail" [D10p2], and the subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

The revealed preferences of tenants themselves point elsewhere. In the NGO survey, 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, "does not name rent. He names the waiting list" [D3p3]. The binding constraint is queue length, not price: the segment is tight because the queue is long, and every enforcement dollar spent policing rents is a dollar not spent on fire, partition and ventilation harms that are "documented and immediate" [D11p2].

Mrs Wong's confidence that landlords "cannot exit" because units are their pension [D4p2] is contradicted by her own observation of two flats converted to storage [D4p3]—and by the landlords' commitment to exit below mortgage cost [D5p2], however self-interested and thinly evidenced [D5p3]. Even the legal route is fraught: a hard cap framed as deprivation invites compensation claims and judicial review that "would outlast a Council term" [D9p1][D9p3].

The proportionate answer is the middle instrument counsel describes: tenure security plus an indexed per-square-foot band on initial rents, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], paired with the certification infrastructure the band requires [D10p1]. This captures much of the incumbent gain while avoiding the entry-margin loss that a hard cap guarantees. The government's expected "floor-standard + rent band" package, with Berlin cited as caution [D12p1], is the right instinct. Tighten the tenancy, not the rent.
