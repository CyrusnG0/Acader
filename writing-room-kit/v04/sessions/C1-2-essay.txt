Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in a narrow way. It should add real security of tenure and, at most, a light band on initial rents. It should not bring in a hard cap on initial rents. My reason is that the people a hard cap is meant to help are the same people who lose the most when units leave the market, and the evidence suggests that tenants care more about security than about the rent level.

First, the problem is real. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they pay 15 to 25 percent more per square foot than tenants in ordinary flats [D1p1]. These tenants are mostly new arrivals, single parents and people waiting for public housing, an average wait of about five and a half years [D1p2]. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance only capped rent increases inside an existing tenancy and did not cap the starting rent [D1p3]. After it, the stock of units kept growing [D2p1]. Yet the rent burden still rose from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. So mild regulation did not make landlords leave, but it did not fix affordability either.

The question is how far to go, and here the evidence warns against a hard cap. In Berlin, a cap on about 1.5 million flats cut asking rents, but listings then shrank within a year because owners held flats back or moved them to uncontrolled segments [D7p1]. The lesson in that note is that the tenants a cap protects are those already inside, and the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3]. The academic working paper makes the same point in theory. It separates hard caps on rent levels from caps on increases within tenancies. Hard caps show consistent contraction of supply, while caps on increases show small or no effect, which fits Hong Kong's stable stock after 2021 [D6p2]. It estimates that a 10 percent fall in rent removes about 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. For a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut would give incumbents about 900 dollars a month, while 3,000 to 4,000 households a year could not get into the segment [D6p4].

The strongest objection comes from tenants who need relief now. Mrs Wong, who has lived in a unit for nine years, supports a hard cap and believes landlords will never leave because the units are their pension [D4p2]. Her view deserves respect, since she never received a written tenancy agreement and the ordinance changed nothing she could see [D4p1]. The landlords' association argues the opposite, that a hard cap is confiscatory and members will exit [D5p2]. I think both sides are only partly right. The landlords' evidence is weak: they admit they have no data on members' actual margins, and their cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. But their warning matches what Berlin and the model show for hard caps. Mrs Wong's own account also cuts against her, because the interviewers note that two flats in her stairwell were turned into storage after the ordinance, even though she believes landlords cannot leave [D4p3].

My reply to the objection is that relief now does not need a hard cap. Mr Chan says the worst part of his life in a 120 square foot cubicle is not price but insecurity, because his landlord ended an earlier tenancy on two weeks notice after he asked to fix a leaking window [D3p1]. A survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would accept a rent rise in return for three years of security of tenure [D11p1]. That report supports a narrow tightening of tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, and it opposes a hard cap [D11p3]. It also argues that enforcement capacity is limited, and that inspectors are better used on fire and ventilation than on policing rents [D11p2]. A rule that cannot be enforced helps nobody.

My claim has limits. Tenure security does not shorten the queue, and Mr Chan says that is what he really wants, as he would accept almost any rent if the wait fell from five and a half years to three [D3p3]. My sources also do not test a light band on initial rents, because the paper only compares hard caps with caps on increases [D6p2]. I think a smaller cut should cost less supply, since losses grow with the size of the rent cut [D6p1], but that is my own inference and not a finding. Finally, the stock index may hide small exits, because its figures after 2023 rely on survey extrapolation [D2p3]. For those reasons I would tighten step by step, measure the stock each year, and stop if units start to disappear.
