# Regulate the Tenancy, Not the Price: A Case Against Tightening Rent Control on Hong Kong's Subdivided Units

The question of whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) is not a question about whether tenants deserve relief. They plainly do. It is a question about which instrument delivers relief to the households who need it most, without shrinking the stock those households depend on. On the evidence in this archive, a hard cap on initial rents fails that test. The better answer is a narrow tightening — tenure security plus a light, indexed band on initial rents — paired with enforcement that reaches the most exploited tail.

The strongest argument for a hard cap is that the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance left the actual pain point untouched. It capped increases within an existing tenancy but not the initial rent level [D1p3], and Mrs Wong, nine years in her SDU with no written agreement, reports that "the ordinance changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. Mr Chan's landlord ended his tenancy on two weeks' notice when he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. These are real harms, and they are not primarily price harms. They are security harms.

The contraction risk is not hypothetical. The academic literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for SDU-type marginal stock at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The same paper's model of a 15 percent initial-rent cut yields a net transfer of about $900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]. Those are precisely the populations named in D1 as the SDU's core residents [D1p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The bill is handed to those still outside.

Defenders of tightening point to the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance — 100,800 units in 2019 rising to 111,200 by 2025 [D2p1] — as proof that regulation does not drive exit. But the working paper explains why: first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) show consistent contraction, while second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies) show small or null effects [D6p2]. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which is exactly why the stock held. The visible stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument, not evidence that a binding one would behave the same way [D12p2]. Mrs Wong's confidence that "none of whom would leave the business" [D4p2] sits awkwardly beside her own report of two flats in her stairwell converted to non-residential storage after the ordinance [D4p3] — a contradiction the interviewer notes she does not see.

The Landlords Association's threat of exit [D5p2] should be discounted, but not dismissed. Its own submission concedes no data on actual margins and cites a 2022 survey with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported [D5p3]. That is weak evidence for confiscation. Yet the elasticity literature does not depend on landlord rhetoric; it measures observed behavior [D6p1]. A 3 to 5 percent stock loss over five years is small in percentage terms and devastating in human terms if you are one of the 3,000 to 4,000 households locked out each year [D6p4].

What, then, should be done? The NGO report's survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan himself, offered a hard cap, names the waiting list rather than rent: "If the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave" [D3p3]. The legal opinion offers a defensible middle path: cap initial rent by reference to a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, framed as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. The internal minute warns that enforcement capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and that the newest-arrival tail is systematically missed because the tenants least able to complain have no written agreement [D10p2] [D10p3]. That is an argument for building the register and the inspection regime first — not for abandoning regulation, and not for a hard cap that outruns the state's capacity to enforce it fairly.

The honest position is this: tighten, but tighten the tenancy, not the price. A hard initial-rent cap buys incumbents a modest monthly transfer and charges it to the households still outside the door. Tenure security, a light indexed band, and enforcement aimed at the most exploited tail deliver relief to the people the policy is meant to protect — without asking them to win a lottery of who gets a unit before the stock shrinks.
