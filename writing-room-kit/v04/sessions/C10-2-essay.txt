Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

My answer is yes, but only in a narrow way. Hong Kong should extend and enforce the cap on rent increases inside a tenancy, pair it with tenure security, and fund the inspection system that makes it real. It should not introduce a hard cap on initial rents. When I started, I thought any tighter control would help poor tenants. The evidence in the archive changed that, and I now think the type of control matters more than how strict it is.

First, some background. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they are mostly new arrivals, single-parent families and people waiting for public housing, where the wait is around five and a half years [D1p2]. For these people the unit is not a choice but a leftover option while they wait. They also pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot compared with ordinary flats [D1p1]. So there is a real problem of weak bargaining power. Hong Kong already has one tool, the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance, which caps increases within a tenancy but not the starting rent [D1p3].

My first reason is that increase caps do not seem to damage supply. The estimated stock of subdivided units grew from 100,800 in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, including after the 2021 ordinance [D2p1]. The working paper explains why: first-generation controls, which cap rent levels, consistently reduce stock, while second-generation controls, which cap increases, show small or null effects [D6p2]. This was the thing I worried about most at the start, because if landlords leave, tenants lose homes and my position would fail. For increase caps, the data does not show that.

My second reason is that hard caps on starting rents hurt the same people they are meant to help. The paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1], and it models a 15 percent cut in initial rents as giving incumbents about 900 dollars a month while 3,000 to 4,000 households a year cannot enter the segment [D6p4]. Berlin points the same way. After its 2020 cap, listings fell as owners withheld or moved flats to other segments, and when the law was struck down rents jumped above the earlier trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The lesson is that caps protect those already inside and the bill goes to those outside [D7p3].

The strongest objection comes from the NGO report. It surveyed 440 households and found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of tenure security [D11p1]. It argues that a cap treats the price symptom of a quantity problem, because the queue is long, and that enforcement spent on rents is not spent on fire and ventilation inspections [D11p2]. I take this seriously, and it is why I add tenure security to my proposal. But I do not think it defeats the increase cap. The survey shows tenants value security, not that they want no limit on rent. A three-year tenancy means little if the landlord can raise the rent until the tenant leaves. The report itself supports a narrow tightening with tenure security and a light band on initial rents [D11p3], so even the NGO accepts some price limit.

The landlords make the opposite objection. They call a hard cap confiscatory and promise to exit if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1][D5p2]. I think this threat is plausible for hard caps, given the estimates above, but it is weak as evidence. The association admits it has no data on members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. It also says nothing about increase caps, under which stock kept growing.

The weakest part of my position is enforcement. Inspection covers under 6 percent of stock, and a certifiable tenancy register would need 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain are those without written agreements and the newest arrivals, so enforcement would miss the most exploited group [D10p2]. The committee could not find a design that protects them first rather than last [D10p3]. Data is also thin: reporting of new tenancy terms is voluntary below the ordinance threshold, so recent figures are extrapolated [D2p3]. And rent burden still rose from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the ordinance [D2p2]. I cannot claim an increase cap solves affordability. The queue for public housing is the deeper cause.

So my conclusion is a limited one. Tightening should mean bringing more units under the increase cap, including those left out in 2021, funding the register and inspections, and giving tenants real security. A hard cap on initial rents is not worth the supply risk. This will not fully protect new arrivals, and I admit that, but it improves the position of people already inside without evidence of shrinking the stock.
