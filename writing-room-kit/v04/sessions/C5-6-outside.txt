# Tighten, but Not the Way the Debate Suggests

The question "should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units?" invites a yes-or-no answer that the evidence does not support. A hard cap on initial rents would likely shrink the stock that houses roughly 110,000 households, while doing nothing would leave tenants exposed to the very insecurity they name as their worst condition. The defensible position is a targeted tightening—tenure security plus a light, indexed band on initial rents—rather than the hard cap that dominates public debate.

**The case against a hard cap is strong.** The academic literature estimates the long-run supply elasticity of marginal housing at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Modelled for a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Mr Chan's fear that landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants [D3p2] is not anecdotal noise; Berlin's Mietendeckel shows listing supply contracting within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The bill for protecting insiders is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

**But the case for doing nothing is weaker still.** Mrs Wong has lived nine years in an SDU with no written tenancy agreement, and reports the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. The Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents—the actual pain point—unregulated [D1p3][D9p1]. Real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income even under the ordinance [D2p2]. And the stable stock cited by those who want more control [D2p1] is precisely what theory predicts from a non-binding, second-generation instrument: caps on increases show small or null stock effects, while caps on levels show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The stability is an artifact, not a licence.

**The decisive evidence is what tenants themselves say.** In the NGO survey, 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, does not name rent—he names the waiting list [D3p3]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not price [D11p1]. This reframes the policy: rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2].

**The enforcement evidence points the same way.** Current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement or the newest arrivals [D10p2]—the very population named as the policy's intended beneficiary [D1p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

**My position:** tighten, but narrowly. Adopt the "floor-standard + rent band" package already signalled in the consultation [D12p1]: a published, indexed per-square-foot band on initial rents, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], paired with mandatory written tenancies and security of tenure. This captures the incumbent gains without the contraction arithmetic of a hard cap [D6p4], and it redirects enforcement toward conditions—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. Crucially, it must be paired with a credible public-housing supply commitment, because every tenant interview in this archive says the same thing: the queue, not the rent, is the real cage [D3p3][D11p1].

A hard cap would protect today's tenants by shrinking tomorrow's supply. Doing nothing protects no one. The middle instrument is not a compromise for its own sake—it is what the elasticity data, the Berlin precedent, and the tenants' own stated preferences independently recommend.
