**Tighten Tenure, Not Rents: A Qualified No to a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on initial rents in subdivided units. It should instead tighten regulation along the dimension the evidence identifies as the actual harm: tenancy insecurity. The case against a hard cap rests on three findings that recur across the archive: first-generation controls contract marginal stock [D6p2]; the households priced out are the same population the cap is meant to protect [D6p3]; and tenants themselves rank rent below queue length and security of tenure [D3p3][D11p1]. A cap would purchase a visible transfer for incumbents at the cost of invisible exclusion for entrants, and it would consume the enforcement capacity that conditions inspection urgently needs [D10p1][D11p2].

Start with what a cap actually does. The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity in marginal stock at 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while 3,000 to 4,000 households a year — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households — cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. That is not a hypothetical harm. The SDU population is already disproportionately new arrivals and households waiting five and a half years for public housing [D1p2]; they are the residual users of a residual market. A cap that shrinks the residual market does not house them; it queues them elsewhere.

The counter-argument from the 2021 experience deserves a fair hearing and then a precise rebuttal. Stock rose from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, continuing through the ordinance, which suggests moderate regulation did not trigger visible exit [D2p1]. Mrs Wong makes the intuitive version of this case: her building's four SDU landlords would not leave, because units are their pension [D4p2]. But the ordinance was second-generation — it capped increases within an existing tenancy and left the initial rent untouched [D1p3], and the academic paper is explicit that second-generation controls show small or null stock effects precisely because they do not bind on first rent [D6p2]. The stable stock is evidence that a non-binding instrument does not contract supply; it is not evidence that a binding one would not. The think-tank dispute in the news digest captures this exactly: one side reads stability as room for more control, the other as an artifact of non-bindingness [D12p2]. The elasticity literature, the Berlin case and the New York case all point the same way. Berlin's cap cut new-listing offers, then supply contracted as owners withheld or rerouted flats [D7p1]; when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p2]. New York's stabilisation avoids heavy contraction only because it is light at first rent — the same point Hong Kong's ordinance left untouched [D8p2]. The lesson is consistent: caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents, and the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

The enforcement argument compounds the economic one. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain are those without written agreements [D4p1] and the newest arrivals, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. Meanwhile the NGO report is blunt that every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire, partition and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

So what should be done? The revealed preferences are unambiguous: 61 percent of surveyed tenants would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan does not name rent when asked what would help most; he names the waiting list [D3p3]. Mrs Wong's real grievance is not the rent level but that she has never had a written agreement and the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. The policy package should therefore be tenure security — mandatory written tenancies, minimum notice periods, protection against retaliatory eviction — plus a light, indexed band on initial rents of the kind counsel judges defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], and a serious acceleration of public-housing supply. The legal opinion warns that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income would attract compensation claims and judicial review that would outlast a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]; a tenure-and-band instrument avoids that trap while targeting the documented harm.

This is a qualified no, not a defence of the status quo. The consultation's expected "floor-standard plus rent band" package is close to right [D12p1], provided the band is genuinely enforced and the tenure provisions are not diluted. What must be rejected is the hard initial-rent cap, because it transfers money to today's tenants by removing tomorrow's units, and because the evidence says the deepest harm in this segment is not the price of a cubicle but the insecurity of living in one.
