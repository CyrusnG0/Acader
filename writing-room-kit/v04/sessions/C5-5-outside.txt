**Tighten Tenure, Not Rents: A Qualified No to a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) by imposing a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead tighten *tenure security* and condition standards, paired with a light, indexed band on initial rents. The archive supports this position on three grounds: a hard cap would likely shrink the stock that houses the most desperate; it would systematically fail the tenants it aims to protect; and the binding constraint tenants themselves name is not price but the public-housing queue.

Start with the supply arithmetic. Standard theory predicts contraction under binding ceilings, and the empirical elasticity for marginal housing is estimated at 0.31–0.48 — a 10 percent cap-induced cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The paper's crucial distinction is between first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, and second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies), which show small or null effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which plausibly explains the stable stock in the index — 100,800 units in 2019 rising to 111,200 by 2025 [D2p1]. But that stability is evidence that a *non-binding* instrument did not bite, not that a binding one would be safe. As one think-tank piece notes, the visible stability may be an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and a binding one behaves differently [D12p2]. The modelled tightening scenario is sobering: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while 3,000–4,000 households per year — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households — cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. Berlin is the cautionary case: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law was struck down, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The bill for protecting insiders is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Second, enforcement would miss the most exploited tail. The Housing Authority subcommittee's own modelling records that if landlords reprice above-band initial rents, the first instrument tenants hold is complaint — but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, who has never had one in nine years [D4p1], or the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. Current inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Post-2023 data already rely on voluntary reporting below the ordinance threshold [D2p3]. A hard cap would thus be announced loudly and enforced thinly — precisely where harm is greatest.

Third, tenants themselves do not name rent as the binding constraint. Mr Chan, paying about a third of household income, says the worst part is insecurity — a two-week notice period — and that if the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p1][D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem: the segment is tight because the queue is long, and every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is one not spent inspecting fire, partition and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

The counterarguments deserve their due. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap and argues her building's four SDU landlords would not exit because units are their pension [D4p2]. But she also reports two flats in her stairwell converted to non-residential storage after the ordinance — a contradiction she does not see [D4p3]. The Landlords Association claims thin margins and threatens exit, but concedes it has no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures rest on a 210-member survey with a 34 percent self-reported response rate [D5p2][D5p3]. That weakness cuts both ways: it undermines the exit threat, but also means we cannot confidently calibrate a cap. The legal opinion reinforces caution: a hard cap framed as deprivation of income would attract compensation claims and judicial review, with a litigation timeline outlasting a Council term; a middle instrument — a published, indexed per-square-foot band — is defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation, but requires survey infrastructure that does not exist [D9p1][D9p2][D9p3].

The workable package is therefore the one the consultation appears to be moving toward: a floor-standard plus rent band rather than a hard cap [D12p1], combined with the tenure security the evidence actually demands. A light band on initial rents addresses the per-square-foot premium tenants pay [D1p1] without triggering the contraction arithmetic; mandatory written tenancies and notice periods address Mr Chan's real grievance [D3p1]; and enforcement resources go to conditions and to shortening the queue, which is the quantity problem underneath the price [D11p2]. Tightening tenure, not rents, is the defensible answer — and the one least likely to hand the bill to the households still outside.
