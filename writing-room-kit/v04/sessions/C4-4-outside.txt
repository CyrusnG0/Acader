**Tighten Tenure, Not Rent: A Qualified No to a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on initial rents in subdivided units. It should instead tighten regulation along the dimension tenants themselves name as binding — security of tenure — paired with a light, indexed band on initial rents and a serious enforcement build-out. The archive supports this position on three grounds: the contraction arithmetic of first-generation controls, the revealed preferences of tenants, and the enforcement impossibility of protecting the newest-arrival tail under a hard cap.

The strongest argument against a hard cap is that it targets price while the real constraint is quantity. The segment is tight because the public-housing queue is long — averaging around five and a half years — and the SDU is a residual option while the queue moves [D1p2]. Mr Chan makes this explicit: asked what would help most, he does not name rent but the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households corroborates this at scale: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. A policy that suppresses price while leaving the queue and tenure insecurity untouched treats the symptom, not the disease.

Second, the supply evidence cuts against a binding cap. The stable SDU stock through the 2021 ordinance — 100,800 units in 2019 rising to 111,200 by 2025 [D2p1] — is real, but the working paper explains why it is not evidence that a harder instrument is safe. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation: it caps increases within tenancies but leaves initial rents unregulated [D1p3][D6p2]. Second-generation controls show small or null stock effects; first-generation controls on levels show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The reviewed elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The paper's Hong Kong-like model is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while pricing 3,000 to 4,000 households per year out of the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin confirms the mechanism — caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Third, a hard cap cannot be enforced where it is needed most. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. More damning, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement — like Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU with no tenancy document [D4p1] — and the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2], and the subcommittee could not identify any design under which that tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. Mrs Wong's confidence that landlords cannot exit is contradicted by her own report of two flats converted to storage [D4p3], and the Association's exit threat, however thin its evidence [D5p2][D5p3], is credible at the margin where returns approach mortgage cost.

This is not an argument for doing nothing. The 2021 ordinance left the actual pain point — the initial rent — unregulated [D1p3][D9p1], and real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. A published, indexed per-square-foot band on initial rents is legally defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], and the consultation appears to be moving toward exactly this "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap [D12p1]. Paired with enforceable tenure security — written agreements, notice periods, a complaints channel that reaches tenants without documents — this addresses what tenants say they need [D11p3] while avoiding the contraction arithmetic [D6p4].

The honest caveat is that a band without enforcement is theatre, and enforcement infrastructure does not currently exist [D9p2][D10p1]. That is an argument for sequencing: build the register and inspection capacity first, legislate the band with it, and treat faster rehousing as the primary instrument. A hard cap would deliver visible relief to incumbents while quietly shrinking the stock that houses the people the policy claims to protect. Hong Kong should tighten tenure, not rent.
