# Tighten Tenancy Security, Not the Initial Rent

Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on initial rents in subdivided units. It should instead tighten the existing tenancy-control regime—extending security of tenure and adding a light, indexed band on initial rents—while redirecting enforcement capacity toward the queue and toward conditions. The archive supports this position on three grounds: the economics of first-generation controls, the revealed preferences of tenants themselves, and the enforcement arithmetic that decides who actually gets protected.

The strongest argument against a hard cap is that it attacks the wrong margin. Standard theory predicts supply contraction under binding rent ceilings, but the relevant question is empirical, and the evidence is specific: the long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply to controlled rents is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Critically, the literature distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies): the former show consistent contraction, the latter small or null effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is second-generation, and the stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 through the ordinance period [D2p1]—stability that the working paper explains precisely by the instrument's non-binding character [D6p2]. A hard cap would move Hong Kong into the first-generation category, where the contraction evidence is consistent, not speculative.

The welfare arithmetic is uncomfortable and should be faced directly. In the modelled tightened-cap scenario, a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents—but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those excluded are the same population the cap intends to protect [D6p3]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell sharply, but listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law was struck down the suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is explicit—the cap protects those already inside and hands the bill to those still outside [D7p3].

The most striking evidence, however, comes from tenants. Mr Chan, a delivery driver paying 5,800 dollars for a 120 sq ft cubicle, is split on a hard cap: it would free maybe 800 dollars a month, but he fears landlords would rather convert units back to storage, and he has already seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p1][D3p2]. Asked what would help most, he does not name rent—he names the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households confirms this at scale: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not price.

Mrs Wong's case cuts the other way, and honesty requires engaging it. She has lived in an SDU for nine years, has never received a written tenancy agreement, and says the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. She supports a hard cap, arguing her building's four SDU landlords would not exit because "where else would the money come from?" [D4p2]. But the interviewer notes a contradiction she does not see: two flats in her stairwell were converted to non-residential storage after the ordinance, even as she believes landlords cannot exit [D4p3]. Her lived insecurity is real and is exactly what a tenure-security tightening would address; her confidence about landlord behavior is contradicted by her own building.

Enforcement is the decisive practical objection. Current inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap requires a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, if landlords reprice above-band rents, the first instrument tenants hold is complaint—but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would therefore be strongest on paper and weakest exactly where harm is greatest.

The legal route reinforces the same conclusion. A hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law, while contractual-term regulation sits on firmer ground but leaves initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated [D9p1]. Counsel's middle instrument, a published per-square-foot band that regulators can defend as rate-capping rather than confiscation, is both legally safer and consistent with the NGO's preferred narrow tightening [D9p2][D11p3].

The counter-argument—that the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance shows capacity for more control [D12p2]—is answered by the elasticity literature itself: that stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and a binding one behaves differently [D6p2]. The policy package now expected—"floor-standard + rent band" rather than a hard cap, citing Berlin as a cautionary tale [D12p1]—is the right direction.

Hong Kong should tighten tenure security and add a defensible, indexed initial-rent band, paired with genuine queue reduction and condition enforcement. A hard cap would transfer income to today's incumbents while shrinking the stock, missing the newest arrivals, and consuming enforcement capacity that conditions inspection needs more. The tenants' own answers—queue and security—should set the policy.
