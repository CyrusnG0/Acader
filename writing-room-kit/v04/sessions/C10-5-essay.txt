Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but not in the way most people first imagine. My view is that the law should make tenants harder to push out and should limit rent rises inside a tenancy more firmly. It should not impose a hard cap on starting rents. I began the essay thinking a stronger rent cap was the answer. The evidence in the course archive changed the target of my argument, though not its direction.

The case for doing something is clear. The 2021 ordinance limits rises inside a tenancy. Under it, rents for these units still rose 3.1 percent in 2021 to 2023, while general private rents rose only 1.7 percent, and the share of income that households spend on rent went from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. So the current rule has not protected tenants enough. The problem is also about security more than price. Mr Chan, a delivery driver living with his family in a 120 square foot cubicle, says the worst part is insecurity. His landlord ended his previous tenancy with two weeks notice after he asked for a leaking window to be repaired [D3p1]. A survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would accept a nominal rent increase in return for three years of security, and 74 percent would trade for a faster public housing offer [D11p1]. Tenants are telling us that being pushed out is the thing they fear.

The strongest objection is that rent control shrinks the supply of the cheapest housing. A working paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The households who lose units are the same people the cap is supposed to help [D6p3]. Landlords say they would leave the market if returns fell below their mortgage cost [D5p2]. New York shows a second kind of cost. After its rules were tightened in 2019, available stabilised listings fell and owners had less reason to maintain units [D8p3]. There is also a legal problem. Counsel thinks a hard cap on initial rents would almost certainly bring compensation claims under the Basic Law, while rules on tenancy terms are on firmer ground [D9p1].

I accept most of this, and it is why I do not support a hard cap. But the objection does not defeat the narrower tightening I support. The same working paper separates caps on rent levels from caps on increases within tenancies. It finds consistent contraction for the first and small or null effects on stock for the second [D6p2]. Hong Kong's own data fits this: the stock of these units grew from 100,800 in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021 and 111,200 in 2025, through the ordinance [D2p1]. New York, which has regulated rises within tenancies for decades, has seen tenants stay longer and only modest stock contraction, because it is light on first rents [D8p2]. Longer stays and fewer forced moves are what I count as tenants gaining.

I should be fair about the landlords. Their association says its members will leave, but it admits it has no data on members' actual margins, and its compliance cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. That makes the threat weaker evidence than it sounds. I still treat the risk as real, because the units that disappear house someone tonight [D5p2], so my proposal keeps the burden on landlords modest.

There is also a limit to what my own proposal can do. Mr Chan says that if the queue for public housing were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent in order to leave [D3p3]. Rent rules cannot fix a queue. The data also has a weakness: figures after 2023 depend on survey extrapolation because reporting of new tenancies is voluntary below the ordinance threshold [D2p3]. So the first step should be to extend the rule to small tenancies and gather proper data.

My recommendation is therefore a staged one. First, strengthen tenure security and lower the permitted rise within tenancies. Second, build the survey data. Only then consider a published, indexed per-square-foot band for starting rents, which counsel thinks could be defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation, but which needs survey infrastructure that does not yet exist [D9p2]. This tightens the law where tenants are hurt most, and avoids the supply loss and legal risk that come with a hard cap.
