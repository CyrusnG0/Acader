Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but carefully. My main reason is that the people living in these flats have very little choice, and the numbers show they are still being squeezed.

First, who lives in subdivided units? About 110,000 households do, and the flats are carved into small cubicles, often with shared kitchens and bathrooms [D1p1]. Even though the median rent is about 30 percent lower than a normal private flat, tenants actually pay 15 to 25 percent more per square foot [D1p1]. So the cheap headline rent hides a bad deal. On top of that, the tenants are mostly new arrivals, single-parent households and people waiting for public housing, and the average wait is around five and a half years [D1p2]. For them a subdivided unit is not a real choice. It is what they have while they wait in the queue. People with no alternatives cannot bargain with a landlord, which is the usual reason to have rent rules at all.

Second, the current rules have not stopped rents from rising quickly. Rent per square foot in these units went up 6.8 percent in 2019 to 2021 and another 3.1 percent in 2021 to 2023, while general private rent grew only 2.4 and 1.7 percent in the same periods [D2p2]. The 2021 ordinance seems to have slowed the rise, but subdivided rents still grew faster than the rest of the market. Also, the share of income spent on rent got worse for these households, going from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. That means tenants who are already poor are losing more of their money to rent.

The usual argument against stricter control is that landlords will leave the market and the supply of units will shrink. The data I read does not show that so far. The estimated stock went from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, and it kept growing through the 2021 ordinance [D2p1]. I should be honest that this does not prove the rules were harmless. The stock could have grown for other reasons, such as high demand from people stuck in the queue, and I only read a short excerpt. A tighter rule than the 2021 one might also act differently from a moderate one. So I am not saying the numbers prove safety. I am saying they give no sign yet that moderate control caused landlords to leave.

Because of this, I think the sensible step is a measured tightening, for example a limit on how much rent can rise between tenancies, and not a hard cap that is very strict. The reasoning is simple. Tenants have few options, they pay a premium for their space, and their rent burden is getting heavier. At the same time, the supply risk is real but not visible in the evidence I have. A moderate change lets the government check the stock figures each year and step back if units start to disappear.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units in a moderate way. The tenants are stuck and paying more than they should [D1p1][D1p2], and their burden is rising faster than the wider market [D2p2], while the stock has kept growing under the current rules [D2p1].
