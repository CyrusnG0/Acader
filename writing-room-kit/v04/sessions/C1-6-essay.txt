Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? My answer is yes, but only in a narrow way. Hong Kong should not set a hard cap on initial rents. It should tighten tenure security and, at most, add a loose band on initial rents. A hard cap would probably hurt the people it is meant to protect.

First, who lives in subdivided units (SDUs)? About 110,000 households do, in flats carved into small cubicles, and they pay 15 to 25 percent more per square foot than tenants in ordinary flats [D1p1]. Most are new arrivals, single parents and people waiting for public housing, so the SDU is a residual option while they wait in the queue [D1p2]. The one rule Hong Kong has so far is the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance. It capped rent increases inside an existing tenancy, but it did not cap the starting rent [D1p3]. So the real question is whether to go further and cap the level of rents.

The best argument for going further is that the soft rules have not fixed affordability. The rent burden of SDU households got worse under the ordinance, from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. A hard cap could free about 800 dollars a month for a tenant like Mr Chan [D3p2], which is a real gain.

However, the evidence says a hard cap has a cost that falls on other people. A working paper reviews the literature and estimates that a 10 percent cut in rent caused by a cap removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of SDU stock over five years [D6p1]. The paper separates two kinds of control. Hard caps on rent levels show consistent contraction, while caps on increases inside a tenancy show small or no effects on stock [D6p2]. Hong Kong's ordinance is the second kind, and the stock data fits that: stock grew from 107,300 in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. For a tighter cap, the paper models a 15 percent cut in initial rents. Incumbent tenants would gain about 900 dollars a month, but 3,000 to 4,000 households a year could not enter the segment, mostly new arrivals [D6p4]. Berlin agrees. After its 2020 cap, listings contracted within a year as owners withheld flats or moved them to uncontrolled segments [D7p1]. When the law was struck down, rents for new contracts jumped above the earlier trend [D7p2].

The landlords make the opposite objection. The Landlords Association says a hard cap is confiscatory and that members will leave the segment if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1] [D5p2]. I do not accept this at face value. The Association admits it has no data on its members' real margins, and its cost numbers come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Also, stock kept growing under the softer 2021 rules [D2p1]. So I treat landlord exit as a real risk of a hard cap, not as a proven fact. It matters because Mr Chan fears the same thing. He has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2].

So my reply to both sides is that price is not the main problem. Mr Chan himself says what would help most is not lower rent but a shorter waiting list, and that he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. A survey of 440 SDU households found the same: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of secure tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public housing offer [D11p1]. The NGO report argues that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem, and that enforcement effort is better spent on inspecting fire and ventilation conditions [D11p2]. Mr Chan's first complaint was insecurity, since his landlord ended an earlier tenancy on two weeks notice when he asked for a repair [D3p1]. Tenure security would deal with that directly.

There are limits to my argument. A light band on initial rents is still a cap on levels, so it could shrink supply if it binds. My answer is that it should be loose enough not to bind much, but the sources give no number for that, so I cannot say where the line is. I also found nothing in the archive that explains why rent burden rose under the ordinance, so I cannot say whether the soft control was simply too weak.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten, but by giving tenants security and a loose band, not by a hard cap on initial rents. That protects tenants like Mr Chan from sudden eviction without pushing the poorest newcomers out of the market. The real fix is the waiting list.
