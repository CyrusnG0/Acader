Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units by adding a hard cap on initial rents. It should tighten security of tenure instead, with at most a light band on starting rents. The evidence in the archive points this way for three reasons.

The stability of SDU stock under the 2021 ordinance, 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1], is not evidence that a binding cap is safe. It is evidence that a non-binding instrument does not bind. The working paper is explicit: first-generation hard caps show consistent contraction; second-generation increase caps show small or null effects, "which explains the stable stock in the D2 index" [D6p2]. One think-tank piece cited in the news digest makes exactly the wrong inference from this data [D12p2].

But the mechanism matters more than the direction. The academic literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is stark. A 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while 3,000 to 4,000 households per year, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households," cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. That is the same population D1 identifies as the segment's core tenants [D1p2]. Berlin reinforces the point: caps bind differently on entrants than incumbents, and "the bill is handed to those still outside" [D7p3].

Tenure security, by contrast, addresses the harm tenants actually name. Mr Chan says the worst part is not price but insecurity, a landlord ending his tenancy with two weeks' notice [D3p1], and that he would "accept any rent to leave" if the public-housing queue fell from five and a half years to three [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]. Mrs Wong's own building shows landlords do sometimes exit, with two flats converted to storage [D4p3], but the Landlords Association's threat of mass exit is weakly evidenced: it concedes no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3].

The landlord side must be taken seriously, but only partly. The Landlords Association says owners will leave the segment if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]. I accept the exit threat for hard caps because the research backs it, but not as a reason to avoid limits on increases.

There are limits to my argument. The NGO report is written by a group that wants tenure security, so I do not rely on it alone, but the academic paper reaches a similar view on hard caps [D11p3]. Also, rent burden for these households still worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2], and figures after 2023 rely on survey extrapolation [D2p3], so doing nothing is not enough. This is why I support tenure security and a light band rather than leaving things as they are.

In conclusion, a hard rent cap would help those who already have a unit and hurt those trying to get one. Tighter security of tenure gives tenants what they say they want without the supply loss.
