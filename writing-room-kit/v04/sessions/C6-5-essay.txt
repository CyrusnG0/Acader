**The Case Against Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) by imposing a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points consistently to a narrow, uncomfortable conclusion: a binding cap on initial rent levels would protect some incumbents at the cost of shrinking the bottom of the housing market and excluding the very households the policy names as its beneficiaries. A better path is to pair light initial-rent bands with tenure security and, above all, to attack the queue that creates the segment's desperation pricing.

The strongest argument for tightening is moral: SDU tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent per-square-foot premium precisely because their options are exhausted [D1p1]. Mrs Wong, paying $4,200 with no written agreement and nine years of tenure, captures the injustice—the 2021 ordinance "changed nothing she can see" because it left initial rents untouched [D4p1][D1p3]. Yet good intentions do not settle elasticity. The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity in marginal stock at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The paper's Hong Kong-like model is blunter: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about $900 monthly to incumbents while 3,000 to 4,000 households per year—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households—cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. Those excluded are the same population the cap intends to protect [D6p3].

The counter-argument—that the 2021 ordinance produced stable, even growing, stock—misreads the instrument. SDU stock rose from 100,800 in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1], but the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance was second-generation: it capped increases within tenancies, not initial rents [D1p3]. The literature finds second-generation controls show small or null stock effects, while first-generation hard caps show consistent contraction [D6p2]. The visible stability is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument; a binding cap would behave differently [D12p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: capped initial offers fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the Mietendeckel fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The tenants protected were those already inside; the bill went to those still outside [D7p3].

Contraction is not hypothetical here. Mr Chan has watched two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2], and Mrs Wong's own stairwell contains two flats converted to non-residential storage—a contradiction she reports without seeing [D4p3]. The Landlords Association's threat to exit is self-interested and its data are weak: no margin data, and compliance costs drawn from a 210-member survey with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported [D5p3]. But weak evidence of exit is not evidence of no exit, and the Association's 18,000 units represent real supply [D5p1]. Even a modest 3 to 5 percent contraction is thousands of units removed from the poorest segment.

Enforcement compounds the problem. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals are least able to complain [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap also invites compensation claims or judicial review, with litigation timelines outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

What tenants themselves say is decisive. Mr Chan, offered an $800 monthly saving, fears conversion and names the waiting list: if the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p2][D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem; every unit of enforcement spent policing rents is one not spent on fire, partition, and ventilation harms [D11p2].

The proportionate response is therefore a narrow tightening: tenure security plus a light, published, indexed band on initial rents—defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]—combined with accelerated public-housing supply. This protects incumbents somewhat, avoids the contraction arithmetic, and directs scarce enforcement where harm is immediate. Hong Kong should not tighten rent control with a hard cap. It should shorten the queue.
