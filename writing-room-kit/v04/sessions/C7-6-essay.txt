# Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units: A Case for Targeted Tenure Reform Over Hard Caps

Hong Kong's 110,000 subdivided-unit households face rents that extract a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream flats, precisely because they are priced at the margin of desperation [D1p1]. The question is whether tightening rent control would help them or shrink the stock they depend on. I argue that Hong Kong should tighten regulation—but through tenure security and indexed rent bands, not a hard cap on initial rents. A hard cap would produce measurable stock contraction, systematically fail the most exploited tenants, and leave the underlying quantity problem untouched. The evidence points toward a narrower instrument.

**The case against a hard cap**

The strongest evidence against a hard cap is the supply arithmetic. The reviewed literature estimates the long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply at 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about $900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000–4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. This is the welfare trap at the heart of the debate: the households priced out are the same population the cap intends to protect [D6p3].

Crucially, the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance does not prove that a binding cap is safe. The 2021 instrument was second-generation—it capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents untouched [D1p3, D6p2]. The elasticity literature distinguishes exactly this: first-generation controls on levels show consistent contraction; second-generation controls show small or null effects [D6p2]. The visible stability in the 2019–2025 index is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and a binding one would behave differently [D12p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: when the Mietendeckel capped initial offers, listing supply contracted measurably within a year, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1, D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is precise—caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

**The case against doing nothing—and for a narrower tightening**

Yet the status quo fails tenants in ways a hard cap would not fix. Mrs Wong has lived in an SDU for nine years without a written agreement, and reports the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. Mr Chan's worst problem is not price but insecurity: his landlord ended a tenancy with two weeks' notice when he asked to repair a leaking window [D3p1]. When asked what would help most, he does not name rent—he names the waiting list [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households confirms this revealed preference: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The binding constraint is queue length and insecurity, not rent level [D11p1].

This reframes the policy question. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem: the segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2]. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire, partition, and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

**A defensible middle instrument**

The legal opinion identifies a middle path: cap initial rent by reference to a published, indexed per-square-foot band, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation—unlike a hard cap, which would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's property protections [D9p1, D9p2]. The NGO report supports exactly this combination: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, opposing a hard cap on the D6 contraction arithmetic and its own survey's revealed preference [D11p3].

Two caveats must be acknowledged. First, a band requires survey infrastructure that does not currently exist [D9p2], and a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. Second, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals, are least able to complain [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. Any tightening must therefore be paired with proactive registration and condition inspection, not complaint-driven enforcement alone.

**Conclusion**

Hong Kong should tighten rent control—but not with a hard cap. The elasticity evidence [D6p1, D6p4], the Berlin precedent [D7p1–D7p3], and the landlord exit threat [D5p2] all point to contraction that would fall on the most vulnerable entrants. Instead, the government should adopt the floor-standard-plus-rent-band package already signaled in the consultation [D12p1]: indexed bands on initial rents, enforceable tenure security, and inspection capacity redirected toward conditions. This treats the price symptom without shrinking the stock, and it addresses what tenants actually name as their binding constraint—security and the queue, not the rent cheque [D3p3, D11p1].
