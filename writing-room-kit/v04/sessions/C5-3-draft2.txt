Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units only narrowly. It should add security of tenure and perhaps a light band on initial rents, but it should not bring in a hard cap.

The case for doing something is that the status quo fails on its own terms. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent untouched [D1p3], and Mrs Wong, nine years in her SDU, reports it "changed nothing she can see" because her landlord still charges "whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. Mr Chan's worst problem is not price but insecurity: his previous tenancy ended with two weeks' notice after he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent even under the ordinance [D2p2]. A regime that regulates only renewals, in a segment where many tenants have no written agreement at all [D4p1], is regulating the margin that matters least.

Yet the strongest evidence in the archive cuts against a hard initial-rent cap. The reviewed literature estimates the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents would transfer about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment at all [D6p4]. The paper is explicit that first-generation controls show consistent contraction, while second-generation controls show small or null effects [D6p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law was struck down, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2].

Tenants also say rent is not their main problem. In a survey of 440 households, 61 percent would trade a rent increase for three years of tenure security [D11p1]. So security of tenure is the better tightening.

The landlords say they will leave if returns fall [D5p2], but their data is weak and self-reported [D5p3]. I still think the supply risk from a hard cap is serious because it fits the working paper.

Enforcement is the decisive practical objection. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain, those with no written agreement and the newest arrivals, are exactly those a complaint-driven regime would miss, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. A cap that protects incumbents while missing the most vulnerable, and shrinks entry at the same time, is a poor trade.
