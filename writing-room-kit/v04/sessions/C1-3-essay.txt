Hong Kong should tighten the rules on subdivided units, but only narrowly. It should strengthen tenure security and add a light band on initial rents. It should not set a hard cap on rent levels. My reason is that the hard cap risks hurting the people it is meant to help, while tenants say their biggest problems are insecurity and the long wait for public housing.

First, who lives in these units? About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, which are flats carved into small cubicles [D1p1]. They pay about 15 to 25 percent more per square foot than people in ordinary flats [D1p1]. Many of these tenants are new arrivals, single parents and people waiting for public housing, and the average wait is around five and a half years [D1p2]. For them the unit is not a choice but a place to stay while the queue moves [D1p2]. Mr Chan, a delivery driver, pays 5,800 dollars for a 120 sq ft cubicle, and he says the worst part is not the price but the insecurity, because a landlord once ended his tenancy on two weeks notice after he asked for a repair [D3p1].

Hong Kong already has a mild rule. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped rent increases inside a tenancy but did not cap the starting rent [D1p3]. After it, the number of units kept growing, from 107,300 in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. The working paper explains why. It separates hard caps on rent levels from caps on increases inside a tenancy, and finds that the first kind shows consistent contraction while the second shows small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. So making the 2021 type of rule stricter looks fairly safe. One warning is that the data after 2023 relies on survey extrapolation, so I cannot be fully sure [D2p3].

A hard cap is the risky choice. The working paper estimates that a 10 percent fall in rent removes about 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. In Berlin, a cap was put on about 1.5 million flats, and the supply of listings fell within a year as owners held back flats [D7p1]. When the law was struck down, rents for new contracts jumped above the earlier trend [D7p2]. The lesson is that a cap protects tenants already inside, and the bill goes to people still outside [D7p3]. The Landlords Association says its members would leave the market if returns fell below mortgage cost [D5p2]. I do not fully trust this, because they admit they have no data on their real margins and their cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. I use it only as a warning that matches the other evidence.

The best argument for a hard cap is that it helps people who are already inside. Mr Chan says a cap would free about 800 dollars a month, which is food for a month [D3p2]. That is a real benefit and I do not want to wave it away. But he also fears that landlords would rather turn cubicles back into storage rooms, and he has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked [D3p2]. The working paper says the households that lose units are the same population the cap is meant to protect, and whether tenants gain depends on how many stay long enough to offset those who can no longer find a unit [D6p3]. So the gain for insiders is uncertain and the loss for outsiders is likely.

What do tenants actually ask for? When Mr Chan was asked what would help most, he named the waiting list, not rent, and said he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. An NGO survey of 440 households found the same pattern: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent rise for three years of tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public housing offer [D11p1]. The NGO also argues that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem, and that enforcement effort is better spent on inspecting fire and ventilation [D11p2]. This is why my comparison of the two rules is weighted toward tenure. Security of tenure is the main part of the policy, and the band on initial rents is the smaller part.

My conclusion is a narrow tightening. Strengthen tenure security, add a light band on initial rents once real margin data is collected, and avoid a hard cap on rent levels. This matches the NGO's own recommendation of tenure security plus a light band [D11p3]. The limits of my argument are that the NGO is an advocacy group, the Berlin case is a different city, and the Hong Kong data after 2023 is partly estimated. Even so, the evidence points the same way, which is that the hard cap saves a few insiders money while costing the people who are already worst off.
