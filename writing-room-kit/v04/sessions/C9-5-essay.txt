**Tighten Tenure, Not Rents: A Qualified No to a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) with a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead tighten *tenure security* and condition standards, paired with a light, reviewable rent band. The evidence in this archive points consistently to one conclusion: a hard cap would bind hardest on the households it claims to protect, while the actual pain point named by tenants is not the rent level but the queue and the insecurity.

Start with the diagnosis. SDU tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households and public-housing waiters facing a queue of about five and a half years; the SDU is a residual option, not a choice [D1p2]. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, does not name rent — he names the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households confirms this at scale: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. A rent cap addresses the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2].

Now the supply arithmetic. The academic literature estimates long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is stark: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while 3,000 to 4,000 households a year — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households — cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. Those excluded are precisely the population named in [D1p2].

The counter-argument rests on the stable stock under the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance, which capped increases within tenancies but not initial rents [D1p3], with estimated stock rising from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this is not evidence that a binding cap is safe. The working paper distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases), which show small or null effects [D6p2]. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation; its stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument, exactly as one think-tank piece argues [D12p2]. It also excluded SDUs with co-living services from key provisions [D1p3], and post-2023 figures rely on voluntary reporting below the ordinance threshold [D2p3]. Meanwhile real rent burden worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2], and Mrs Wong, whose tenancy predates the ordinance, says it changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. A non-binding instrument that leaves the pain point untouched is not a template for tightening.

The comparative cases reinforce the asymmetry. Berlin's 2020 cap cut initial-offer rents sharply, but supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The lesson is explicit: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, protecting those already inside and handing the bill to those still outside [D7p3]. New York's second-generation scheme shows modest contraction precisely because it is light at first rent — the point Hong Kong left untouched [D8p2] — yet even there, tightening is associated with a fall in available stabilised listings [D8p3].

Enforcement compounds the problem. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap would need a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. The subcommittee's own modelling finds that enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail — tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals [D10p2]. The chair struck from the public version the admission that no enforcement design protects the newest-arrival tail first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap would also invite compensation claims and judicial review that would outlast a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

What should be done instead? The legal opinion's middle instrument — a published, indexed per-square-foot band defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2] — combined with the NGO's preferred package of tenure security plus a light band [D11p3], and the consultation's expected "floor-standard + rent band" [D12p1]. This addresses Mrs Wong's real grievance, which is not the level of rent but that the landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1] — a problem of unregulated, insecure tenancies, not of an uncapped initial price alone. Pair it with enforcement spending on fire, partition and ventilation, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2], and with genuine progress on the queue that Mr Chan actually named [D3p3].

The honest position is uncomfortable: a hard cap would help some incumbents at the expense of the excluded, and the excluded are the poorest. Hong Kong should tighten tenure and standards, not initial rents.
