**Tighten the Tenancy, Not the Rent: A Case Against a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) by capping initial rents. It should instead tighten tenure security and enforcement of living conditions, paired at most with a light, indexed band on first rents. The archive points consistently to one conclusion: a hard cap on initial rent levels would transfer income to today's incumbents while shrinking the residual stock on which tomorrow's most vulnerable households depend, and it would do so through an enforcement apparatus that cannot reach the tenants it claims to protect.

The strongest evidence for tightening is the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance: SDU units grew from 100,800 in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, and median rent per square foot rose 3.1 percent in 2021–2023, down from 6.8 percent in 2019–2021 [D2p1][D2p2]. One think tank reads this as proof that "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2]. But the academic literature explains the stability differently: the 2021 ordinance is a second-generation control—capping increases within tenancies, not initial levels—and such controls show "small or null effects on stock," whereas first-generation hard caps show "consistent contraction effects" [D6p2]. The visible stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument; a binding one behaves differently [D12p2]. The same literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1].

The welfare arithmetic is uncomfortable and should decide the question. Modelling a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about HK$900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment—"mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]. Those excluded are precisely the population the cap intends to protect: new arrivals, single-parent households, and public-housing waiters [D1p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. New York's second-generation scheme has modest contraction precisely because it is "light at first-rent" [D8p2]—the exact point Hong Kong's ordinance also left untouched [D6p2].

Landlord exit threats deserve skepticism, but not dismissal. The Association's claim of confiscatory margins is unsupported—it concedes no data on actual margins, and its compliance-cost figures rest on a 210-member survey with a 34 percent self-reported response rate [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation that units are landlords' pensions cuts the other way too: she reports two flats in her stairwell converted to storage after the ordinance while still believing landlords cannot exit [D4p2][D4p3]. Mr Chan has seen two units on his floor "emptied and locked for a year" [D3p2]. Exit is not hypothetical; it is local and visible.

Enforcement is the decisive practical objection. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. The tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement—Mrs Wong has never had one, and says the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]—or the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore "systematically miss the most exploited tail" [D10p2], and the subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap also invites judicial review: framed as deprivation of income it attracts compensation claims; framed as contract-term regulation it leaves initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated [D9p1]. Litigation would outlast a Council term [D9p3].

What tenants actually name is not rent. Mr Chan, offered a hard cap, worries about exit and says he would "accept any rent to leave" if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey finds 61 percent would trade a nominal increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The segment is tight because the queue is long; caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2].

The defensible package is therefore the one already signalled: a floor-standard plus rent band, not a hard cap [D12p1]. Cap increases within tenancies, mandate written agreements and minimum notice, index a published per-square-foot band so regulators can defend it as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], and spend enforcement capacity on fire, partition, and ventilation—where harm is immediate [D11p2]. Tighten the tenancy, not the rent.
