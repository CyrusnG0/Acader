Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but in a moderate and targeted way. My view is that control helps tenants a lot and, if it is designed carefully, it does not push landlords out of the market. The evidence in the course archive supports this, although it also shows that the current rules leave some gaps.

The first point is about supply. A common worry is that rent control makes landlords sell up or stop renting, so there are fewer homes. The archive's index of subdivided units does not show this. The estimated stock was 100,800 units in 2019, 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. That growth carried on after the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, so the data suggests that moderate regulation did not cause a visible exit of stock. For me this is the strongest reason not to fear a tighter but still moderate rule.

The second point is that tenants still face heavy pressure. Median rent per square foot in these units rose 6.8 percent in 2019 to 2021, then 3.1 percent in 2021 to 2023 under the ordinance, while general private rents rose 2.4 and 1.7 percent in the same periods [D2p2]. So rents in subdivided units still grew faster than the general market even with the ordinance. Also, the rent burden for these households got worse, from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. This tells me the present control is not enough to protect people, which is why I say it should be tightened.

The third point is about learning from other places. New York has regulated rents since 1947 and now covers about 44 percent of its rental stock [D8p1]. Its scheme is called second generation, because increases are capped inside a tenancy while first rents and vacancy are only lightly regulated [D8p1]. The note says stock contraction there is modest because the scheme is light at first rent [D8p2]. That is a useful lesson for Hong Kong, because the note says our 2021 ordinance also left that first-rent point untouched [D8p2]. So tightening does not have to mean a hard cap on everything. It could mean keeping the moderate approach while looking more carefully at how rents are set at the start.

There are costs I should admit. The New York note says regulated units show lower mobility, with tenants staying longer and a wide gap between regulated and unregulated rents [D8p2]. That could be unfair to people who are looking for a first home and cannot get into a regulated unit. I do not think this cancels my argument, but it means the policy should not be pushed too far.

In conclusion, I support tighter but moderate rent control on subdivided units. Supply kept growing under the 2021 ordinance [D2p1], rents and burdens still rose [D2p2], and the New York example shows that a light and careful scheme can work without big stock losses [D8p2]. The government should tighten the rules step by step and watch the stock numbers as it does so.
