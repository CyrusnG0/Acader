Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units only in a narrow way. It should add security of tenure and perhaps a light band on initial rents, but it should not bring in a hard cap.

About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they pay 15 to 25 percent more per square foot than people in ordinary flats [D1p1]. Many have no choice because the public housing wait is about five and a half years [D1p2]. The 2021 ordinance only capped increases inside a tenancy [D1p3], and the stock of units kept growing afterwards [D2p1]. But rent burden still rose from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2].

Berlin shows the risk of a hard cap. Listings fell within a year because owners held back flats [D7p1], and the people protected were those already inside while those outside paid the bill [D7p3]. The academic paper says hard caps on levels reduce supply, while caps on increases do not [D6p2]. It estimates that a 15 percent cut would give incumbents about 900 dollars a month but block 3,000 to 4,000 households a year [D6p4].

The strongest objection is from tenants who want relief now. Mrs Wong supports a hard cap [D4p2]. But her own account undercuts her, because two flats in her stairwell became storage [D4p3]. Mr Chan says the worst part is insecurity, since his landlord ended a tenancy after he asked to repair a window [D3p1]. In a survey, 61 percent would accept a rent rise for three years of security [D11p1].

There are limits. Tenure security does not shorten the queue, which Mr Chan names as his real problem [D3p3]. The stock data may also hide local exits, as post-2023 figures are extrapolated [D2p3].
