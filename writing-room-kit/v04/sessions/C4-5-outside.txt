**Tighten Tenure, Not Rents: A Qualified No to a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) by imposing a hard cap on initial rents. It should instead tighten tenure security and enforcement, paired at most with a light, indexed band on initial rents. The archive points consistently to one conclusion: a binding first-generation cap would shrink the very stock the poorest households depend on, while the binding constraint tenants themselves name is not price but the queue.

The case for a hard cap rests on real pain. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium precisely because they are at the most desperate margin [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU with no written agreement, says the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. Mr Chan's landlord ended his tenancy with two weeks' notice when he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. These are genuine harms, and they deserve a policy response.

But the evidence says a hard cap is the wrong instrument. The academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies): first-generation controls show consistent supply contraction, while second-generation controls show small or null stock effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which explains why stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. That stability is not evidence that a binding cap is safe; it is evidence that the existing instrument does not bind at the point of pain. The think-tank argument that "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2] misreads a non-binding instrument's stability as proof that a binding one would behave the same way.

The contraction arithmetic is uncomfortable and specific. The estimated elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rents is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents would transfer about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The tenants a cap protects are those already inside; the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

Two further problems make a hard cap worse than it looks. First, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, a band-based cap needs a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years, and the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement or the newest arrivals [D10p1][D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. Second, a hard cap framed as deprivation of rental income would attract compensation claims and judicial review, with a litigation timeline outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. The Landlords Association's threat of exit is self-interested and its evidence is thin — no margin data, a 34 percent response rate on a self-reported survey [D5p3] — but the elasticity literature does not depend on the Association's credibility.

The strongest argument against a hard cap comes from tenants themselves. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, does not name rent; he names the waiting list, and says he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The segment is tight because the queue is long; rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2].

The defensible package is therefore narrow: statutory tenure security with written agreements, a light indexed band on initial rents defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], and enforcement capacity redirected toward fire, partition, and ventilation conditions where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. This would address Mrs Wong's real grievance — a landlord raising rent at will with no contract — without triggering the contraction that would fall hardest on the households the cap claims to protect. Tighten tenure, not rents.
