# The Case Against Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units

Hong Kong's subdivided unit (SDU) crisis is real and urgent: roughly 110,000 households live in cubicles carved from larger flats, paying a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream private housing [D1p1]. The intuitive response—cap the rents—is understandable, but the evidence in this archive points toward a different conclusion. Hong Kong should **not** tighten rent control on SDUs with a hard cap on initial rents. Instead, it should pursue tenure security, enforcement of living conditions, and above all, a shorter public-housing queue. A hard cap would help some incumbents while quietly shrinking the stock that future tenants depend on, and it would systematically fail the most vulnerable households it claims to protect.

## The Contraction Arithmetic Is Real, If Modest

The strongest argument for a cap is that the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance did not trigger visible stock exit: SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this stability is explained by the instrument's design, not by landlords' benevolence. The 2021 ordinance is a *second-generation* control—it caps increases within an existing tenancy but leaves the initial rent untouched [D1p3]. The academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels), which show consistent contraction, from second-generation controls (caps on increases), which show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The stable stock in [D2p1] is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument; a binding cap on initial rents would behave differently.

How differently? The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for SDU-type stock at 0.31 to 0.48—meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A modeled 15 percent cut in initial rents would transfer about HK$900 per month to incumbents, but 3,000 to 4,000 households per year—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households—would be unable to enter the segment at all [D6p4]. That is the uncomfortable welfare arithmetic: the cap protects those already inside and hands the bill to those still outside.

## The People the Cap Is Meant to Help Would Be Hit Last

The enforcement problem compounds the supply problem. Current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement—like Mrs Wong, who has lived in her SDU for nine years without a tenancy document and says the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]—or the newest arrivals. Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify *any* enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

Meanwhile, the tenants themselves do not name rent as their binding constraint. Mr Chan, a delivery driver paying HK$5,800 for a 120 sq ft cubicle, says the worst part is not price but insecurity—his previous landlord ended his tenancy with two weeks' notice [D3p1]. Asked what would help most, he does not name rent; he names the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households confirms this: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem; the segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2].

## The Berlin Cautionary Tale

Berlin's 2020 Mietendeckel capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats. Initial-offer rents fell sharply—but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. When the constitutional court struck the law down in 2021, a backlog of suppressed increases released into the market and new-contract rents jumped above the pre-cap trend line [D7p2]. The lesson: caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

## What Should Be Done Instead

A narrow tightening—tenure security plus a light band on initial rents—is defensible, and the NGO report supports precisely this [D11p3]. Tenure security directly addresses Mr Chan's stated fear [D3p1] and the revealed preference of 61 percent of surveyed tenants [D11p1]. A published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, would also sit on firmer legal ground than a hard cap, which risks compensation claims and judicial review that would outlast a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. But the primary instrument must be supply and queue reform: every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire, partition, and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

The most honest summary comes from the tenants themselves. Mr Chan does not ask for cheaper rent; he asks for a shorter queue [D3p3]. A hard cap would give him—and the 3,000 to 4,000 households priced out each year [D6p4]—the opposite of what he needs. Hong Kong should tighten tenure security and conditions, not initial rents.
