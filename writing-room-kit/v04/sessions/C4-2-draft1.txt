Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units by adding a hard cap on initial rents. It should tighten security of tenure instead, with at most a light band on starting rents. The evidence in the archive points this way for three reasons.

First, the stock data shows that the milder rule already in place has not hurt supply. Estimated stock of subdivided units grew from 100,800 in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, and it kept growing through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance [D2p1]. Rents under the ordinance rose more slowly, 3.1 percent against 6.8 percent before, though still faster than general private rents [D2p2]. One caution is that figures after 2023 rely on survey extrapolation [D2p3].

Second, a hard cap is a different kind of rule and the research says it behaves differently. The working paper separates hard caps on rent levels from caps on increases within a tenancy. Hard caps show consistent contraction of stock, while the Hong Kong ordinance is the second kind, which explains why stock stayed stable [D6p2]. The paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes about 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Its model of a 15 percent cut in initial rents gives incumbents about 900 dollars a month, but locks 3,000 to 4,000 households a year out of the segment [D6p4]. The households that lose units are the same people the cap is meant to protect [D6p3].

Third, tenants themselves care more about security than price. Mr Chan pays 5,800 dollars a month for a 120 square foot cubicle, yet says the worst part is insecurity, since a landlord once ended his tenancy on two weeks notice after he asked for a repair [D3p1]. He is split on a hard cap. It would free about 800 dollars a month, but he fears landlords would empty units instead, and he has seen two units on his floor locked for a year [D3p2]. A survey of 440 households found that 61 percent would trade a small rent increase for three years of security [D11p1].

The landlord side must be taken seriously, but only partly. The Landlords Association says owners will leave the segment if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]. However it admits it has no data on real margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. I accept the exit threat for hard caps because the research backs it, but not as a reason to avoid limits on increases.

There are limits to my argument. The NGO report is written by a group that wants tenure security, so I do not rely on it alone, but the academic paper reaches a similar view on hard caps [D11p3]. Also, rent burden for these households still worsened from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2], so doing nothing is not enough. This is why I support tenure security and a light band rather than leaving things as they are.

In conclusion, a hard rent cap would help those who already have a unit and hurt those trying to get one. Tighter security of tenure gives tenants what they say they want without the supply loss.
