# Tighten, but Not the Way the Debate Suggests

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) — but with a tenure-security-first instrument, not a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive points to a narrow, defensible tightening and a clear rejection of the maximalist version.

The case for tightening at all rests on a moral fact and a market fact. The moral fact: SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium precisely because their options are exhausted, and they are disproportionately new arrivals, single parents, and households stuck in a public-housing queue averaging five and a half years [D1p1][D1p2]. The market fact: the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within tenancies but left initial rents — the actual pain point — untouched, and excluded co-living SDUs [D1p3]. Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU with no written agreement, reports the ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. A regime that regulates the increment while ignoring the entry price regulates the symptom the incumbent feels least.

Yet the archive is equally clear that a hard initial-rent cap would backfire. The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity in marginal stock at 0.31–0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents while locking out 3,000–4,000 households a year, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those excluded are the same population the cap claims to protect [D6p3]. Berlin is the cautionary precedent — listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. Mr Chan, who would gain 800 dollars a month, already fears landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than accept regulated tenants; he has watched two units on his floor sit locked for a year [D3p2]. Mrs Wong's confidence that landlords "cannot exit" sits awkwardly beside her own report of two flats in her stairwell converted to non-residential storage [D4p2][D4p3]. The Landlords Association's threat to exit is self-interested and its evidence thin — self-reported, 34 percent response rate, no actual margin data [D5p3] — but the academic elasticity estimates do not depend on the Association's credibility.

The stability of SDU stock since 2021 is not proof that binding caps are safe. The stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1], but the working paper explains why: 2021's ordinance is second-generation, capping increases rather than levels, and such controls show small or null stock effects — unlike first-generation hard caps, which contract consistently [D6p2]. One think-tank's inference that stable stock proves "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2] therefore misreads the natural experiment. A binding instrument behaves differently from a non-binding one.

What, then, should tightening look like? Three elements. First, tenure security: 61 percent of surveyed tenants would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security, and 74 percent for a faster housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan names the queue, not the rent, as what would help most [D3p3]. Second, a light, indexed band on initial rents — the legal opinion's "rate-capping rather than confiscation" framing, which survives judicial review where outright deprivation invites compensation claims and litigation outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p2][D9p3]. Third, enforcement honesty: current inspection covers under 6 percent of stock annually, a certifiable tenancy register needs 40–60 staff-years, and any complaint-driven regime will systematically miss the newest arrivals and those without written agreements [D10p1][D10p2]. The subcommittee could not design an enforcement model that protects the newest-arrival tail first rather than last [D10p3]. That is a reason to build the register and inspection capacity before promising the cap, not a reason to abandon regulation.

The NGO report is right that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem: the segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2]. But "don't cap, fix supply" is not a complete answer either, because the queue will not shorten this decade, and tenants are being harmed now. The defensible position is a narrow tightening: tenure security, a reviewable initial-rent band, and enforcement investment targeted at the most exploited tail. Reject the hard cap — the contraction arithmetic [D6p1][D6p4] and Berlin's lesson [D7p2] are too costly — but do not use that rejection as an excuse for the status quo that left Mrs Wong unprotected for nine years.
