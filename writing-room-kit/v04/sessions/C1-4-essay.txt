Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only narrowly. It should strengthen the cap on rent increases, give tenants security of tenure, and consider a light, published band on starting rents. It should not impose a hard cap on the initial rent. The archive supports this middle position better than either doing nothing or capping hard [D11p3].

The problem is real. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, which are flats carved into small cubicles, and per square foot they pay 15 to 25 percent more than tenants in mainstream flats [D1p1]. Many are new arrivals, single parents and people waiting for public housing, where the wait is around five and a half years [D1p2]. For them the unit is a leftover option, not a choice. Rent burden also got worse, from 31 to 34 percent of income, even after the 2021 ordinance [D2p2]. So doing nothing is not a good answer.

The current rule is weak by design. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases inside a tenancy but did not cap the initial rent [D1p3]. Stock kept growing afterwards, from about 107,300 units in 2021 to about 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. A working paper explains why. It says hard caps on rent levels cause consistent contraction, while caps on increases have small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. One news summary reports a think-tank saying stable stock just shows the ordinance does not bind. I agree, and that is exactly why I do not think the stable stock proves a hard cap would be safe.

The strongest argument against a hard cap is about supply. The same paper estimates that a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That matters because the households that lose units are the same people the cap is meant to protect [D6p3]. Berlin is a useful example. After its 2020 cap, new listings fell as owners withheld flats or moved them to uncontrolled segments [D7p1]. When the court struck the law down, rents on new contracts jumped above the earlier trend [D7p2]. The lesson was that tenants already inside gain, and the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

The landlords' association argues that members will leave the segment if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2]. I do not accept this at face value. The association admits it has no data on members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. Still, I cannot rule out that some owners would exit, and the paper's numbers show the risk is not imaginary. That is why I only support a light band and not a hard cap.

There are also practical limits. A legal opinion says a hard cap would almost certainly attract compensation claims under property rights, while an indexed, reviewable per-square-foot band could be defended as rate-capping [D9p1][D9p2]. But the survey system a band needs does not exist yet [D9p2], and judicial review could take longer than a Council term [D9p3]. So the first step is building the data, not passing a cap.

Finally, tenants themselves say rent level is not their main problem. In a survey of 440 households, 61 percent would accept a nominal rent rise for three years of secure tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public housing offer [D11p1]. The same report says enforcement effort spent policing rents is effort not spent on inspecting fire safety and ventilation, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. I take this as a reason to put tenure security and inspections first.

My answer has gaps. The archive gives no number for how large a light band should be, so I can only say it should be much smaller than the 10 percent cut in the supply estimate. Recent figures in the rent index also rely on survey extrapolation [D2p3]. Even so, the evidence points one way: tighten gently, protect tenure, and avoid a hard cap that could shrink the housing poor tenants depend on.
