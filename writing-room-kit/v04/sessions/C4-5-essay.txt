Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only in a careful way. My position is that the government should limit rent increases inside tenancies and add longer security of tenure, and it should not impose hard caps on rent levels.

But the evidence says a hard cap is the wrong instrument. The academic literature distinguishes first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) from second-generation controls (caps on increases within tenancies): first-generation controls show consistent supply contraction, while second-generation controls show small or null stock effects [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which explains why stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. That stability is not evidence that a binding cap is safe; it is evidence that the existing instrument does not bind at the point of pain. The think-tank argument that "capacity exists for more control" [D12p2] misreads a non-binding instrument's stability as proof that a binding one would behave the same way.

The contraction arithmetic is uncomfortable and specific. The estimated elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rents is 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents would transfer about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The tenants a cap protects are those already inside; the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

Still, the data show that tenants are not much better off. Median rent growth was 3.1 percent in 2021 to 2023, slower than the earlier 6.8 percent, but it was still higher than general private rent growth of 1.7 percent [D2p2]. Rent burden even rose from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. This suggests that the 2021 ordinance was too weak, and a modest tightening of the increase limit is justified.

The strongest argument against a hard cap comes from tenants themselves. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, does not name rent; he names the waiting list, and says he would accept any rent to leave if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. The segment is tight because the queue is long; rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2].

The NGO also says enforcement effort spent on policing rents is not spent on inspecting fire and ventilation conditions, where harm is immediate [D11p2]. I take this seriously, and it is a reason to keep the tightening simple and cheap to enforce.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten, but by lowering the allowed increases and extending tenure security. This keeps the stock stable, as the evidence suggests, while responding to rising rent burden.
