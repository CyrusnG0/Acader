Hong Kong should tighten its rules on subdivided units (SDUs), but the tightening should be about security of tenure and a light limit on rent increases, not a hard cap on rent levels. 

My first view was that SDU tenants are poor, landlords have the power, and so rents should be capped harder. The first part is true. SDUs hold about 110,000 households, and tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot compared with ordinary flats, because the space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. Most tenants are new arrivals or people waiting for public housing, so the SDU is a last option, not a choice [D1p2]. And the current rules are not enough. Rent burden for SDU households rose from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. The 2021 ordinance capped increases inside a tenancy but not the initial rent [D1p3].

The second part of my first view, that a stronger cap is the fix, did not hold up. A working paper separates hard caps on rent levels from caps on increases within a tenancy. Hard caps consistently shrink supply, while the second kind has small or null effects, and Hong Kong's 2021 law is the second kind [D6p2]. The numbers fit: the stock of SDUs grew from 100,800 in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. The harm of shrinking supply falls on the same poor households the cap is meant to help [D6p3]. Berlin gives a rough warning. After its 2020 cap, listings fell and owners moved flats to uncontrolled segments [D7p1], and the tenants protected were those already inside while the cost went to those outside [D7p3].

The landlords say they will leave if returns fall [D5p2]. I take this seriously but not at face value. Their own data is weak: the cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate, and they admit they have no data on actual margins [D5p3]. Mr Chan, a tenant, has seen two units on his floor emptied [D3p2], so the fear is not invented, but the stock figures show no mass exit under the milder control.

What do tenants actually need? In a survey of 440 households, the main problem named was queue length and insecurity of tenure, not the rent level, and 61 percent would accept a small rent rise for three years of security [D11p1]. Mr Chan's landlord ended an earlier tenancy with two weeks notice after he asked for a window repair [D3p1].

This is also why enforcement matters. Inspections cover under 6 percent of SDU stock each year [D10p1]. Enforcement depends on complaints, but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement and the newest arrivals [D10p2]. Mrs Wong has lived in her SDU for nine years without ever receiving a written agreement, and she says the 2021 ordinance changed nothing for her [D4p1]. Her case shows how my idea could fail: protection that depends on paperwork misses the people who most need it. So the law should presume a tenancy after a set time, even with no paper, and ban eviction as punishment for complaints.

There is a serious objection. The NGO report says rent caps treat a price symptom of a quantity problem, and that enforcement staff spent policing rents are not spent on safety inspections, where the harm is documented [D11p2]. I accept part of this. The root problem is the queue, which is about five and a half years [D1p2], and a policy on SDUs cannot fix that alone. Also, a heavy initial-rent register would be expensive, since one estimate is 40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]. So I would keep any band on initial rents light, as the report itself supports [D11p3], and put the main effort into tenure, which tenants can enforce themselves through a tribunal and which needs fewer inspectors.

I should be honest about two limits. First, the archive does not tell me how many tenants have no written agreement. Second, an internal minute admits that nobody found an enforcement design that protects the newest arrivals first rather than last [D10p3]. Presuming a tenancy helps people who stay, but not someone who has just arrived, and a band on initial rents could even make landlords less willing to take newcomers, as the Berlin case hints [D7p3].

So my position is yes, tighten, but carefully. Cap increases more firmly, protect tenants from retaliation, presume a tenancy without paper, keep any initial-rent band light, and treat the waiting list as the real long-term fix.
