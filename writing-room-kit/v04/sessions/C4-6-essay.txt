Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but only moderately. The best policy is to strengthen tenure security and add a light band on initial rents, and not to impose a hard cap on rent levels. This essay explains why tenants need more protection and why a hard cap would backfire.

The strongest argument for tightening is the lived reality of tenants. Mr Chan pays HK$5,800 for 120 square feet, roughly a third of household income, and describes insecurity as worse than price: his landlord ended a tenancy with two weeks' notice when he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. Mrs Wong has lived in an SDU for nine years with no written agreement, and reports that the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent even after the ordinance [D2p2]. These are serious harms, and the status quo is not defensible on its own terms.

The main argument against tightening is that landlords will leave. The landlords association says a hard cap is confiscatory and that members will convert or empty units if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1] [D5p2]. This is a serious warning because every lost unit means someone has no home tonight. However, the evidence behind it is weak. The association admits it has no data on members' actual margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. So the threat is a claim from a group with a stake, and it is not proven.

How differently would a binding cap behave? The reviewed elasticity estimates for marginal SDU-type stock run 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about HK$900 per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism in practice: capped initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law was struck down a backlog of suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is explicit: the cap protects those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Two further problems make a hard cap administratively self-defeating. First, enforcement capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Second, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, Mrs Wong's situation [D4p1], or the newest arrivals, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A legal opinion adds that a hard cap framed as deprivation of income would invite compensation claims and judicial review, with a litigation timeline outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten, but in a careful way. Tenants pay a premium and have few options [D1p1] [D1p2], yet hard caps risk shrinking the stock of housing for the same people [D6p1] [D6p3]. A light band on initial rents with three-year tenure security protects tenants while keeping landlords in the market [D11p3]. The landlords' exit threat should be taken seriously but not accepted at face value given its weak data [D5p3]. The policy should also be reviewed with better data, because current figures are partly extrapolated [D2p3].
