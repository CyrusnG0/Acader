# The Case Against Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) through a hard cap on initial rents. The evidence in this archive suggests that such a cap would shrink the already-tight stock, fail to protect the tenants it targets, and misdiagnose a quantity problem as a price problem. A narrow, enforceable package of tenure security and light rate-banding is the better path.

The stock data appear to offer comfort to cap proponents: SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, continuing through the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance [D2p1]. But this stability is precisely what theory predicts for a non-binding instrument. The 2021 ordinance was second-generation—capping increases within tenancies while leaving initial rents untouched—and such controls show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The same source estimates the elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A hard cap on initial rents is first-generation control, and first-generation controls show consistent contraction effects [D6p2]. The visible stability under the 2021 ordinance is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument, not evidence that a binding one would be safe [D12p2].

The welfare arithmetic is uncomfortable. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. These are the same populations the cap intends to protect [D1p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell sharply, but classified-listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The bill was handed to those still outside [D7p3].

Enforcement makes the picture worse. Current SDU inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. If landlords reprice above-band rents, the first instrument tenants hold is complaint—but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement [D4p1] or the newest arrivals, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A cap that cannot be enforced against the worst actors is not protection; it is a signal.

Tenants themselves point elsewhere. Mr Chan, facing insecurity rather than price as his worst problem [D3p1], is split on a hard cap: it would free perhaps 800 dollars a month, but he fears landlords would rather convert cubicles back to storage than deal with regulated tenants, and he has seen units emptied and locked for a year [D3p2]. Asked what would help most, he names the waiting list, not rent [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mrs Wong's support for a hard cap is undercut by her own observation of flats converted to non-residential storage after the ordinance [D4p3]—the exit mechanism she denies is happening around her.

The landlords' submission must be read with care: it concedes no data on actual margins and cites a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. This weakens their confiscation claim, but it does not rescue the cap. The legal opinion confirms that a hard cap framed as deprivation of rental income would attract compensation claims, while a cap framed as contractual-term regulation sits on firmer ground but leaves initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated [D9p1]. A middle instrument, capping initial rent by reference to a published per-square-foot band, is defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], and the consultation document is expected to float exactly this "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap [D12p1].

The binding constraint is queue length, not rent level [D11p1]. The segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2]. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire, partition, and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. The right move is a narrow tightening: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, supported by the NGO report [D11p3] and consistent with the legal opinion's defensible middle instrument [D9p2]. A hard cap would protect incumbents, punish entrants, and outrun the enforcement capacity that would make it real. Hong Kong should tighten tenure security and conditions—not initial rents.
