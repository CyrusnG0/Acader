# Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units?

Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs), but only narrowly: a hard cap on initial rents would likely backfire, whereas a package combining tenure security with a light, indexed rent band addresses the harm that is actually documented while avoiding the contraction arithmetic that makes first-generation controls dangerous. The archive supports tightening in principle and cautions strongly about tightening in the wrong form.

The case for doing *something* is that the status quo fails on its own terms. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent untouched [D1p3], and Mrs Wong, nine years in her SDU, reports it "changed nothing she can see" because her landlord still charges "whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. Mr Chan's worst problem is not price but insecurity: his previous tenancy ended with two weeks' notice after he asked for a window repair [D3p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent even under the ordinance [D2p2]. A regime that regulates only renewals, in a segment where many tenants have no written agreement at all [D4p1], is regulating the margin that matters least.

Yet the strongest evidence in the archive cuts against a *hard* initial-rent cap. The reviewed literature estimates the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. A modelled 15 percent cut in initial rents would transfer about 900 dollars a month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households per year — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households — unable to enter the segment at all [D6p4]. The paper is explicit that first-generation controls (caps on levels) show consistent contraction, while second-generation controls (caps on increases) show small or null effects [D6p2]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law was struck down, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The bill went to those still outside.

The landlords' threat of exit should be discounted, but not ignored. The Association's submission is self-interested and evidentially weak — no data on actual margins, compliance costs drawn from a 210-member survey with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported [D5p3]. Mrs Wong's observation is sharper: units are her landlords' pension, and "where else would the money come from?" [D4p2]. The stable stock through the 2021 ordinance [D2p1] shows moderate regulation did not trigger visible exit. But that stability is precisely what D6 predicts for a *non-binding* instrument [D6p2], and the think-tank debate over whether it proves capacity for more control or is an artifact of lightness remains unresolved [D12p2]. The honest reading: we cannot infer from 2021 that a binding cap is safe.

Enforcement is the decisive practical objection. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain — those with no written agreement, the newest arrivals — are exactly those a complaint-driven regime would miss, so enforcement would "systematically miss the most exploited tail" [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A cap that protects incumbents while missing the most vulnerable, and shrinks entry at the same time, is a poor trade.

The NGO survey points to what tenants actually want: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan says the same: he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. This reframes the problem. The segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2], and every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is one not spent inspecting fire, partition and ventilation conditions, where harm is immediate [D11p2].

My position: tighten, but with a second-generation-plus instrument. Enact statutory tenure security and written tenancy agreements as the priority; add a light, published per-square-foot band on initial rents, indexed and reviewable, which counsel judges defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2]. This targets the documented harm — insecurity and the initial-rent pain point — while keeping the instrument non-confiscatory and avoiding the contraction D6 and Berlin predict for hard caps. The government should also be candid that a hard cap would invite compensation claims and judicial review outlasting a Council term [D9p1][D9p3], and that no enforcement design currently protects the newest arrivals first [D10p3]. Tightening is justified; the form of tightening determines whether it helps the tenants it names.
