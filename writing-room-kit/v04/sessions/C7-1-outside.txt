# The Case Against a Hard Cap on Subdivided Unit Rents

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units through a hard cap on initial rents. The archive points toward a narrower package: tenure security plus a light, indexed rent band. A hard cap is the instrument most likely to hurt the very households it names, for three reasons: it attacks the price symptom of a quantity problem, it is unenforceable against the most exploited tenants, and it risks a stock contraction whose costs fall on new arrivals.

The first problem is diagnostic. The binding constraint for SDU households is not primarily rent level but queue length and insecurity. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan says the worst part is not price but insecurity—his previous tenancy ended with two weeks' notice—and when asked what would help most, he names the waiting list, not rent [D3p1][D3p3]. Mrs Wong's landlord raises rent "by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. These are symptoms of a long queue, not of an unregulated price. A cap treats the symptom.

Second, the arithmetic of contraction is real. The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The working paper's Hong Kong-like model is blunter: a 15 percent initial-rent cut yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin shows the mechanism in practice: capped initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The tenants a cap protects are those already inside; the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

Third, enforcement would miss the worst tail. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40 to 60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. The tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals—so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A cap that cannot reach the exploited is not protection; it is paper.

The counter-argument is that the 2021 ordinance did not shrink stock—the index shows growth from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1]. But this stability is an artifact of a non-binding instrument. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within tenancies, not initial rents, and excluded co-living SDUs [D1p3]; the academic paper distinguishes first-generation hard caps, which show consistent contraction, from second-generation increase caps, which show small or null effects [D6p2]. The stable stock under a second-generation instrument says nothing about what a first-generation cap would do—exactly the point one think-tank made against the other [D12p2].

Nor is the legal path clean. A hard cap framed as deprivation of rental income would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law; a contractual-term framing sits on firmer ground but leaves the initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated [D9p1]. Counsel's middle instrument is a published, indexed per-square-foot band defensible as rate-capping [D9p2]. That is the direction the consultation document is already floating: a "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap, citing Berlin [D12p1].

The better package is therefore the NGO's narrow tightening: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, with enforcement capacity redirected to conditions—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2][D11p3]. This addresses Chan's insecurity and Wong's arbitrary increases without shrinking the stock that houses someone tonight. The real fix remains the queue: if the wait were three years instead of five and a half, Chan would accept any rent to leave [D3p3]. Until then, regulate the terms, not the price.
