Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but gently. It should extend tenant protections and add only a light band on initial rents, not a hard cap.

About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot compared with normal flats [D1p1]. Many of them are new arrivals or families waiting for public housing, so the unit is not a real choice [D1p2]. This is why doing nothing is hard to defend.

But the choice of instrument matters. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance was second-generation regulation: it capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched [D1p3]. The stock index shows SDU units grew from 100,800 in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, continuing through the ordinance—consistent with the academic finding that second-generation controls show small or null stock effects [D2p1][D6p2]. New York's long-running stabilisation scheme tells the same story: modest contraction because it is light at first-rent, with costs surfacing instead as reduced maintenance and falling stabilised listings after the 2019 tightening [D8p2][D8p3]. The 2021 ordinance's stability is real, but it is stability under a non-binding instrument [D12p2].

A hard cap on initial rents is a different animal. The reviewed literature estimates long-run supply elasticity for marginal SDU-type stock at 0.31–0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong-like scenario is more concrete: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars monthly to incumbents, while 3,000–4,000 households per year—mostly new arrivals and newly formed households—cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. Berlin is the cautionary case: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year, and when the Mietendeckel was struck down, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The Berlin lesson is explicit: the cap protects those already inside; the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3]. Mr Chan, notably, fears exactly this—landlords converting cubicles to storage rather than dealing with regulated tenants, and he has seen two units locked for a year [D3p2].

The enforcement and legal picture reinforces the case against a hard cap. Current inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years to stand up [D10p1]. Worse, enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail: tenants with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong, or the newest arrivals are least able to complain, so the instrument would protect those easiest to reach, not those most in need [D10p2][D10p3]. Legally, a hard cap framed as deprivation of income stream invites compensation claims and judicial review that would outlast a Council term; a rate-capping band is more defensible but requires survey infrastructure that does not exist [D9p1][D9p2][D9p3].

The landlords say a hard cap is confiscatory and they would leave [D5p2]. Their cost data is self-reported with a low response rate, so I do not fully trust it [D5p3]. Still, the Berlin case suggests the exit threat is real.

In conclusion, tightening should focus on tenure security and a light band on initial rents, which is the approach the NGO report supports [D11p3].
