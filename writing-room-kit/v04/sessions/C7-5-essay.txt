# The Case Against a Hard Cap on SDU Rents

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) through a hard cap on initial rents, though a narrow package of tenure security and a light, indexed rent band is defensible. The evidence in this archive points consistently to one conclusion: a binding cap on initial rent levels would shrink the very stock that houses the poorest households, while leaving the actual binding constraint—queue length and insecurity—untouched.

The strongest argument for tightening is that the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance is toothless where it matters. It capped increases within tenancies but left the initial rent unregulated, and Mrs Wong, nine years in an SDU, reports the landlord "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium precisely because space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. So the pain point is real and the current instrument misses it.

But a hard cap on that initial rent would trigger contraction. The academic literature puts the long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply at 0.31–0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. First-generation controls—hard caps on levels—show consistent contraction effects, unlike the second-generation increase caps that explain the stable stock under the 2021 ordinance [D6p2]. The apparent stability in the index [D2p1] is therefore an artifact of a non-binding instrument, not evidence that capacity exists for more control [D12p2]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is stark: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents but leaves 3,000–4,000 households a year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Those excluded are the same population the cap intends to protect [D6p3].

The Berlin case confirms the mechanism. Capping initial-offer rents sharply reduced new-listing supply within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats [D7p1], and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p2]. The lesson is exact: caps protect incumbents and hand the bill to those still outside [D7p3]. New York's stabilisation scheme, light at first-rent, shows modest contraction but falling maintenance and a measurable fall in available stabilised listings after the 2019 tightening [D8p2][D8p3].

Landlord exit threats should be discounted—the Association has no data on members' actual margins and cites a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]—but the contraction arithmetic does not depend on landlord motives. It depends on elasticity, and the elasticity is the empirical finding [D6p1].

Enforcement is the second fatal problem. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock per year, and a band-based cap requires a certifiable tenancy register costing 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals—so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. The subcommittee could identify no design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3].

What would actually help? Mr Chan does not name rent; he names the waiting list, saying he would accept any rent if the queue were three years instead of five and a half [D3p3]. The NGO survey finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem [D11p2]. The revealed preference of tenants themselves is for tenure security and queue relief, not a hard cap.

The position that follows is the one the consultation appears to be floating: a floor-standard plus rent band package rather than a hard cap [D12p1]. A published, indexed per-square-foot band is legally defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], and a narrow tightening—tenure security plus a light band on initial rents—matches both the contraction arithmetic and tenants' stated preferences [D11p3]. A hard cap would free Mr Chan perhaps 800 dollars a month [D3p2], but at the cost of thousands of households who never get a unit at all. That is not a trade Hong Kong should make.
