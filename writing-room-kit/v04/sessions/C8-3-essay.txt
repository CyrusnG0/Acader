# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

**Position: No — not with a hard cap on initial rents. Hong Kong should tighten tenure security and conditions, plus a light indexed rent band, while treating the public-housing queue as the real instrument.**

The case for tightening is intuitive. SDU tenants pay a 15–25 percent per-square-foot premium for the worst housing in the city [D1p1], and real rent burden has worsened from 31 to 34 percent of income [D2p2]. Mrs Wong, nine years in a unit with no written agreement, says the 2021 ordinance "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. Mr Chan's landlord evicted him with two weeks' notice for requesting a window repair [D3p1]. Something plainly needs to change.

But the archive distinguishes two kinds of tightening, and only one is defensible. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance was second-generation: it capped increases within a tenancy but left initial rents untouched [D1p3]. The academic literature finds first-generation controls (hard caps on levels) show consistent supply contraction, while second-generation controls show small or null stock effects [D6p2]. The stable SDU stock — 100,800 units in 2019 rising to 111,200 in 2025, through the ordinance [D2p1] — is therefore evidence that *moderate* regulation is survivable, not that a binding cap is safe. As one think-tank piece notes, that stability may be "an artifact of a non-binding instrument" [D12p2].

The contraction arithmetic for a hard cap is uncomfortable. The reviewed elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply is 0.31–0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. Modelled for a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent initial-rent cut transfers about HK$900 a month to incumbents while leaving 3,000–4,000 households a year unable to enter the segment — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin is the cautionary case: initial-offer rents fell, listing supply contracted within a year, and when the Mietendeckel was struck down a backlog of suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend line [D7p1][D7p2]. The tenants a cap protects are those already inside; the bill goes to those still outside [D7p3].

The enforcement problem is equally decisive. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and a band-based initial-rent cap would require a certifiable tenancy register costing an estimated 40–60 staff-years [D10p1]. Worse, the tenants least able to complain — those with no written agreement, like Mrs Wong [D4p1], or the newest arrivals — are precisely the ones enforcement would miss, so a cap would "systematically miss the most exploited tail" [D10p2]. The subcommittee could not identify any design under which the newest-arrival tail is protected first rather than last [D10p3]. A hard cap also invites compensation claims and judicial review that would outlast a Council term [D9p1][D9p3].

What, then? Three measures fit the evidence. First, tenure security. The NGO survey of 440 households found 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. Mr Chan himself, offered a hard cap, named the waiting list instead: "If the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave" [D3p3]. Second, a light indexed band on initial rents — defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2] — accepting that it will not reach the most exploited tail, and pairing it with the register investment that makes enforcement possible. Third, redirect enforcement capacity to conditions: fire, partition, ventilation, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2].

The deeper point is that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem: the segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2]. Mrs Wong's confidence that landlords "would not leave the business" [D4p2] sits awkwardly beside her own report of two flats converted to storage in her stairwell [D4p3] — and the Association, representing 18,000 units, commits to exit if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p2], albeit on thin, self-reported evidence [D5p3]. The government's expected "floor-standard + rent band" package [D12p1] is the right shape. Tighten tenure and conditions; band initial rents lightly; and treat shortening the five-and-a-half-year queue [D1p2] as the policy that actually houses people. A hard cap would help those already inside at the expense of those still outside — the very households the policy claims to protect.
