Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, but it should do it in stages and not with a hard cap on rents. First build a tenancy register and survey data, then give tenants security of tenure, and only then add a published band on initial rents. When I started, my instinct was a simple "yes, cap the rents". The evidence changed that into something more careful, but it did not change the basic answer.

The reason to act is that subdivided unit tenants have very little power. About 110,000 households live in these units, and they pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot compared with ordinary flats [D1p1]. They are mostly new arrivals, single parents and people waiting for public housing, for whom the unit is a leftover option while the queue moves [D1p2]. The one rule we have, the 2021 ordinance, limits rent increases inside a tenancy but does not cap the initial rent [D1p3]. The numbers show tenants are still squeezed. Rents in these units grew more slowly than general rents after 2021, but the share of income spent on rent still rose from 31 to 34 percent [D2p2]. Mrs Wong, who has no written agreement, says the ordinance changed nothing she can see [D4p1].

The strongest objection is from the landlords. Their association says a hard cap is confiscatory and that members will leave the business if returns fall below mortgage cost [D5p1] [D5p2]. I take the threat seriously, but I trust it less because the same submission admits it has no data on members' real margins, and its cost figures come from a self-reported survey with a 34 percent response rate [D5p3]. The wider evidence is mixed in a useful way. A working paper estimates that hard caps on rent levels remove roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock for every 10 percent rent cut, and the people who lose units are the same poor households the cap is meant to protect [D6p1] [D6p3]. That is why I do not support a hard cap. But the same paper says controls that only limit increases inside tenancies have small or null effects on stock [D6p2], and the data agree, since stock rose from 107,300 in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025 [D2p1].

A second objection comes from an NGO report. It surveyed 440 households and found that 61 percent would trade a rent rise for three years of security of tenure [D11p1]. It says rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem, because the queue is long, and that inspection effort is better spent on fire and ventilation [D11p2]. I accept most of this. Rent control cannot fix a waiting time of around five and a half years [D1p2], and faster public housing is the real long-term answer. This is why my plan puts tenure security first. But I do not accept that rent level does not matter. The survey asks about a trade, which is not the same as saying rent is unimportant, and the burden rising to 34 percent shows rent still hurts [D2p2].

The hardest problem is enforcement, and it is why I say "in stages". A draft internal minute, marked not for circulation and so not final policy, says inspection covers under 6 percent of the stock each year and that a band on initial rents needs a certifiable register that would take 40 to 60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. Even so, its worst warning fits what I saw elsewhere. If tenants must complain to enforce the rule, then the newest arrivals and those with no written agreement will be missed first [D10p2].

The law adds a second reason for stages. Counsel thinks a hard cap on initial rents would likely lead to compensation claims on property rights, while the 2021 style sits on firmer ground but leaves the initial rent unregulated [D9p1]. A published, indexed band is more defensible, but it needs a survey system that does not exist yet [D9p2]. So the register and the survey data are not only an enforcement tool, they are also what makes a band legal and workable.

My position is therefore this. Step one is to build the register and survey data, which also helps inspections. Step two is to give tenants stronger security of tenure, which they say they want. Step three is to bring in a band on initial rents once the data and enforcement can support it. If the register cannot be built, the band should wait and not be passed as an empty rule. But a cap that shrinks the stock, loses in court, or cannot be enforced would leave the poorest tenants worse off.
