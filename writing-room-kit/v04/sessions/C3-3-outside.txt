**Tighten Tenure, Not Rent: A Qualified Case Against a Hard Cap on Subdivided Units**

Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) by capping initial rents. It should instead tighten the *dimension tenants actually name*: security of tenure. The archive supports a narrow intervention—mandatory written tenancies with minimum notice periods, plus a light, indexed band on initial rents—while rejecting a hard cap. The case rests on three findings: a hard cap would contract the stock at the bottom; it would protect incumbents at the expense of the newest arrivals; and the binding constraint tenants themselves identify is queue length and insecurity, not price.

First, the supply evidence. The 2019–2025 index shows SDU stock *growing* through the 2021 ordinance, from 100,800 to 111,200 units [D2p1]. But this stability is not proof that control is costless. The 2021 instrument capped increases within tenancies, not initial rents [D1p3], and the academic literature distinguishes precisely these cases: second-generation controls show small or null stock effects, while first-generation hard caps show "consistent contraction effects" [D6p2]. The stable stock is therefore evidence that a *non-binding* instrument did not bite—not that a binding one would be benign. The estimated elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply is 0.31–0.48, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3–5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent initial-rent cut transfers about $900 monthly to incumbents while 3,000–4,000 households a year—"mostly new arrivals and newly formed households"—cannot enter the segment at all [D6p4]. Berlin illustrates the mechanism: capped initial offers fell, listing supply contracted within a year, and when the law fell, suppressed increases released above the pre-cap trend [D7p1][D7p2]. The bill for protecting insiders is handed to outsiders [D7p3].

Second, the distributional problem is not incidental; it is the core. The enforcement minute is unusually candid: the subcommittee "could not identify any enforcement design under which the newest-arrival tail—the population named in D1—is protected first rather than last" [D10p3]. Inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of stock annually, and the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement [D10p2], exactly Mrs Wong's situation after nine years [D4p1]. A hard cap would thus be enforced most reliably against the most documented landlords and least reliably for the most exploited tenants. Mr Chan's fear—that landlords would rather convert cubicles to storage than deal with regulated tenants—is not hypothetical: he has seen two units on his floor emptied and locked for a year [D3p2], and Mrs Wong reports two flats in her stairwell converted to non-residential storage even as she insists landlords cannot exit [D4p3]. The Landlords Association's threat to exit below mortgage cost is self-interested and its margin data is thin—a 2022 survey of 210 members with a 34 percent response rate, self-reported [D5p3]—but the direction of the risk is corroborated by the academic and comparative evidence.

Third, tenants themselves rank the alternatives differently than the policy debate does. Mr Chan, asked what would help most, "does not name rent. He names the waiting list"—if the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave [D3p3]. The NGO survey of 440 households finds 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year tenure security and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. This is a revealed preference against treating price as the primary lever. Rent caps address "the price symptom of a quantity problem": the segment is tight because the queue is long [D11p2]. Every enforcement dollar spent policing rents is a dollar not spent on fire, partition and ventilation conditions, "where harm is documented and immediate" [D11p2].

None of this argues for doing nothing. The 2021 ordinance left the actual pain point—the initial rent—untouched, and Mrs Wong reports it "changed nothing she can see" [D4p1]. A hard cap, however, would likely trigger compensation claims or judicial review, and litigation alone would outlast a Council term [D9p1][D9p3]. The defensible middle instrument is the one counsel and the consultation both gesture toward: a published, indexed per-square-foot band on initial rents, framed as rate-capping rather than confiscation [D9p2], paired with a certifiable tenancy register. That register is the precondition for tenure security, and its cost—40 to 60 staff-years [D10p1]—is the price of enforcement that reaches the newest-arrival tail rather than missing it.

The recommendation, then: tighten tenure, not rent. Mandate written agreements and minimum notice; build the register; adopt a reviewable initial-rent band, not a hard cap; and treat public-housing throughput as the primary instrument, since that is what tenants say would change their lives. A hard cap would buy incumbents $900 a month and sell out the households still outside the door.
