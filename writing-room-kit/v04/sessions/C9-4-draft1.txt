**Tighten Carefully, but Don't Cap the First Rent**

The question of whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) is really two questions: should the government regulate rents more aggressively, and if so, which instrument? The archive points toward a defensible position: yes, tighten—but through tenure security and a band-based cap on initial rents, not a hard cap on rent levels. A hard cap would likely help today's tenants while shrinking the stock that tomorrow's tenants depend on, and it would systematically fail the most vulnerable households it claims to protect.

Start with the strongest argument for tightening: the status quo is not working. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents untouched and excluded co-living SDUs from key provisions [D1p3]. Mrs Wong's experience illustrates the gap: nine years in an SDU, no written tenancy agreement, and a landlord who "still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears" [D4p1]. Real rent burden for SDU households worsened from 31 to 34 percent even under the ordinance [D2p2]. The instrument that exists regulates the wrong margin.

But the case for a hard cap collapses under the supply evidence. The academic literature estimates long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48—a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The modelled Hong Kong scenario is starker: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars per month to incumbents while leaving 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, "mostly new arrivals and newly formed households" [D6p4]. Those excluded are precisely the population named in D1: new arrivals, single-parent households, public-housing waiters [D1p2]. The cap protects insiders and bills outsiders.
