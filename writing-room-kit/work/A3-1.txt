**Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units?**

Roughly 110,000 households live in subdivided units (SDUs) in Hong Kong. Median SDU rent is about 30 percent below mainstream private flats, yet per square foot tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium, because space is carved and priced at its most desperate margin. [D1p1] Any tightening raises the classic rent-economics question: does capping initial rent help tenants, or shrink the stock they depend on? [D1p4] The answer depends on supply elasticity, enforcement capacity, and what happens to households if units exit the market. On balance, Hong Kong should not impose a hard cap on initial SDU rents now; it should tighten enforcement of conditions and build the survey infrastructure for a rate-band instrument, while treating the queue for public housing as the real quantity problem. The case for capping initial rent is real. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent level unregulated, and excluded SDUs with co-living services from key provisions. [D1p3] That leaves the actual pain point — the rent a desperate household pays on day one — untouched. Tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households, and residents waiting for public housing, where average waiting time has hovered around five and a half years. For them the SDU is not a choice but a residual option while the queue moves. A cap on initial rent would target precisely the margin where exploitation is sharpest. But Berlin is the cautionary tale. Its 2020 cap on roughly 1.5 million flats made reported initial-offer rents for new listings fall sharply — and then classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year, as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments. [D7p1] Hong Kong's SDU market is far more elastic in the exit direction: partitions are cheap to remove, units can be sold, recombined, or converted to other uses, and landlords face few sunk costs. A hard cap on initial rent would likely shrink the very stock tenants depend on, pushing households toward worse options: illegal partitions, further overcrowding, or the street. New York's second-generation scheme, which covers roughly 44 percent of rental stock, caps increases within tenancies while lightly regulating initial rents and vacancy — and even there, vacancy decontrol only ended in 2019. [D8p1] That is not a model Hong Kong can transplant overnight. There is also a legal constraint. A hard cap on initial SDU rents would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's protection of property rights if framed as a deprivation of the rental income stream. A cap framed as contractual-term regulation, like the 2021 ordinance, sits on firmer ground — but leaves the initial rent unregulated. Counsel's proposed middle instrument, capping initial rent by reference to a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, could be defended as rate-capping rather than confiscation. The problem is that bands require a survey infrastructure that does not currently exist. Imposing a band without that data risks setting the rate too low and triggering the Berlin exit, or too high and achieving nothing. Meanwhile, every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting conditions — fire, partition, ventilation — where harm is documented and immediate. [D11p2] The segment is tight because the queue is long, not because prices are unregulated. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem. The better path is sequenced. First, tighten enforcement of habitability and fire-safety standards, and close the co-living loophole in the 2021 ordinance, so existing protections actually bite. Second, build the survey infrastructure — transaction data, unit registries, condition inspections — that any defensible band would require. Third, expand transitional housing and accelerate public-housing supply, because five and a half years of waiting is what makes the SDU margin so desperate. Only once data and supply-side measures are in place should Hong Kong consider a published per-square-foot band on initial rents, and even then as a rate cap, not a confiscation. Tightening rent control without those prerequisites would help some tenants today at the cost of leaving fewer units for the tenants who come next.
