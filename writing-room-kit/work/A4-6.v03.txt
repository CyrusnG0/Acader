# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Hong Kong's subdivided units (SDUs) sit at the sharp end of the city's housing crisis: the Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped increases within an existing tenancy but left initial rents uncapped and carved out SDUs with co-living services. [D1p3] The question of whether to tighten further turns on a deceptively simple trade-off: does capping the initial rent help tenants, or does it shrink the very stock they depend on? [D1p4] Standard theory predicts supply contraction under binding rent ceilings, but the size of that contraction is an empirical question, not a matter of doctrine. The reviewed literature estimates the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels at 0.31 to 0.48 in marginal stock—units already at the bottom of the market. [D6p1] That means a cap-induced rent reduction of 10 percent removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years. This is a real cost, and it falls on the same low-income households the policy is meant to protect. Berlin's experience is instructive: after rents on ~1.5 million flats were capped in 2020, reported initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply, but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments. [D7p1] The lesson is not that rent control is futile, but that a cap on initial rents is only as good as the stock it governs—and marginal stock is the most mobile. Yet the case for tightening does not collapse on this evidence. The 2021 Ordinance's exclusion of SDUs with co-living services, and its failure to cap initial rents, means the instrument currently misses precisely the segment where exploitation is most acute. Enforcement, however, is the deeper problem. The subcommittee's own modelling note records a risk the public consultation omits: if landlords reprice above-band initial rents, the first instrument tenants hold is complaint—but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement or the newest arrivals. [D10p2] Enforcement would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail. A tighter cap that cannot be enforced on the most vulnerable tenants risks being symbolic: it may suppress reported rents while pushing the real market further into informal, unrecorded arrangements. The sensible answer is therefore conditional and sequenced, not a simple yes or no. First, tighten where enforcement is feasible: extend the cap to initial rents for SDUs with written agreements and strengthen the tenancy registry, so that the complaint mechanism actually reaches the tenants it is designed to protect. Second, pair any cap with supply-side measures—transitional housing, land release, and incentives for compliant landlords—so that the 3 to 5 percent five-year stock loss is offset rather than simply borne by tenants. Third, treat the co-living carve-out as a loophole to close, not a permanent exemption, but only once the enforcement apparatus can police it. A blanket tightening without these safeguards would repeat Berlin's mistake at a smaller scale: lower headline rents, thinner marginal stock, and a shift into informal arrangements that leave the worst-off tenants exactly where they started. A targeted, enforced, and supply-accompanied tightening, by contrast, could improve conditions for the tenants who most need protection without destroying the stock they depend on. The question is not whether to regulate, but how to regulate in a way that survives contact with the market it governs.
