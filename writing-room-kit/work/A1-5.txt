Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

My position is that Hong Kong should not adopt a hard cap on initial rents for subdivided units (SDUs) at this stage. It should tighten only in a narrower way, building the enforcement capacity first. The pain is real, but the evidence in the course archive suggests that a blunt cap would hurt the people it is meant to protect.

The case for acting is strong. About 110,000 households live in SDUs, and although the median rent is about 30 percent below mainstream private flats, tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium per square foot [D1p1]. That premium is the clearest sign of a market where space is priced at its most desperate margin. Mrs Wong says the 2021 ordinance changed nothing she can see, and she supports a hard cap on initial rents [D4p2]. Her view deserves weight, because the existing law is weak at exactly the point where tenants feel the pain: the Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped increases within an existing tenancy but did not cap the initial rent [D1p3]. So there is a gap between the current rules and what tenants experience.

However, the archive gives me three reasons to doubt that a hard cap closes that gap well.

First, the supply risk. The primer frames the central question as whether capping the initial rent helps tenants or shrinks the very stock they depend on [D1p4]. Berlin is a warning. Its cap made initial-offer rents for new listings fall sharply, but listing supply then contracted within a year as owners withheld flats or moved them to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. SDU tenants are already at the bottom of the market, so if units exit, the households who lose out are the ones the policy was written for. I should be honest that Berlin is a very different market, with about 1.5 million flats covered, so it is a caution rather than a proof. Still, the mechanism it shows, that cheaper new listings coincide with fewer listings, is the one that matters here.

Second, the comparison with New York cuts the other way and shows what kind of control seems safer. New York's stabilisation, a scheme that regulates within tenancies, shows only modest stock contraction because it is light at first rent, which is the same point Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance left untouched [D8p2]. This implies that Hong Kong's stable experience under its current law may not tell us what happens under a binding cap on first rent. The place where tenants want more protection is also the place where the risk to supply is greatest. I cannot wave this tension away, and any honest position has to admit it.

Third, and most decisive for me, is enforcement. An internal minute records that a tightened cap is only as real as its inspection regime. Current inspection covers under 6 percent of SDU stock per year, and a band-based initial-rent cap would need a certifiable tenancy register that the Rating and Valuation Department estimates would take 40 to 60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. A cap that cannot be policed becomes a rule on paper. Worse, a rule that depends on tenants complaining is likely to favour those with documents and bargaining power, though the passages I received do not test this directly.

There is also a point about incentives. Mrs Wong believes landlords cannot leave the business because the units are their pension [D4p2]. That may be true of some owners, and it is a fair argument that a cap would not drive everyone out. But it is one tenant's belief about her stairwell, not data about owner margins, so I treat it as a reason for caution about the worst-case predictions rather than as proof that supply is safe.

What would I do instead? I would tighten in stages. Start with the machinery: a tenancy register and more inspection capacity, since the minute shows these are the binding constraints [D10p1]. Extend the protection that already works in lighter schemes, namely limits within tenancies and written agreements, since these sit on the side of the line where New York shows modest contraction [D8p2]. Only then consider a modest band on initial rents, ideally piloted, so that Hong Kong learns from Berlin's experience of falling supply [D7p1] without repeating it.

In short, the problem described in the primer is serious, but a hard cap on initial rents is the wrong first tool. The sensible path is a slower tightening that builds enforcement first, protects tenancies, and treats initial-rent limits as a later and carefully tested step.
