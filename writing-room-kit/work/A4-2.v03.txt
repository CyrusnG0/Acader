# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Any answer must begin with what tightening means. The Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent free and carved out SDUs with co-living services. [D1p3] A "hard cap" on initial rents is therefore a different instrument from the one already in force, and it is the one that carries the Berlin risk: when the controlled price sits below the market-clearing price, owners withhold or reroute units. [D1p4] Berlin's 2020 freeze on roughly 1.5 million flats produced exactly that pattern—initial asking rents fell, and classified-listing supply contracted within a year. [D7p1] The reviewed literature on marginal stock puts the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48, implying that a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of units over five years. [D6p1] In a segment where every unit is someone's home, that is not a rounding error. But elasticity is an empirical question, and Hong Kong's SDU market has features that cut against the standard prediction. The woman who supports a hard cap makes the strongest point in the debate: her building's four SDU landlords would not exit, because the units are their pension and there is nowhere else for the capital to go. [D4p2] If supply is genuinely inelastic—if owners have no alternative use and no alternative asset—then a cap redistributes rather than destroys. The Chief Executive's consultation document nonetheless leans toward a "floor-standard + rent band" package rather than a hard cap, and that instinct is sound. [D12p1] A band that limits how far initial rents can exceed comparable prevailing rents, paired with the existing in-tenancy cap, captures most of the tenant-protection gain while leaving less room for the withholding response that Berlin demonstrated. [D8p1] The deeper problem is that rent control treats the price symptom of a quantity problem. The segment is tight because the queue is long. The tenant who would "accept any rent to leave" if the waiting list fell from five and a half years to three is telling us where the binding constraint lies. [D3p3] Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire escapes, partitions and ventilation—harms that are documented and immediate, unlike the speculative long-run supply loss that dominates the theoretical case against caps. [D11p2] That asymmetry matters. A tenant who is overcharged is poorer; a tenant in a unit with a blocked fire exit may be dead. The honest position is therefore conditional and incremental. Hong Kong should tighten rent control, but not to a hard cap on initial rents. It should extend the 2021 Ordinance's in-tenancy protection to cover SDUs currently excluded for co-living services, tighten the permitted increase rate, and add a rent band on initial offers that is enforced through the same inspection regime that already monitors conditions—not through a separate rent police. It should simultaneously and visibly expand public housing supply, because the rent band is a tourniquet and the waiting list is the wound. If the band proves enforceable without measurable exit over three years, it can be tightened further. If units start disappearing, the band is too tight and should be loosened before the stock loss compounds. The Berlin lesson is not that rent control always fails. It is that a cap set without regard to supply response, in a market where owners have somewhere else to put their flats, produces the shortage it was meant to cure. Hong Kong's SDU landlords may have nowhere else to go—but "may" is not a basis for policy. A band, an inspection regime and a housing pipeline are. The hard cap can wait until the evidence justifies it. The tenants cannot.
