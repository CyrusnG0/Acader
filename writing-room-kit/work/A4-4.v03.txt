**Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?**

About 110,000 Hong Kong households live in subdivided units — flats carved into cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms. Median SDU rent runs roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats, yet per square foot tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium, because space is carved and priced at its most desperate margin. [D1p1] The question of whether to tighten rent control on this segment is therefore not abstract. It is a question about what actually helps the people living in it. The case for tightening rests on an obvious injustice: the poorest households pay the highest unit price for the worst housing. A hard cap on initial rents would immediately lower the entry price, and Berlin's experience shows that capped initial-offer rents do fall sharply. But Berlin also supplies the cautionary half of the story. Within a year of its 2020 cap on roughly 1.5 million flats, classified-listing supply contracted measurably as owners withheld units or rerouted them to uncontrolled segments. [D7p1] The mechanism is standard: under binding ceilings, some landlords exit or convert, and the tenants who lose are precisely those at the bottom of the queue. How large would that exit be in Hong Kong? The reviewed literature estimates the long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels at 0.31 to 0.48. A 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction would therefore remove roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years — several thousand units, in a market where queue length is already the binding constraint. [D6p1] That estimate is not a reason to do nothing, but it is a reason to be precise about what is capped. Precision matters because Hong Kong's 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance was deliberately light at the first-rent margin: it capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent level untouched, and excluded SDUs with co-living services from key provisions. [D1p3] The evidence since then is instructive. Estimated SDU stock rose from 100,800 units in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025. Growth continued through the ordinance, suggesting that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit. [D2p1] This is consistent with the Berlin finding that stock contraction is modest when a scheme is light at first-rent — and it is the exact margin that any tightening would now touch. [D8p2] Here the survey evidence becomes decisive. Across 440 SDU households, the binding constraint tenants name is not rent level but queue length and tenancy insecurity: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer. [D11p1] Tenants are telling policymakers that their deepest problem is not the price of the unit but the risk of being moved, and the wait for an exit. A hard cap addresses neither. It may even worsen both, by shrinking the stock that absorbs households while they queue. The better answer is a targeted tightening that stops short of a hard initial-rent cap. [D1p4] A floor-standard plus rent band — the package the consultation document is expected to float — can constrain the worst per-square-foot premiums without setting a ceiling that pushes marginal landlords out. [D12p1] Pairing it with enforceable security of tenure, so that the 61 percent who would trade rent for stability actually get it, addresses the constraint tenants themselves identify. And accelerating public-housing throughput speaks to the 74 percent. Berlin's void order (D7) is cited in the policy agenda as a cautionary tale for good reason: a cap that looks generous on announcement can quietly reduce the stock it was meant to protect. So the answer is a qualified no to tightening in the blunt sense, and a qualified yes to tightening smartly. Hong Kong should not cap initial SDU rents, because the elasticity evidence and Berlin's experience both warn that the stock loss lands on the poorest. It should instead tighten standards, band rents within a defensible range, and legislate tenure security — measures that relieve the constraint tenants actually report, without giving marginal landlords a reason to exit. The goal is not a lower number on a lease. It is a shorter queue and a safer tenancy.
