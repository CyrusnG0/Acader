# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Any tightening of rent control raises the classic question of rent economics: does capping the initial rent help tenants, or does it shrink the very stock they depend on? [D1p4] The answer depends on supply elasticity, enforcement capacity, and what happens to households if units exit the market. Hong Kong's experience with the 2021 tenancy control ordinance offers a rare natural experiment—and its early results suggest that moderate regulation has not triggered visible stock exit. Yet the case for tightening is weaker than it first appears, because the binding constraint is not rent but the waiting list, and because caps bind differently on incumbents and new entrants. Start with supply. If rent control shrinks the stock of subdivided units (SDUs), tenants lose more than they gain. The evidence so far is reassuring: estimated SDU stock rose from 100,800 units in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023, and 111,200 in 2025. Growth continued through the 2021 ordinance, suggesting that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit. [D2p1] That is a meaningful data point against the strongest objection to tightening. But it is not decisive. The stock could be growing because overall housing scarcity is so severe that even capped rents remain profitable, or because enforcement is too weak to bite. If tightening is aggressive enough to change landlord behavior, the elasticity that currently looks benign could turn hostile. Enforcement is the second hinge. Mrs Wong, 67, retired, has lived in an SDU for nine years and pays $4,200. Her tenancy predates the 2021 ordinance. She has never received a written tenancy agreement, and says the ordinance changed nothing she can see: the landlord still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears. [D4p1] Mrs Wong's experience is the central problem. A cap that exists on paper but cannot be enforced against informal tenancies protects no one. Tightening rent control without a credible enforcement mechanism—written agreements, rent registers, penalties that landlords actually fear—would mostly generate symbolic victories. Worse, it could push landlords further into informality, making tenants more vulnerable rather than less. The third hinge is distributional. The Berlin lesson for the brief is that caps bind differently on new entrants than incumbents: the tenants the cap protects are those already inside, and the bill is handed to those still outside. [D7p3] In Hong Kong, the incumbents are people like Mrs Wong, whose $4,200 rent is already below market. Tightening initial rent caps would mainly help new entrants—but only if units remain available to them. If landlords respond by converting units, selling, or reserving them for tenants who can pay side payments, the queue outside gets longer, not shorter. That is why the most revealing answer in the readings comes not from Mrs Wong but from the tenant who, asked what would help most, does not name rent. He names the waiting list. If the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave. [D3p3] This is the crux. For households trapped in SDUs, rent control treats a symptom; the disease is the shortage of public rental housing and the length of the queue. A tenant who would pay any rent to escape is not primarily a rent-control problem. He is a supply problem. So should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? A qualified yes—but only as part of a package, and only if tightening is paired with enforcement capacity and a credible exit path. Tightening alone risks protecting incumbents while lengthening the queue for newcomers. The better sequence is: enforce existing rules so that Mrs Wong's landlord cannot raise rent arbitrarily; expand public housing supply so the waiting list shortens; and tighten caps only where the stock evidence and enforcement capacity show that regulation will not shrink the units tenants depend on. Rent control can buy time. It cannot build homes.
