**Title: Beyond the Symptom: Why Hong Kong Should Not Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units**

The debate over rent control in Hong Kong's subdivided units (SDUs) is a study in compassionate economics: how do we protect the most vulnerable without destroying the fragile market they rely on? [D1p1] While the impulse to tighten rent control is morally compelling, the evidence suggests that a hard cap on initial rents would be a policy error. Instead, Hong Kong should focus on targeted enforcement of existing regulations and accelerating public housing supply, addressing the root cause rather than the price symptom. The central argument against tightening rent control is that it treats a quantity problem with a price solution. The SDU market is tight not because rents are unregulated, but because the queue for public housing is long—hovering around five and a half years. For tenants—disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households, and those waiting for public housing—the SDU is not a choice but a residual option. [D1p2] Artificially suppressing rents does not shorten the queue; it risks shrinking the very stock these households depend on. [D1p4] Hong Kong's recent experience is instructive. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance, a second-generation control that capped rent increases within existing tenancies but left initial rents unregulated, did not trigger visible stock exit. [D2p1] SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, continuing its upward trajectory through the ordinance's implementation. This stability aligns with international evidence: second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock, while first-generation controls—hard caps on rent levels—consistently produce contraction. [D6p2] Berlin's 2020 rent cap offers a cautionary tale. When the city capped rents on approximately 1.5 million flats, reported initial-offer rents fell sharply, but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments. [D7p1] The lesson is clear: when regulation targets the price symptom without addressing supply, the market adapts in ways that harm the very tenants it aims to protect. A hard cap on initial SDU rents would also face significant legal hurdles. Under the Basic Law's protection of property rights, such a cap could attract compensation claims if framed as a deprivation of rental income stream. [D9p1] A cap framed as contractual-term regulation, like the 2021 ordinance, sits on firmer legal ground but leaves the initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated. This legal constraint is not merely a technicality; it reflects a deeper principle: policy must balance tenant protection with property rights in a way that is sustainable and enforceable. The enforcement argument is equally compelling. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent on inspecting conditions—fire safety, partition stability, ventilation—where harm is documented and immediate. [D11p2] SDU tenants face not only high rents but also substandard living conditions. Tightening rent control would divert scarce resources from these urgent health and safety concerns, leaving tenants in safer but equally unaffordable housing, or worse, in unsafe housing with no rent relief. What, then, should Hong Kong do? First, it should strengthen enforcement of existing tenancy controls and building safety regulations, ensuring that the 2021 ordinance is fully implemented and that landlords cannot evade its provisions by excluding SDUs with co-living services. [D1p3] Second, it should accelerate public housing supply, addressing the root cause of the SDU queue. Third, it should explore targeted subsidies or transitional housing for the most vulnerable tenants, rather than broad-brush rent caps that risk unintended consequences. In conclusion, tightening rent control on SDUs would be a well-intentioned but misguided policy. The evidence from Hong Kong's own experience and from international cases like Berlin suggests that hard caps on initial rents risk shrinking supply, diverting enforcement resources, and facing legal challenges—all without addressing the fundamental shortage of affordable housing. The compassionate path is not to cap the price symptom but to treat the quantity problem: build more public housing, enforce safety standards, and support the most vulnerable while the queue moves. Only then can Hong Kong claim to have truly protected its SDU tenants.
