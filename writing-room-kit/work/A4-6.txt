# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Hong Kong's roughly 110,000 subdivided unit (SDU) households sit at the sharp end of the city's housing crisis. The Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 was a first step, but its design limits are now well documented: it caps increases within an existing tenancy while leaving the initial rent unregulated, and it carves out SDUs with co-living services from key provisions. [D1p3] The question is whether tightening — capping initial rents, closing exclusions — would help the tenants it targets, or shrink the stock they depend on. [D1p4] The honest answer is that tightening is justified, but only as a bounded intervention paired with enforcement capacity, not as a standalone fix. The strongest argument against tightening is supply contraction. Standard theory predicts that binding rent ceilings reduce supply, and the reviewed literature offers a concrete estimate for this marginal segment: a long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels of 0.31 to 0.48. [D6p1] That means a cap-induced 10 percent rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years. Berlin's 2020 freeze on about 1.5 million flats is the cautionary case: initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply, but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld units or rerouted them to uncontrolled segments. [D7p1] In a market where SDUs are already the bottom rung, a 3 to 5 percent contraction is not an abstraction — it is several thousand households pushed toward worse options, including illegal structures or homelessness. Yet the status quo is not a neutral baseline. The 2021 Ordinance's failure to cap initial rents means a landlord can lawfully reset rent to any level at each new tenancy, which converts the within-tenancy cap into a weak constraint. Tenants who fear re-letting cannot bargain, and the ordinance's exclusions for co-living SDUs create an obvious incentive to rebrand units to escape coverage. The current regime therefore delivers the appearance of protection without the substance, and the households bearing that gap are precisely those with the least voice. This is where enforcement capacity becomes decisive rather than secondary. The subcommittee's own modelling note identifies a risk the public consultation omits: if landlords reprice above-band initial rents, the tenant's first instrument is complaint — but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement or the newest arrivals. [D10p2] Enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail. A tightened cap that cannot be enforced does not merely fail; it displaces exploitation into less visible forms, such as informal side-payments or undocumented occupancy, while leaving the compliant landlord bearing the cost. The defensible position is therefore conditional tightening. First, cap initial rents, but set the ceiling with reference to the measured elasticity — a moderate cap that avoids the sharpest reductions, rather than a Berlin-style freeze. Second, close the co-living exclusion, since it is a designed loophole rather than a genuine category difference. Third, and most importantly, pair the cap with enforcement infrastructure aimed at the informal tail: proactive registration, complaint channels that do not require a written tenancy, and penalties calibrated to deter evasion rather than merely to exist on paper. Fourth, treat the policy as transitional, with the cap easing as SDU-grade supply expands through land and planning measures. Tightening rent control on SDUs is the right direction, but not because rent caps are costless. It is right because the current half-measure already imposes costs on tenants while delivering little protection, and because the supply risk can be managed if the cap is moderate and enforcement is real. A cap without enforcement is theatre; enforcement without a cap leaves the initial-rent loophole open. Hong Kong should tighten — carefully, and with the administrative machinery to make the tightening mean something for the tenants who cannot complain.
