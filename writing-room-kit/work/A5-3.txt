# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Any answer to this question has to begin with a distinction that the evidence makes unavoidable: first-generation rent controls — hard caps on the level of rent — consistently shrink housing supply, while second-generation controls, which cap increases within tenancies, show small or null effects on stock. [D6p2] Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is second-generation. That is why the D2 index shows a stable stock of subdivided units despite the regulation, and it is the single most important fact in deciding whether to tighten. The case for tightening starts with the desperation premium. Median SDU rent sits roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats, but per square foot, SDU tenants pay 15 to 25 percent more. [D1p1] That premium is not a market reward for quality; it is a price extracted at the most desperate margin. Roughly 110,000 households — people living in cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms — are paying more per unit of space precisely because they have nowhere else to go. A regime that caps increases within tenancies does little for a new tenant signing a fresh lease, because the cap applies to the renewal, not the initial rent. Tightening could mean extending protections to new tenancies, capping the initial rent against comparable market rates, or strengthening enforcement so that landlords cannot circumvent the rules through service charges and fees. Each of these would directly address the premium that defines the SDU market. The case against tightening is the supply response. The reason second-generation controls look harmless in the data is that they leave the landlord's exit option intact: a landlord who cannot raise rent mid-tenancy can still repossess at the end of the term, sell, or convert the unit back to a whole flat. Tighten too far — cap initial rents, restrict repossessions, extend security of tenure indefinitely — and the ordinance starts behaving like a first-generation control. The stock contracts. Landlords withdraw subdivided units from the market, and the 110,000 households do not disappear; they are pushed into worse options: illegal partitions, industrial buildings, or the street. The stable D2 stock is not evidence that controls are costless. It is evidence that the current controls are mild enough to avoid the contraction effect. The honest answer, then, is a qualified yes — tighten, but only along the second-generation margin. The policy should target the desperation premium without triggering exit. Concretely: cap the rent on new tenancies at a benchmark tied to comparable private stock, not at whatever the landlord can extract; require standardised leases and transparent service charges so the effective rent is visible; strengthen enforcement with a dedicated SDU regulation office; and pair the controls with a credible supply-side commitment — transitional housing, public rental expansion, and incentives for landlords to register and upgrade rather than exit. The goal is to compress the premium, not to abolish the market. What Hong Kong should not do is tighten so aggressively that the ordinance crosses into first-generation territory. The evidence is clear that hard caps on levels contract supply, and the people who bear that contraction are the tenants the policy is meant to help. A rent control regime that empties the SDU market is not a victory for the poor; it is a transfer of suffering from high rent to no housing at all. So: tighten the second-generation controls to close the new-tenancy loophole and the enforcement gap, but leave the landlord's exit option intact. That is the narrow path between exploitation and contraction — and given 110,000 households living in cubicles, it is the only path worth taking.
