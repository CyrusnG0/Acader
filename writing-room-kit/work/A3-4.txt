# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Approximately 110,000 households in Hong Kong live in subdivided units (SDUs)—flats carved into small cubicles, often with shared kitchens and bathrooms. Median SDU rent runs roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats, but per square foot, SDU tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent, because space is carved and priced at its most desperate margin. [D1p1] Any tightening of rent control therefore raises the classic question of rent economics: does capping the initial rent help tenants, or does it shrink the very stock they depend on? [D1p4] The evidence suggests that the answer depends on the type of control. First-generation controls—hard caps on rent levels—show consistent contraction effects. Berlin capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats in 2020. Reported initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply, but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments. [D7p1] Hong Kong's own experience points the other way. Estimated SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023, and 111,200 in 2025. Growth continued through the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance, suggesting that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit. [D2p1] The distinction matters: first-generation controls cap levels; second-generation controls cap increases within tenancies. First-generation controls show consistent contraction effects; second-generation controls show small or null effects on stock. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation, which explains the stable stock. This is precisely why a hard cap on initial SDU rents would be a mistake. It would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's protection of property rights if framed as a deprivation of rental income stream. [D9p1] A cap framed as contractual-term regulation, like the 2021 ordinance, sits on firmer ground but leaves the initial rent—the actual pain point—unregulated. Tightening in the first-generation sense would thus risk both legal challenge and stock contraction, without addressing the initial rent that tenants actually pay. There is also a deeper problem with treating rent caps as the solution. Rent control treats the price symptom of a quantity problem: the segment is tight because the queue is long. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent on inspection of conditions—fire, partition, ventilation—where harm is documented and immediate. [D11p2] SDU tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households, and residents waiting for public housing, where average waiting time has hovered around five and a half years. For these households the SDU is not a choice but a residual option while the queue moves. Their most urgent needs are safety and supply, not a marginally lower initial rent that may never materialize if units exit the market. That said, doing nothing is not tenable either. The 2021 ordinance excluded SDUs with co-living services from key provisions, leaving a loophole that weakens enforcement. A better path is to strengthen second-generation controls—tightening caps on increases within tenancies, closing the co-living exclusion, and pairing regulation with expanded public housing and transitional housing supply. This approach preserves the stable stock that second-generation controls have delivered while channelling enforcement capacity toward conditions inspection. Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on SDUs in the first-generation sense of capping initial rents. The Berlin evidence warns of contraction; the Basic Law warns of compensation claims; and the enforcement trade-off warns of neglected safety hazards. Instead, Hong Kong should deepen the second-generation approach it already has: close exemptions, enforce conditions, and above all, shorten the public housing queue that makes SDUs a residual option in the first place. Rent control can treat the symptom; only supply and safety can treat the disease.
