# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? The case for tightening rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) rests on an undeniable moral fact: people are living in cages, paying premium rents. The case against rests on a less visible but equally real fact: the stock of SDUs is not fixed, and policies that make landlordism less profitable make the cages disappear—along with the people inside them. The honest answer is that tightening rent control on SDUs is not a housing policy at all. It is a price policy aimed at a quantity problem, and Hong Kong should be wary of mistaking one for the other. Start with what already exists. The Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped increases within an existing tenancy but deliberately left initial rents free. [D1p3] That design was not an oversight; it reflected the standard insight that controlling initial rents is what triggers supply responses, because landlords can no longer price new lettings to reflect risk, maintenance, and the option value of holding the unit. [D4p2] The reviewed literature estimates the long-run elasticity of marginal SDU-type supply at 0.31 to 0.48—meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction would remove roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock within five years. [D6p1] That is not a catastrophic number, but it is not zero either, and the units that exit are the worst ones, occupied by the people with the fewest alternatives. Berlin is the cautionary tale the consultation document cites, and it deserves the citation. When the city capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats in 2020, advertised rents fell sharply—but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld units or rerouted them into uncontrolled segments. [D7p1] The policy did not create housing; it redistributed the shortage, and it did so regressively, since the most mobile and best-connected tenants captured the controlled units. New York, by contrast, has run a second-generation scheme since 1947: increases are capped within tenancies, initial rents and vacancy are lightly regulated, and vacancy decontrol only ended in 2019. [D8p1] New York's approach is more durable precisely because it is less ambitious. It accepts that the initial rent is the release valve that keeps supply from collapsing. The SDU landlord in the woman's building—the one whose pension is four units—is the key figure in this debate, and both sides misread her. She is right that she will not exit tomorrow. But she is wrong that this settles the question. The relevant margin is not the existing landlord's decision to stay or go; it is the next landlord's decision to convert a flat into SDUs, or a tenant's decision to sublet a room, or an owner's decision to maintain rather than milk a unit. A hard cap on initial rents does not evict today's tenants. It quietly closes the door on tomorrow's. There is also an enforcement argument that cuts against tightening. Every unit of inspection capacity spent policing rent levels is a unit not spent policing conditions—fire escapes, partitions, ventilation—where the harm is documented and immediate. The Chief Executive's "floor-standard + rent band" package, which regulates conditions and limits increases without capping initial rents, is a more defensible allocation of scarce enforcement capacity than a hard cap, even if it satisfies fewer activists. [D1p4] But the strongest argument against tightening is the one the tenant himself makes. Asked what would help most, he does not name rent. He names the waiting list. If the queue were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave. That is the tell. The SDU segment is tight because the public housing queue is long; rent control treats the symptom while the disease—insufficient supply of decent, affordable, permanent housing—goes untreated. Tightening rent control on SDUs would make a few tenants' monthly bills smaller and a few landlords' margins thinner. It would not shorten the queue by a single day. Hong Kong should fix the queue first, and treat rent control as the palliative it is.
