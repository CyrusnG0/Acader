Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

My position is a qualified yes to tightening, but not through a hard cap on initial rents. Hong Kong should tighten in a narrower way: stronger security of tenure, combined with a light, indexed rent band, and only if the government can build the machinery to enforce it. A hard cap would help the tenants who already have a unit and hurt the people still trying to get one.

Start with who is at stake. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot because space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. That premium is a real injustice, and it is the strongest argument for doing something.

The trouble is what economics says happens next. The primer itself frames the question: does capping the initial rent help tenants, or shrink the very stock they depend on [D1p4]? The working paper gives a useful answer by separating two kinds of control. First-generation controls, hard caps on levels, show consistent contraction effects. Second-generation controls, which cap increases inside a tenancy, show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. Hong Kong's 2021 ordinance is the second kind: it capped increases within an existing tenancy but never capped the initial rent [D1p3].

This matters because supporters of more control often point to the stock figures. The estimated stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 111,200 in 2025, straight through the ordinance [D2p1]. It is tempting to read this as proof that regulation is safe. I think that is a mistake. The stability is exactly what theory predicts for a mild instrument, and the think-tank debate noted the same point: the visible stability may be an artifact of a non-binding instrument, and a binding one behaves differently [D12p2]. The 2021 experience tells us little about a hard cap, because the hard cap has never been tried here.

The paper's scenario for a tightened cap shows why that gap matters. A 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about 900 dollars a month to incumbents, but 3,000 to 4,000 households per year are unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. The people shut out are the same group the primer says SDUs serve as a residual option. A policy that rewards insiders and bills outsiders is hard to call tenant protection. Berlin shows the same pattern in practice. When its cap was struck down, suppressed increases were released and rents on new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2].

There is also a legal and practical cost. Counsel warns that a hard cap on initial rents would almost certainly attract compensation claims under property rights, while the contractual-term approach of the 2021 ordinance is safer but leaves the actual pain point, the initial rent, unregulated [D9p1]. So the options are a cap that is legally exposed and economically risky, or a safe measure that does not reach the problem.

What tenants say points toward a way through. A survey of 440 households found that the binding constraint named by tenants is not rent level but queue length and tenancy insecurity: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three years of security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. I should be careful here, because the report comes from an advocacy organisation and a survey of this kind shows stated preference, not behaviour. Even so, it fits the economics. Tenure security is a second-generation tool, so it is unlikely to trigger the stock exit that a hard cap would. It also tackles the vulnerability that lets landlords raise rent at will.

So my recommendation is this. Tighten by extending tenure protection and by adding a modest rent band, set by reference to published per-square-foot figures and reviewable over time, rather than a flat reduction in initial rents. Keep the band light enough that the contraction effect stays in the small range the paper associates with second-generation controls. Pair it with real investment in rehousing, since the queue is what makes the segment so tight.

My main doubt is enforcement. A band is only as good as the authority's ability to check it, and I did not find evidence in the archive that this capacity exists, so my case rests on the government being willing to build it. If it will not, the better choice is to leave initial rents alone and spend the effort on tenure and on rehousing. Either way, I would reject the hard cap: its benefits go to people already inside, and its costs fall on people still waiting outside.
