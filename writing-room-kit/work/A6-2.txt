### A Matter of Survival: Why Hong Kong Must Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units

Hong Kong is a city defined by its verticality and density, but also by a profound and persistent housing crisis. At the sharpest end of this crisis are subdivided units (SDUs)—small, often substandard flats carved out of larger apartments. For the estimated 220,000 residents living in these spaces, life is a constant struggle against cramped conditions, safety hazards, and, most critically, exploitative rents. While some argue that market intervention could stifle supply, the evidence from the course archive compels a different conclusion: Hong Kong has a moral and practical imperative to tighten rent control on subdivided units to protect its most vulnerable citizens from a market that has fundamentally failed them.

The primary argument for strengthening rent control is that the current housing market for SDUs is not a free and fair market, but one defined by severe power imbalances and market failure. As course materials on urban economics highlight, a functional market requires informed participants and reasonable alternatives. Neither exists for SDU tenants. Landlords operate in an opaque environment, often with little to no formal regulation, allowing them to impose exorbitant rents, arbitrary fees, and sudden evictions. Tenants, frequently low-income families, new arrivals, or the elderly, lack the bargaining power to negotiate and are trapped by a chronic shortage of public housing and affordable private alternatives. The archive’s analysis of the 2021 Vacancy Refund and tenancy control scheme, while a step forward, is often criticized for its loopholes and insufficient penalties, failing to curb the most egregious practices. To argue this is a functioning market is to ignore the reality of a captive population with no exit options.

Furthermore, the human and social costs of inaction are a direct indictment of the current laissez-faire approach. The archive’s case studies on public health and social inequality paint a grim picture of life in SDUs. These are not merely small apartments; they are environments detrimental to physical and mental well-being. Fire safety is a constant concern, with illegal structural modifications and blocked escape routes. Sanitation is often poor, contributing to the spread of disease. The psychological toll on residents, particularly children, is immense, with a lack of space for study or play perpetuating cycles of poverty. When a society allows its most vulnerable members to live in conditions that violate basic safety and dignity, it is not a matter of market efficiency but of social neglect. Tightening rent control is not just an economic policy; it is a public health intervention and a moral imperative.

Critics of rent control often cite economic theory, arguing that price ceilings discourage investment in new housing and lead to a deterioration of existing stock. This argument, however, is a red herring in the context of SDUs. The problem is not a lack of investment, but a lack of *regulated* investment. The current system incentivizes landlords to maximize profits by minimizing maintenance and maximizing the number of units, often in violation of building codes. The goal of tighter control is not to freeze the market but to correct its perverse incentives. A well-designed policy would cap rent increases at a reasonable level, tied to inflation or a fixed percentage, and establish a robust enforcement body with the power to inspect units and penalize non-compliant landlords. This would compel landlords to maintain their properties to retain tenants, shifting the focus from exploitation to sustainable management. It would also provide tenants with the stability they need to plan their lives, rather than living under the constant threat of displacement.

In conclusion, the question of whether to tighten rent control on subdivided units is not a simple debate between free-market principles and interventionism. It is a test of Hong Kong’s commitment to social justice and the well-being of its people. The course archive demonstrates that the SDU market is a case study in market failure, where the vulnerable are subject to the unchecked power of landlords. The human costs—in health, safety, and dignity—are too high to ignore. A tighter, more enforceable rent control policy is not a panacea for Hong Kong’s housing woes, but it is an essential, immediate step to alleviate the suffering of its most marginalized residents. It is a necessary measure to ensure that in one of the world’s wealthiest cities, a basic standard of living is not a luxury afforded only to the few.
