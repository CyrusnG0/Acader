# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Any tightening of rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) raises the classic question of rent economics: does capping the initial rent help tenants, or does it shrink the very stock they depend on? [D1p4] The answer depends on supply elasticity, enforcement capacity, and what happens to households if units exit the market. Hong Kong's own data, the experience of individual tenants like Mrs Wong, and the cautionary tale of Berlin all suggest that tighter caps alone are a blunt instrument—one that protects those already inside while charging the bill to those still outside. [D7p3] The supply evidence from Hong Kong is, on its face, reassuring. The estimated SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023, and 111,200 in 2025. Growth continued straight through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, suggesting that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit. [D2p1] This matters because the strongest argument against tightening is that landlords will withdraw units, shrinking the housing options of the very people the policy intends to help. So far, that fear has not materialized in the aggregate numbers. But aggregate stock tells us nothing about distribution—who is protected and who is priced out. Mrs Wong, 67 and retired, has lived in an SDU for nine years and pays $4,200. Her tenancy predates the 2021 ordinance. She has never received a written tenancy agreement, and says the ordinance changed nothing she can see: the landlord still raises rent by whatever the market of desperate newcomers bears. [D4p1] Mrs Wong's experience exposes the enforcement gap. A cap on paper is worthless if tenants lack written contracts, if they fear retaliation, or if they do not know their rights. Tightening the statutory cap without strengthening enforcement would simply widen the distance between the law and the lived reality of tenants like her. The more troubling distributional point comes from the other end of the queue. Asked what would help most, one tenant does not name rent at all—he names the waiting list. If the queue for public housing were three years instead of five and a half, he would accept any rent to leave. [D3p3] This is the crux of the matter. For households trapped in SDUs while waiting for public rental housing, rent control is a second-best remedy for a problem whose real solution is faster rehousing. A tighter cap might shave a few hundred dollars off a monthly bill, but it does nothing to shorten the five-and-a-half-year wait that keeps them in subdivided units in the first place. Berlin offers the sharpest lesson for this brief: caps bind differently on new entrants than on incumbents. The tenants the cap protects are those already inside; the bill is handed to those still outside. Landlords facing a binding cap on existing tenancies have strong incentives to reallocate new lettings toward tenants willing to pay market rates, to convert units to other uses, or to exit the regulated segment altogether. Incumbents gain security; newcomers face a thinner, more expensive market. Hong Kong's continued stock growth through 2021 suggests this effect has been modest so far—but "modest" is not "absent," and tightening the cap would sharpen the incentive. So should Hong Kong tighten rent control on SDUs? A cautious yes, but only as part of a package. First, tighten enforcement before tightening the cap: mandatory written tenancy agreements, a statutory rental deposit scheme, accessible dispute resolution, and penalties that make non-compliance costly. Without these, Mrs Wong's landlord will continue to charge whatever the market bears. Second, pair any cap with supply-side measures—streamlining conversion of industrial buildings, incentivizing purpose-built rental housing, and above all accelerating public housing construction to shorten the waiting list. Third, monitor the stock data closely for signs of exit, and be prepared to adjust. Rent control can buy tenants time and dignity. It cannot, by itself, solve a housing shortage. Hong Kong should tighten the cap modestly, but it should tighten enforcement first—and treat the waiting list, not the rent ceiling, as the real measure of success.
