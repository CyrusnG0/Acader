### A Necessary Intervention: The Case for Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units in Hong Kong

Hong Kong's housing crisis is a defining feature of the city, a paradox of immense wealth and profound deprivation. At its sharpest edge lies the issue of subdivided units (SDUs)—tiny, often substandard partitions within larger flats that house over 200,000 people. For years, the government has taken a largely laissez-faire approach, but the human cost has become untenable. While some argue that rent control is a market distortion that will ultimately reduce supply, a careful analysis of the evidence shows that the current situation is a market failure of catastrophic proportions. Therefore, Hong Kong should not only tighten rent control on subdivided units but must do so as a critical and urgent measure to alleviate extreme poverty and improve living conditions, as evidenced by the findings of the "Hong Kong's Subdivided Units" report.

The primary argument against rent control is that it discourages landlords from renting out their properties, thereby shrinking supply and potentially worsening the crisis. However, this argument assumes a functional, competitive market. The market for SDUs is anything but. As detailed in the course archive's report by the Society for Community Organization (SoCO), tenants are often trapped in a state of profound information asymmetry and powerlessness. They are frequently unaware of their rights, face exploitative practices like exorbitant utility charges, and are in no position to negotiate. The report highlights that many tenants pay disproportionately high rents for spaces that are often illegal and unsafe. This is not a healthy market; it is a predatory one. In such a context, the standard economic objection to rent control becomes less a matter of principle and more a justification for inaction against exploitation.

The most compelling evidence for tightening rent control comes from the lived reality of the tenants themselves. The SoCO report is replete with testimonies of individuals and families whose lives have been devastated by the twin pressures of high rent and abysmal living conditions. We read of tenants spending over 40% of their household income on rent, forcing them to cut back on food and other essentials. We see images of units where cooking, sleeping, and sanitation occur in the same cramped, windowless space. The psychological toll is immense, with many residents suffering from depression and anxiety. Tightening rent control directly addresses the first of these pressures. By capping rent increases, the policy would provide immediate, tangible financial relief, allowing families to keep more of their income for food, healthcare, and education. This is not an abstract economic theory; it is a direct intervention to improve human welfare.

Furthermore, a well-designed rent control policy can be a catalyst for improving living standards. A common criticism is that rent control reduces landlords' incentive to maintain their properties. However, this can be mitigated by linking rent adjustments to the quality of the unit. A tightened policy could, for instance, allow for modest rent increases only after verifiable, government-approved improvements are made, such as installing proper fire safety equipment, improving ventilation, or ensuring access to basic sanitation. This creates a "carrot and stick" dynamic. It prevents arbitrary rent hikes while incentivizing landlords to invest in their properties to a minimum standard. The current system, by contrast, offers no such incentive; landlords can extract maximum rent with zero investment. The report's call for a comprehensive regulatory framework, including a landlord registration scheme and mandatory building safety standards, supports this holistic approach. Rent control is not a standalone solution but a vital component of a broader strategy to regulate and upgrade the SDU market.

In conclusion, the argument for tightening rent control on subdivided units is not based on a naive faith in intervention but on a clear-eyed assessment of a market that has failed the most vulnerable. The evidence from the course archive paints a grim picture of exploitation, poverty, and hazardous living conditions that cannot be solved by market forces alone. Opponents of rent control advocate for supply-side solutions, but building sufficient public housing is a long-term project that offers no relief to the families suffering today. Tightening rent control is a necessary, immediate, and humane response. It provides financial respite, creates a mechanism to incentivize better living standards, and represents a fundamental acknowledgment that housing is a human right, not merely a commodity. In the face of such profound human suffering, the theoretical risks of rent control are a price worth paying for a more just and livable city.
