Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units by imposing a hard cap on initial rents. If the government wants to act, it should do so through narrower measures that protect tenants inside their tenancies, and it should put the harder money into the housing queue. My position is therefore against a hard initial-rent cap, but not against all regulation.

Start with who lives in these units. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, and they pay a per-square-foot premium of 15 to 25 percent over mainstream flats because space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. That premium is a real injustice, and it is the strongest argument for a cap. Mrs Wong, who has lived in her unit for nine years, supports a hard cap on initial rents, and she believes her landlords would not leave the business because the units are their pension [D4p2]. But her belief about landlord behaviour is exactly what the evidence has to test, and one tenant's confidence is not a measure of supply response.

What does Hong Kong already do? The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped rent increases within an existing tenancy but did not cap the initial rent level [D1p3]. The primer itself frames the real question: does capping the initial rent help tenants, or does it shrink the very stock they depend on [D1p4]? The working paper gives a useful way to answer it. It separates first-generation controls, which are hard caps on levels and show consistent contraction effects, from second-generation controls, which cap increases within tenancies and show small or null effects on stock [D6p2]. The 2021 ordinance is second-generation. So any claim that "regulation has not hurt supply so far" only speaks to the mild instrument we have, not to a hard cap. Moving to a hard cap means moving to a different class of policy, and the benign track record does not transfer.

How large might the damage be? The same paper reports that the long-run elasticity of this kind of supply to controlled rents is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That sounds modest, but the loss falls on the bottom of the market. For a Hong Kong-like market, the paper models a 15 percent cut in initial rents as giving about 900 dollars a month to incumbents, while 3,000 to 4,000 households a year cannot enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. The people shut out are the same group the primer identifies as disproportionately vulnerable. Berlin shows the pattern in practice: after rents were capped on about 1.5 million flats, new-listing rents fell sharply, but listed supply contracted within a year as owners withheld flats or moved them to uncontrolled segments [D7p1]. A cap helps those already inside and sends the bill to those outside. I would add a caution: these are modelled and comparative results, not Hong Kong measurements, so the exact numbers should be held loosely. The direction, however, is consistent across the sources.

Enforcement is the second problem. An internal minute records that current inspection capacity covers under 6 percent of SDU stock per year, and that a band-based cap would need a certifiable tenancy register estimated at 40 to 60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. A cap that cannot be policed is a promise on paper, and the tenants least able to complain would likely benefit last. I treat that document cautiously because it is a draft not meant for circulation, but its point about capacity is hard to dismiss.

Finally, there is the question of what tenants actually say they need. A survey of 440 households found that the binding constraint named by tenants was not rent level but queue length and tenancy insecurity: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. This does not mean rents do not matter. It means a policy built around price alone is answering the symptom of a quantity problem.

My recommendation follows from this. Keep and strengthen the within-tenancy protections, add security of tenure, and, if the government wants to touch initial rents at all, use a light band rather than a hard cap, phased in only once a tenancy register and inspection capacity exist. Spend the large effort on shortening the queue. The strongest objection to my view is that moderate measures leave the 15 to 25 percent premium in place. I accept that cost. A hard cap that removes units and locks out new arrivals would trade a visible overcharge for a less visible exclusion, and the evidence suggests the excluded would be those with the least power to object.
