Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

My position is that Hong Kong should not adopt a hard cap on initial rents for subdivided units (SDUs). It should instead pursue a narrow tightening that protects tenants inside their tenancies and leaves the starting rent alone until the state can actually enforce a rule. The evidence in the course archive suggests that a hard cap would help people who already have a unit and hurt the people who are still trying to get one, and that those people are the most vulnerable.

The case for acting is real. About 110,000 households live in SDUs, flats carved into cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms, and they pay a per-square-foot premium of 15 to 25 percent over mainstream flats because space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. The existing Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped increases within a tenancy but did not cap the initial rent [D1p3]. So the cost that hurts most, the first rent, is unregulated. A legal opinion makes the same observation: a cap framed as contractual-term regulation is on firmer ground but leaves the initial rent, "the actual pain point", untouched [D9p1]. Anyone who defends the status quo must admit that the current law leaves the main problem alone.

Supporters of a cap can point to one piece of reassuring data. SDU stock grew from 107,300 units in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025, so growth continued through the ordinance and moderate regulation did not trigger visible exit [D2p1]. I do not think this supports a hard cap, though. The same ordinance only limited increases inside tenancies and never touched the initial rent [D1p3]. The stable stock therefore tells us about a light instrument, not a binding one. The working paper in the archive states the mechanism: standard theory predicts supply contraction under binding ceilings, and in marginal stock the long-run supply elasticity is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48, which means a 10 percent cap-induced rent cut removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1].

The strongest argument against a hard cap is who pays. The paper models a 15 percent cut in initial rents for a Hong Kong-like market and finds a net transfer of about 900 dollars a month to incumbents, set against 3,000 to 4,000 households a year who cannot enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. Berlin shows the same pattern in practice. After its cap, listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld flats or moved them to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. When the law was struck down, a backlog of suppressed increases was released and rents on new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2]. A cap, in other words, moves the bill from insiders to outsiders. It is a poor deal when the outsiders are the households with the least alternative.

Two further problems make a hard cap risky even if one accepts its goals. The first is legal. A hard cap on initial rents would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's property protections if it is framed as depriving owners of a rental income stream [D9p1]. The second is enforcement. A tightened cap is only as real as its inspection regime, and current inspection covers under 6 percent of SDU stock each year. A band-based initial-rent cap would also need a tenancy register that officials estimate at 40 to 60 staff-years to build [D10p1]. A rule that cannot be checked will be obeyed mostly by landlords who were already compliant.

The most useful evidence, I think, is what tenants themselves say they need. A survey of 440 SDU households found that the binding constraint named by tenants is not rent level but queue length and tenancy insecurity: 61 percent would trade a nominal rent increase for a three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent for a faster public-housing offer [D11p1]. That finding reframes the question. If tenants rank security of tenure and a quicker route out above a lower rent, then a policy that spends its political, legal and administrative capital on a contested rent ceiling is aiming at the wrong target.

My recommendation is therefore a limited tightening. First, strengthen security of tenure so that tenants are not forced out for asking for repairs. Second, build the survey and register infrastructure before any numerical rent band is attempted, so that the band can be defended and enforced. Third, treat the supply elasticity of 0.31 to 0.48 as a warning about the scale of any future cut and start with a light band rather than a hard ceiling.
