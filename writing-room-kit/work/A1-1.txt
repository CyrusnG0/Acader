Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? My position is that it should not tighten by imposing a hard cap on initial rents. The case for a cap is emotionally strong, but the evidence in the course archive suggests that a level cap would hurt the same people it is meant to protect.

Start with who is at stake. About 110,000 households live in subdivided units, flats carved into small cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms, and they pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot because space is priced at its most desperate margin [D1p1]. That premium is the strongest argument for intervention: tenants really are being charged more for less.

But what Hong Kong already has matters. The Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped rent increases within an existing tenancy but did not cap the initial rent [D1p3]. The working paper in the archive calls this kind of regime second-generation control, and says such controls show small or null effects on stock, while first-generation controls, which are hard caps on levels, show consistent contraction effects [D6p2]. This distinction is the centre of my argument. Supporters of tightening might point to the stable stock since 2021 as proof that more control is affordable, but the paper itself explains that stability by the ordinance being second-generation. A stable result from a light instrument tells us little about how a heavier one would behave, so I do not accept stability as evidence that a hard cap is safe.

How large could the contraction be? The paper reports a long-run supply elasticity of 0.31 to 0.48 for this kind of marginal housing, meaning a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. That is not a catastrophe in any single year, but these are units at the very bottom of the market, so every unit lost is a household with nowhere cheaper to go. The primer frames the question the same way: capping the initial rent either helps tenants or shrinks the very stock they depend on, and the answer depends on supply elasticity, enforcement capacity and what happens to households if units exit [D1p4].

The paper then puts numbers on the trade-off. For a Hong Kong-like market, a 15 percent cut in initial rents gives a net transfer of about 900 dollars a month to incumbents, but 3,000 to 4,000 households a year cannot enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. I find this the most decisive evidence, because it shows who wins and who loses. The winners are people already inside. The losers are people outside who have no voice in the consultation. A policy meant to protect vulnerable tenants would be shifting the burden onto the most vulnerable newcomers.

Berlin is a warning about the same pattern. After the city capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats, initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply, but listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld flats or moved them to non-controlled segments [D7p1]. Still, the direction matches the working paper, and it shows that owners do respond to the rule by changing what they offer.

A tempting counter-argument comes from a tenant, Mrs Wong, who supports a hard cap because she believes her landlords would never leave the business, since the units are their pension [D4p2]. But it is one tenant's belief about landlord behaviour, and it sits against the systematic estimates above. Her account of landlords is a claim about incentives, and incentives are exactly what the elasticity estimates measure.

There is also a practical problem: enforcement. The NGO report argues that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem, and that every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire, partition and ventilation conditions, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. Even if a hard cap were legal and economically harmless, it would compete with safety work for the same scarce inspectors. If the goal is to protect tenants tonight, safety inspection looks like the more direct tool.

So my answer is a qualified no. Hong Kong should keep and enforce the second-generation protections it already has, and put new effort into the quantity side of the problem rather than the price side. If policymakers do want to go further, they should treat any measure touching initial rents as an experiment with a built-in check on whether new households are still able to enter the segment. The decisive reasoning is simple: a cap that helps those inside while locking out thousands of newcomers each year does not protect the people the policy is for.
