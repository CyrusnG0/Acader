Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on subdivided units with a hard cap on initial rents. If the government wants to act, it should choose a narrower and more modest route. My position is that a hard initial-rent cap is the wrong tool, because it risks hurting the very people it is meant to protect.

The basic problem with capping the initial rent is the one the course primer itself raises. Any tightening forces the question of whether the cap helps tenants or shrinks the stock they depend on. The answer, according to the primer, depends on supply elasticity, enforcement capacity and what happens to households if units leave the market [D1p4]. Subdivided units are already the last option for many households. If landlords respond to a hard cap by withdrawing units, the people who lose are the ones with nowhere else to go. A policy that sounds generous could therefore leave the poorest tenants worse off.

There is some evidence that moderate regulation can coexist with a stable stock. The estimated number of subdivided units rose from 100,800 in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, then to 109,800 in 2023 and 111,200 in 2025. Growth continued through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, which suggests that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit [D2p1]. This supports the idea that mild controls are tolerable. But it does not show that a much tougher control would behave the same way. The 2021 measure only limited increases inside an existing tenancy, so it is a weak guide to what a binding cap on starting rents would do. Stability under a light rule is not proof of stability under a heavy one.

The second reason for caution is that rules only matter if they can be enforced. One NGO report in the archive argues that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem, because the segment is tight when the queue is long. It adds that every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent on inspecting conditions such as fire, partitions and ventilation, where harm is documented and immediate [D11p2]. This is a practical point. Public inspectors are limited, and if they are pulled toward checking rent levels, the physical safety of cramped flats may get less attention. For a household living in a cubicle, a fire risk can matter more than a modest saving on rent.

So my answer is a qualified no. I would not support a hard cap on initial rents. The stock has grown under the current light ordinance, and the best use of scarce enforcement staff is inspection of unsafe conditions. If policymakers still want to do more for tenants, they should favour measures that improve security and safety without squeezing the supply of units, and they should watch the stock figures closely after any change. The central risk of tightening is that it removes housing from people who have few alternatives, and the evidence I found does not show that the benefits would outweigh that risk.

The limits of my evidence should be acknowledged. I did not find tenant testimony or detailed enforcement figures in the passages I retrieved, so this argument rests on a small set of sources. A fuller case would test the claim against more of the archive. Even so, the direction of the evidence points toward caution rather than a bold tightening of control.
