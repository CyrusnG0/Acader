# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Any answer to this question must begin with a fact that reframes it. The binding constraint for subdivided unit (SDU) tenants is not the rent level; it is queue length and tenancy insecurity. In a survey of 440 SDU households, 61 percent said they would accept a nominal rent increase in exchange for three-year security of tenure, and 74 percent would do so for a faster public-housing offer. [D11p1] Tenants are not primarily asking for cheaper rent. They are asking for time and predictability. A policy that tightens rent control while ignoring this hierarchy of needs risks treating the symptom and worsening the disease. The case for tightening is real. At 15 to 25 percent per square foot above mainstream private flats, SDU pricing exploits the most desperate margin of the market. [D1p1] The Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 capped increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent untouched, which is precisely where the exploitation occurs: a landlord can set any opening rent and simply re-set it with each new tenant. [D1p3] Tightening initial-rent caps would close that loophole, and the exclusion of co-living SDUs from key provisions invites obvious gaming, since "services" can be bundled to escape regulation. Enforcement capacity matters here, but the current regime's gaps are not hypothetical; they are designed around. Yet the standard objection to rent control is not merely ideological. Capping initial rents below market-clearing levels reduces the return on carving flats into SDUs, and if supply is elastic, units exit the market or never get created. [D1p4] The critical question is what happens to the households in those units. In a segment defined by desperation, exit does not mean tenants move to better housing; it means they crowd into the remaining stock, bid up unregulated rents, or join the public-housing queue from a worse position. Supply elasticity in Hong Kong's SDU market is hard to estimate, but the direction is clear: the stock is created by landlords responding to price signals, and suppressing those signals shrinks it. This does not mean laissez-faire. It means the instrument must match the diagnosis. Three reforms follow from the evidence. First, cap the initial rent, but pair it with a vacancy-control mechanism and a mandatory standard tenancy. A cap without security of tenure simply shifts landlord power from price to eviction. The survey's finding that tenants would trade rent for a three-year term is the design brief: give them the term, and the rent cap becomes sustainable rather than evasive. Second, treat public-housing throughput as the primary lever. If 74 percent of tenants would accept higher rent for a faster offer, then the real rent-control policy is queue reform: more transitional housing, faster means-testing, and priority for SDU households. Rent control cannot substitute for supply; it can only buy time while supply catches up. Third, close the co-living exemption. A loophole that lets landlords rebrand cubicles as serviced accommodation undermines both the 2021 Ordinance and any tightening that follows. The honest answer, then, is qualified yes: tighten, but tighten the right things. Cap initial rents to stop the worst exploitation, but only alongside enforceable security of tenure, because without it tenants will pay for the cap in evictions. And do not mistake rent control for housing policy. The 110,000 households in SDUs are not waiting for a price ceiling; they are waiting for a door to open. A tighter cap that shortens the queue is good policy. A tighter cap that lengthens it, or that lets landlords exit into unregulated co-living, is worse than the status quo.
