# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Roughly 110,000 households live in subdivided units (SDUs), and the numbers tell a story of stubborn persistence: an estimated 100,800 units in 2019, 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023, and 111,200 in 2025. Growth continued straight through the 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance, which suggests that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit. [D2p1] That single fact is the strongest argument for tightening — and the strongest warning against overreaching. The case for tightening begins with the status quo's obvious gap. The 2021 ordinance capped rent increases within an existing tenancy but left the initial rent unregulated, and excluded SDUs with co-living services from key provisions. [D1p3] The initial rent is precisely where the pain is felt. SDU tenants already pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot relative to mainstream private flats, because carved space is priced at its most desperate margin. [D1p1] A tenant signing a first lease has no protection at all; the cap only matters after the harm is done. Meanwhile, the SDU stock kept growing through the ordinance, which undercuts the claim that any regulation necessarily drives landlords out. On the evidence, moderate rules and a growing stock coexisted. The case against a hard cap rests on arithmetic and on experience elsewhere. Berlin capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats in 2020. Initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply — good news, briefly — but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld units or rerouted them to uncontrolled segments. [D7p1] The mechanism is straightforward: if the permitted initial rent falls below what landlords can earn elsewhere, marginal units exit or never enter. Hong Kong's SDU market is small, heterogeneous, and enforcement-weak; a hard cap would likely push some landlords toward non-controlled segments, informal arrangements, or outright withdrawal. The households left behind would not be helped by a cheaper rent on a unit that no longer exists. There is also a legal constraint. A hard cap on initial SDU rents framed as a deprivation of the rental income stream would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's protection of property rights. [D9p1] A cap framed as contractual-term regulation, like the 2021 ordinance, sits on firmer ground — but that framing is exactly what leaves the initial rent unregulated. The legal safe harbor and the policy pain point are the same point. The sensible path is therefore a narrow tightening, not a hard cap. Two components fit the evidence. First, strengthen tenure security so that tenants cannot be churned out to reset the initial rent — the current within-tenancy cap is easily circumvented if tenancies are not renewed. [D11p3] Second, impose a light band on initial rents: a ceiling set relative to prevailing market rents in comparable SDUs, high enough to preserve landlord returns on existing stock, but low enough to stop the most exploitative first offers. New York's second-generation scheme offers a workable template: increases capped within tenancies, initial rents and vacancy lightly regulated rather than frozen. [D8p1] Hong Kong already has the first half of that architecture; it needs the second half, calibrated conservatively. The contraction arithmetic from Berlin cautions against going further. Enforcement capacity matters too: a band that cannot be monitored is a band that punishes only the compliant. And the deeper question remains what happens to households if units exit — there is no credible rehousing pipeline at this scale. [D1p4] A narrow tightening buys time and relief without gambling the stock. A hard cap gambles the stock for relief that may never arrive. Hong Kong should tighten, carefully, and stop short of the cap that would undo itself.
