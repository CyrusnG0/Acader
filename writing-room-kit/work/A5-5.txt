# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Any answer must begin with a distinction that the policy debate often blurs: first-generation rent control caps rent *levels*, while second-generation control caps rent *increases within a tenancy*. The evidence is asymmetric. First-generation controls show consistent contraction effects on rental stock; second-generation controls show small or null effects. [D6p2] Hong Kong's Tenancy Control Ordinance of 2021 is squarely second-generation. It caps increases within an existing tenancy but leaves the initial rent free, and it exempts SDUs with co-living services from key provisions. [D1p3] The question of whether to "tighten" control therefore splits into two very different proposals: strengthening the second-generation instrument, or crossing into first-generation territory. The first is defensible; the second is not. The case for tightening within the existing framework is strong. About 110,000 households live in SDUs—cubicles carved out of flats, often with shared kitchens and bathrooms. Median SDU rent is roughly 30 percent below mainstream private flats, but per square foot tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent, because space is carved and priced at its most desperate margin. [D1p1] This is not a market serving diverse preferences; it is a market clearing at the point of greatest need. Tenants who pay a premium for less space have almost no bargaining power and face high moving costs, which is precisely the captive condition that second-generation controls target. Closing the co-living exemption and tightening the in-tenancy cap would limit the most predatory increases without shrinking the stock—consistent with the stable stock observed in the D2 index. But "tightening" must not mean capping initial rents. The per-square-foot premium exists because landlords price at the desperate margin; a hard cap on levels would make many SDU conversions unprofitable at the margin. The units most at risk are the worst ones—the smallest cubicles in the oldest buildings, where compliance costs and enforcement risk bite hardest. First-generation controls would not create affordable homes; they would remove the housing that currently exists, pushing households toward illegal or informal arrangements. The consistent contraction evidence from first-generation regimes is the relevant warning here. There is also a distributional trap. If tighter controls suppress supply while demand from low-income households remains fixed, the queue lengthens and allocation shifts to those with connections or the ability to pay under the table. The households most in need—new arrivals, single elderly tenants, low-wage workers—are least able to navigate that queue. A policy that improves the position of sitting tenants while worsening access for entrants is not obviously progressive. The better path is targeted tightening within the second-generation model: close the co-living loophole, index the in-tenancy cap to a transparent measure, strengthen enforcement against evasion through side payments, and pair controls with supply-side measures—transitional housing, site identification, and incentives for purpose-built rental. Rent control can buy stability for existing tenants; it cannot manufacture the additional units Hong Kong needs. Treating it as a supply policy would repeat the first-generation mistake. In short, Hong Kong should tighten rent control on SDUs—but only along the second-generation margin. Strengthen the 2021 ordinance; do not replace it with a level cap. The goal is to protect tenants inside the tenancy without destroying the incentive to create the tenancy in the first place.
