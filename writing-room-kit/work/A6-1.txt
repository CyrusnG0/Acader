# The Case for Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units in Hong Kong

Hong Kong's subdivided units—flats partitioned into tiny cubicles, often smaller than a parking space—house an estimated 100,000 households, or roughly 220,000 people, according to the 2016 census. These tenants endure some of the worst housing conditions in the developed world. The question of whether to tighten rent control on these units is not merely economic; it is a question of basic human dignity. I argue that Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units, because current protections are inadequate, tenants lack meaningful bargaining power, and evidence from comparable jurisdictions shows that well-designed controls reduce exploitation without collapsing supply.

The first point is that existing controls are too weak to protect tenants. The Landlord and Tenant (Consolidation) (Amendment) Ordinance 2021 introduced a two-year fixed term with a maximum 15% rent increase upon renewal, and prohibited landlords from charging utilities above cost. On paper, this looks like protection. In practice, the archive materials show that enforcement is patchy and loopholes abound. Landlords can evict tenants at the end of the term without stating a reason, and many tenants report being pressured to sign new agreements that reset the clock. A 15% increase every two years compounds to over 30% in four years—far outpacing wage growth for low-income workers. If the goal is to prevent displacement, the cap is simply too high.

Second, subdivided unit tenants have almost no bargaining power. The typical tenant is a low-income worker, often a new immigrant or elderly person, with limited knowledge of tenancy law and little access to legal aid. The archive documents cases where landlords charged illegal deposits, cut off water and electricity to force compliance, or refused to make repairs. In a market with a vacancy rate near zero for these units, tenants cannot simply "vote with their feet." When exit is impossible and voice is suppressed, the only remedy is a stronger legal floor. Tighter rent control—capping increases at the rate of inflation, for example—would give tenants stability without requiring them to negotiate individually.

Critics argue that tightening rent control will reduce supply, as landlords convert units back to whole flats or sell the buildings. This is a serious concern, but the evidence from the course archive suggests it is overstated. In New York City, rent stabilization has not eliminated the rental market; it has simply shifted investment toward new construction, which is exempt from controls. In Hong Kong, the supply of subdivided units is not primarily driven by rent levels but by the high value of whole-flat rents and the slow pace of public housing. Tightening controls would not make these units disappear overnight; it would redistribute the gains from a scarce asset. Moreover, the government could pair tighter controls with incentives for landlords to upgrade units, such as tax deductions for repairs, and with a faster rollout of transitional housing.

A third argument for tightening is that the social costs of inaction are enormous. Children in subdivided units lack space to study; elderly tenants suffer from heat and poor ventilation; and the psychological toll of insecurity is well documented. The archive includes testimony from tenants who have moved five times in three years, each time losing community ties and school placements for their children. These are not abstract inefficiencies; they are harms that a just society should not tolerate. Rent control is a blunt instrument, but it is one of the few tools available to a government that has been slow to build public housing and reluctant to regulate private landlords.

In conclusion, Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units. The current 15% cap is too generous, enforcement is too weak, and tenants lack the power to protect themselves. While supply concerns are legitimate, they can be mitigated with complementary policies. The alternative—leaving hundreds of thousands of people at the mercy of a speculative market—is morally and politically unsustainable. A city as wealthy as Hong Kong can afford to guarantee its poorest residents a stable roof over their heads.
