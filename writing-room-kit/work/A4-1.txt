# Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units? Any answer must begin with a concession: the status quo is indefensible. Roughly 110,000 households live in subdivided units, paying a 15 to 25 percent per-square-foot premium over mainstream private flats precisely because space is priced at its most desperate margin. [D1p1] These tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households, and residents waiting an average of five and a half years for public housing. [D1p2] For them, the SDU is not a choice but a residual option. The case for intervention is moral, not merely economic. The question is whether a tighter cap is the right intervention. The strongest argument against tightening is the Berlin precedent. When Berlin capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats in 2020, reported initial-offer rents fell sharply—but classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year as owners withheld units or rerouted them to non-controlled segments. [D7p1] A hard cap on initial SDU rents risks the same dynamic. Worse, it would likely attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's protection of property rights if framed as a deprivation of rental income. [D9p1] Framed instead as contractual-term regulation, as the 2021 ordinance was, it sits on firmer legal ground—but then it leaves the initial rent, the actual pain point, unregulated. That is the trap: the legally durable version of rent control does not reach the harm, and the version that reaches the harm is legally fragile. Yet the Hong Kong evidence complicates the exit story. Estimated SDU stock grew from 100,800 units in 2019 to 107,300 in 2021, 109,800 in 2023, and 111,200 in 2025—growth that continued straight through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance. Moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit. This suggests SDU supply is less elastic than Berlin's, perhaps because landlords have few alternative uses for carved-up flats and because demand from the public-housing queue is captive. If supply barely responds to moderate caps, the classic rent-economics objection loses much of its force. But "moderate" is doing heavy lifting in that sentence. The 2021 ordinance capped increases within tenancies while leaving initial rents lightly regulated—closer to New York's second-generation scheme than to Berlin's freeze. New York has regulated rents since 1947, covering roughly 44 percent of rental stock, with capped increases inside tenancies, lightly regulated initial rents, and vacancy decontrol only ended in 2019. That model has survived for decades, but it has also produced a chronically tight, poorly maintained stock. It is a caution, not a template. The deeper problem is that rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem. The segment is tight because the queue is long. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting fire safety, partition standards, and ventilation—where harm is documented and immediate. [D11p2] A tighter cap might shave a few hundred dollars off a monthly bill while leaving a family in a windowless cubicle with a blocked fire exit. So the answer is: not as a standalone measure. Hong Kong should tighten enforcement of conditions and accelerate public-housing supply first, and consider a cap on initial rents only alongside a credible plan to rehouse waiting households. [D1p4] A cap without that plan simply redistributes scarcity. A cap with it addresses the queue itself. The tenants of subdivided units deserve better than a choice between an unaffordable cubicle and a regulated one; they deserve a way out of the cubicle altogether. Until that exit exists, tightening rent control is treating the thermometer instead of the fever.
