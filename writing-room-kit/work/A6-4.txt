### The Case for Tightening Rent Control on Subdivided Units in Hong Kong

Hong Kong’s housing crisis is a defining feature of its modern identity, a paradox of immense wealth set against profound deprivation. At the sharpest end of this crisis are subdivided units (SDUs)—tiny, often substandard flats carved out of larger apartments. For the estimated 220,000 residents living in these spaces, life is a constant struggle against exorbitant rents, cramped conditions, and precariousness. While the government’s recent introduction of a tenant protection regime is a step in the right direction, it is a fatally flawed instrument. To genuinely address the humanitarian emergency within its borders, Hong Kong must significantly tighten rent control on subdivided units. Evidence from the course archive demonstrates that the current, timid approach fails to curb exploitation, while a more robust system is both economically viable and a moral imperative.

The primary justification for tightening rent control is the clear and present failure of the existing regulatory framework. The government’s current policy, introduced in 2022, caps rent increases for a two-year term at the lower of either 10% or the rate of inflation, and prohibits landlords from charging tenants for utilities at a profit. On paper, this offers a veneer of protection. In practice, however, it has been rendered largely ineffective by a critical flaw: the lack of a mandatory, government-administered standard tenancy agreement. As documented in the course archive, this loophole allows unscrupulous landlords to circumvent the spirit of the law. They do so by front-loading the rent at an artificially high level for the first term, thereby ensuring that even a capped increase results in a massive financial burden. Furthermore, many continue to illegally charge for utilities or demand hefty "key money" and renovation fees, knowing that tenants, facing a severe supply shortage and the threat of homelessness, are in no position to negotiate or report them. The current policy, therefore, does not control rents; it merely controls their rate of increase from an already exploitative baseline.

Opponents of tighter controls often raise the spectre of economic fallout, arguing that stringent rent caps will discourage landlords from maintaining or investing in their properties, leading to a deterioration in housing quality and a reduction in supply. This argument, however, is a red herring in the context of SDUs. The course archive reveals that the SDU market is not a normal, competitive market. It is a market born of desperation and sustained by a chronic, systemic undersupply of public housing. The demand is perfectly inelastic; people need a roof over their heads, regardless of the cost or quality. Landlords are not benevolent providers of a service but rentiers extracting maximum profit from a captive population. Their "investment" is often minimal, with many units lacking basic safety features like fire escapes and proper ventilation. The threat of reduced supply is also hollow. Where would these units go? They are typically carved out of older residential buildings that have no other viable use. The choice is not between a well-maintained SDU and no SDU; it is between an exploitative SDU and a slightly less exploitative one. Tightening controls would not destroy the market; it would simply redistribute power within it, forcing landlords to accept a reasonable, rather than a predatory, rate of return.

A more robust rent control policy would yield tangible benefits that far outweigh the theoretical economic risks. The most direct benefit is the immediate alleviation of poverty and suffering for tens of thousands of families. The exorbitant rents in SDUs are a primary driver of in-work poverty, trapping residents in a cycle of debt and despair. By capping rents at a genuinely affordable level—perhaps tied to median income in the district or a fixed percentage of the tenant's earnings—the government could free up significant disposable income for these households. This would allow them to afford better food, healthcare, and educational opportunities for their children, fostering social mobility and reducing long-term societal costs. A tighter policy would also empower tenants. With a legally enforceable, standard lease and a cap on rents, tenants would gain the security and confidence to demand necessary repairs and report illegal practices without fear of arbitrary eviction.

In conclusion, the question is not whether Hong Kong can afford to tighten rent control on subdivided units, but whether it can afford not to. The current policy is a half-measure that fails to address the root cause of the crisis: the unbridled power of landlords in a market defined by desperation. The argument that such controls would harm the market is a defence of a system that is already fundamentally broken and inhumane. A policy that caps rents at a truly affordable level and provides robust tenant protections is not an attack on the free market; it is a necessary intervention to correct a profound market failure. It is a matter of social justice. For a city that prides itself on its prosperity and rule of law, allowing its most vulnerable citizens to languish in squalid, overpriced cages is an intolerable contradiction. Tightening rent control is a crucial, compassionate, and pragmatic step towards building a more just and livable Hong Kong for all its residents.
