**Tighten, Yes — But Not With a Hard Cap**

Hong Kong's subdivided units (SDUs) house roughly 110,000 households, and the stock has kept growing — from about 100,800 units in 2019 to an estimated 111,200 in 2025 — even after the 2021 tenancy control ordinance. [D2p1] That single fact should anchor the debate. Moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit, which means the choice is not between "control" and "no control" but between smarter and blunter instruments. The case for tightening is strong; the case for a hard cap on initial rents is not. Start with the diagnosis. SDU tenants pay 15 to 25 percent more per square foot than mainstream private flat tenants, because space is carved and priced at its most desperate margin. [D1p1] This is not a market clearing at a fair price; it is a queue being rationed by price. The subcommittee's own modelling captures the trade-off precisely: a 15 percent cut in initial rents transfers about HK$900 per month to incumbents, but leaves 3,000 to 4,000 households per year — mostly new arrivals and newly formed households — unable to enter the segment at all. [D6p4] Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem. That is the strongest argument against a hard cap, and it is arithmetic, not ideology. The supply elasticity evidence supports a narrow tightening rather than a freeze. In marginal stock — units already at the bottom of the market — the long-run elasticity of SDU-type supply to controlled rent levels is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48: a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years. [D6p1] Berlin's 2020 cap on about 1.5 million flats is the cautionary tale: initial-offer rents fell sharply, but classified-listing supply contracted within a year as owners withheld or rerouted flats to non-controlled segments. [D7p1] A hard cap in Hong Kong would likely do the same, and the households pushed out would be precisely those the policy claims to protect. There is also a legal constraint that shapes what "tightening" can mean. A hard cap framed as deprivation of the rental income stream would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's protection of property rights. [D9p1] A cap framed as contractual-term regulation, like the 2021 ordinance, sits on firmer ground — but leaves the initial rent, the actual pain point, unregulated. The design space is therefore narrow: tenure security plus a light band on initial rents, not a ceiling. [D11p3] The enforcement argument cuts the same way. The subcommittee's modelling note records a risk the public consultation omits: if landlords reprice above-band initial rents, the tenant's first instrument is complaint — but the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement or the newest arrivals. [D10p2] A hard cap would therefore systematically miss the most exploited tail, while consuming enforcement capacity that is currently spent on fire, partition and ventilation inspections, where harm is documented and immediate. [D11p2] Every unit of capacity policing rents is a unit not spent on conditions. What would a defensible tightening look like? First, extend tenure security and strengthen the written-agreement requirement, so that the most vulnerable tenants have a paper trail before they have a grievance. Second, impose a light band on initial rents — indexed, transparent, and enforced through the same registry that handles tenancy renewals — rather than a hard ceiling. Third, pair it with supply-side measures that actually shorten the queue: transitional housing, public rental expansion, and incentives for purpose-built rental stock. New York's second-generation scheme is instructive: increases are capped within tenancies, initial rents and vacancy are lightly regulated, and vacancy decontrol ended in 2019 — a model of calibrated tightening, not a freeze. [D8p1] The landlords in the member's building are right about one thing: for many SDU owners, the units are their pension, and they will not exit overnight. [D4p2] But that is an argument for gradualism, not for inaction. The status quo already extracts a premium from the most desperate margin, and the stock keeps growing regardless. Tighten — but tighten with a band, a registry, and an enforcement strategy aimed at the tenants who cannot complain. A hard cap would be the one reform most likely to hurt the people it is meant to help.
