**The Limits of Capping the Wrong Number**

Any answer to whether Hong Kong should tighten rent control on subdivided units (SDUs) must begin with a distinction the policy debate has too often blurred: controlling rent *increases* within a tenancy is not the same as controlling the *initial* rent. [D1p3] The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance already does the former. The real question is whether to cap the latter — and on that, the case is far weaker than it first appears. The moral case for action is not in doubt. Roughly 110,000 households live in SDUs, and while median SDU rents sit about 30 percent below mainstream private flats, tenants pay a 15 to 25 percent premium per square foot. [D1p1] That premium is not an accident of the market; it is the price of desperation, charged at the most desperate margin. Tenants are disproportionately new arrivals, single-parent households, and residents waiting for public housing — a queue whose average wait now hovers around five and a half years. [D1p2] For these households, the SDU is not a choice but a residual option. Yet the Berlin experiment is the cautionary tale. When Berlin capped rents on roughly 1.5 million flats in 2020, reported initial-offer rents for new listings fell sharply — and classified-listing supply contracted measurably within a year, as owners withheld units or rerouted them to uncontrolled segments. [D7p1] The cap did not create housing; it made existing housing disappear from the regulated market. Hong Kong's SDU stock is far more elastic in the wrong direction: partitions can be dismantled, buildings redeveloped, units converted back to whole flats. A hard cap on initial rents would shrink precisely the stock that desperate households depend on. [D1p4] There is also a legal constraint that shapes what is even possible. A hard cap framed as a deprivation of the rental income stream would almost certainly attract compensation claims under the Basic Law's protection of property rights. [D9p1] A cap framed as contractual-term regulation — like the 2021 ordinance — sits on firmer ground, but that framing leaves the initial rent, the actual pain point, untouched. Regulators cannot simply wish this tension away. The most compelling argument against tightening, however, is one of opportunity cost. Rent caps treat the price symptom of a quantity problem. The SDU segment is tight because the queue is long — for public housing, for affordable private flats, for land itself. Every unit of enforcement capacity spent policing rents is a unit not spent inspecting conditions: fire safety, partition walls, ventilation. [D11p2] That is where the documented, immediate harm lies. A tenant paying a painful rent in a fire-safe unit is better off than one paying a capped rent in a death trap. If policymakers insist on intervening at the initial-rent margin, the least harmful instrument is the one counsel has floated: a published per-square-foot band, indexed and reviewable, defensible as rate-capping rather than confiscation. [D9p2] But this would require survey infrastructure Hong Kong does not currently possess, and it would still risk driving marginal units out of the regulated segment. [D8p1] The honest conclusion is that Hong Kong should not tighten rent control on SDUs — not because tenants do not deserve relief, but because a cap on initial rents is the wrong lever aimed at the wrong number. The binding constraint is supply and the length of the public housing queue. Until that queue shortens, rent control will redistribute scarcity rather than reduce it. The better use of scarce regulatory capacity is enforcement of living conditions, paired with a credible supply and rehousing strategy. Capping the price of a shortage does not end the shortage. It only changes who bears its cost.
