Should Hong Kong tighten rent control on subdivided units? My position is that it should not adopt a hard cap on initial rents. If it tightens anything, it should do so cautiously and in a way that does not shut people out of the housing they have left.

The instinct behind tightening is easy to understand. Subdivided units are small, crowded and expensive for what they offer. About 110,000 households live in them, and many of those tenants have nowhere else to go. A cap looks like a direct way to ease the burden on people who already have too little. I take that concern seriously, and I do not think the case against a hard cap is a case for ignoring tenants.

But a rent cap does not only act on the people who already hold a tenancy. It also changes who can get a unit in the first place. The strongest evidence in the archive on this point is the working paper's model of a tightened cap in a Hong Kong-like market. It finds that a 15 percent cut in initial rents yields a net transfer of about 900 dollars per month to incumbents, against 3,000 to 4,000 households per year unable to enter the segment, mostly new arrivals and newly formed households [D6p4]. That is the heart of the matter. The benefit goes to people who are already inside, and the cost falls on people who are still outside. Those excluded households are not rich. They are the same newcomers and young families that the policy is supposed to help, so the cap risks protecting one group of poor tenants by locking out another.

Someone might reply that the fear of shrinking supply is exaggerated, because Hong Kong has already regulated and the market held up. There is a real point here. The stock index shows that estimated SDU stock grew from 107,300 units in 2021 to 111,200 in 2025, and the archive reads this as growth that continued through the 2021 tenancy control ordinance, suggesting that moderate regulation did not trigger visible stock exit [D2p1]. I accept that the ordinance has not caused a collapse. But that is a reason to be careful about what it proves. The 2021 ordinance limited increases within an existing tenancy and left the starting rent alone. A hard cap on initial rents is a different and much heavier instrument. Stable supply under a light rule says little about supply under a binding one, so the index cannot be stretched into a case for going further.

So my answer is that Hong Kong should not adopt a hard cap on initial rents. The gain to sitting tenants is real but modest, and the cost lands on newcomers who cannot find a unit at all. Policy should instead keep what has worked, avoid shrinking the stock, and look at measures that help tenants without closing the door behind them, such as stronger protection against sudden eviction. I would also want any future change to be introduced gradually and monitored against the stock figures, so that if units start to leave the market the government can see it and respond before the people at the margin pay for the mistake.

The deeper reason for my view is simple. A housing policy should be judged by what happens to everyone who needs a home, not only to those who already have one. On the evidence I was able to find, a hard cap does better for insiders than for the people waiting outside, and that is not a trade I would want Hong Kong to make.
