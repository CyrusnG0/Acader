Should Hong Kong Tighten Rent Control on Subdivided Units?

I argue that Hong Kong should not adopt a hard cap on initial rents for subdivided units (SDUs) at this stage. If government wants to tighten, it should do so only by strengthening protections inside existing tenancies and by building enforcement capacity first. The evidence in the course archive shows that the people a hard cap is meant to protect are the people most likely to lose out.

The problem is real. About 110,000 households live in SDUs, flats carved into small cubicles with shared kitchens and bathrooms, and although median SDU rent is about 30 percent below mainstream flats, tenants pay a premium of 15 to 25 percent per square foot [D1p1]. Tenants pay more for less space, so any argument against tightening must take that seriously.

What Hong Kong has so far is limited. The 2021 Tenancy Control Ordinance capped rent increases inside an existing tenancy but did not cap the initial rent, and it left out SDUs with co-living services from key provisions [D1p3]. So the real pain point, the rent a new tenant is first quoted, is untouched. That is the argument for tightening: the current rules leave the entry price free.

The strongest evidence on the other side is about supply. The stock estimates show 100,800 units in 2019 rising to 111,200 in 2025, and growth continued through the 2021 ordinance [D2p1]. A supporter of tightening could read this as proof that regulation does not drive landlords out. I think that reading is too quick. The ordinance only limited increases within tenancies, so it never tested what a binding limit on initial rents would do. The archive's working paper explains why this matters: standard theory predicts contraction under binding ceilings, and in marginal stock like SDUs the long-run supply elasticity is estimated at 0.31 to 0.48, so a 10 percent cap-induced rent reduction removes roughly 3 to 5 percent of stock over five years [D6p1]. The stable stock is therefore weak evidence about a hard cap. It shows only that a light instrument did not bite.

The primer frames the core trade-off clearly. Capping initial rent might help tenants, or it might shrink the very stock they depend on, and the answer turns on supply elasticity, enforcement capacity and what happens to households if units leave the market [D1p4]. The elasticity range says some units would leave. If a tenth off the rent removes up to five percent of 110,000 households' housing, that is thousands of units at the very bottom of the market. These are not luxury flats that can be replaced. The households who lose them are the same group the cap is supposed to help, and a family that is protected by a lower rent but cannot find any unit at all has not been helped.

Berlin is a useful warning about what happens when a cap is introduced and then fails. Its law was struck down on federal-competence grounds, not economic ones, but when it fell a backlog of suppressed increases was released and rents for new contracts jumped above the pre-cap trend [D7p2]. Berlin is a different city, and the passage speaks only to the collapse of the law. Still, it shows that a cap which gets reversed can leave new tenants worse off than if nothing had been tried, which is a real risk for a policy that would likely face legal challenge.

Enforcement is the point where I am most convinced. Even a well-designed cap depends on tenants complaining when landlords reprice. An internal subcommittee note records that the tenants least able to complain are those with no written agreement, or the newest arrivals, so enforcement would systematically miss the most exploited tail [D10p2]. This matters because new arrivals are exactly the group the primer says is overrepresented in SDUs. A cap that protects articulate, established tenants with paperwork and misses recent arrivals would be tightening in name and unequal in effect. I treat this as the decisive reason to build the registry and inspection capacity before any hard cap, not after.

My position has limits: I could not retrieve tenant or landlord material, so how landlords would respond is my reasoning, not evidence.

In short: the entry price is a real harm, but a hard cap risks shrinking the stock, invites a reversal like Berlin's, and cannot be enforced where it is needed most. Hong Kong should tighten in steps, starting with enforcement capacity and protections within tenancies, and only then consider touching the initial rent.
